~/history/h7-hack-timeline

A timeline of the worms, breaches and bugs that shaped security, from a joke program in 1971 to the XZ backdoor in 2024. Five of them have full case studies.

1simple2mid3complex4hands-on+ref

01 simple // the idea in plain words

Hacks are not random. Each famous one exposed a weakness that was already there, and each one changed how defenders work. The short version:

  • 1971, Creeper. A harmless self-moving program on ARPANET. Reaper, the first "antivirus", chased it.
  • 1982, Elk Cloner. A teenager's boot-sector prank on Apple II floppy disks.
  • 1986, Brain. Early PC boot-sector virus.
  • 1988, Morris worm. The first internet worm. Case study.
  • 1989, AIDS Trojan. Early ransomware, delivered on floppies by mail.
  • 1999, Melissa. A Word macro virus that mailed itself to your contacts.
  • 2000, ILOVEYOU. A script worm sent as a love letter. Spread over email and hit tens of millions of computers.
  • 2003, SQL Slammer. 376 bytes, spread across the internet in minutes.
  • 2010, Stuxnet. Malware aimed at Iranian centrifuges. Case study.
  • 2014, Heartbleed. Case study. Also Shellshock, a Bash flaw (CVE-2014-6271).
  • 2016, Mirai. Botnet from cameras with default passwords; knocked out DNS provider Dyn.
  • 2017, WannaCry and NotPetya. WannaCry. NotPetya spread via a hijacked update to Ukrainian accounting software and caused billions in damage.
  • 2020, SolarWinds. Attackers poisoned a software build and shipped a signed backdoor to thousands of organisations.
  • 2021, Log4Shell. Case study. Also Colonial Pipeline, a ransomware attack that halted a US fuel pipeline.
  • 2023, MOVEit. A SQL injection flaw (CVE-2023-34362) in a file-transfer product let a ransomware gang steal data from hundreds of organisations.
  • 2024, XZ Utils. A backdoor slipped into a compression library by a long-term fake contributor, found by accident. And the CrowdStrike outage (July): not an attack, a faulty security-product update that crashed millions of Windows machines.

02 mid // how it's actually used

What changed after each wave

  • 1988 gave us CERT and incident coordination.
  • Code Red and Slammer (2001, 2003) gave us Patch Tuesday and firewalls on by default.
  • Stuxnet pushed ICS/OT security into the mainstream.
  • Heartbleed led to funding for critical open source and to the OpenBSD fork LibreSSL.
  • WannaCry made people disable SMBv1 and take backups seriously.
  • SolarWinds drove SBOMs, build provenance and executive orders on software supply chain.
  • Log4Shell made "do we use this library?" a question every company had to answer in a weekend.

Naming a few families

  • Virus: needs a host file or user action to spread. Worm: spreads by itself over a network. Trojan: pretends to be something useful. Ransomware: encrypts or steals and demands payment. Botnet: many infected devices under one controller. Rootkit: hides itself at a low level.

03 complex // what the pros do

How to read an incident report

  1. What was the initial access? (phishing, exposed service, stolen credentials, supply chain)
  2. What was the root cause? (one bug, or a chain of small failures)
  3. What failed in detection? Dwell time (days between entry and discovery) is a measure of detection quality.
  4. What limited the damage? (segmentation, backups, least privilege)
  5. What would have stopped it cheaply? Often a patch, MFA or an egress rule.

ATT&CK as a vocabulary

MITRE ATT&CK describes attacker behavior as tactics (initial access, persistence, lateral movement, exfiltration) and techniques (T-numbers). Map any case study onto it and you see which controls would have broken the chain. The attack chain page on this site does this interactively.

Where to read primary sources

  • Incident reports: Mandiant, CrowdStrike, Microsoft Threat Intelligence, CISA advisories, the Cyber Safety Review Board (its Log4j report, 2022).
  • Classic papers: Spafford (1988) on Morris; Symantec's *W32.Stuxnet Dossier* (2011); MS17-010 bulletin.
  • Books: *The Cuckoo's Egg* (Stoll), *Countdown to Zero Day* (Zetter), *Sandworm* (Greenberg).

malware families

virus
attaches to files or boot sectors; needs a host and often a user action
worm
copies itself across a network without a user
trojan
looks legitimate, does something else
ransomware
encrypts or steals data and demands payment
botnet
many compromised devices controlled together
rootkit
hides malware from the OS and tools
backdoor
hidden access path, planted or built in
zero-day
a flaw the vendor does not know about yet
n-day
a known, patched flaw that organisations have not patched
dwell time
time between compromise and detection
