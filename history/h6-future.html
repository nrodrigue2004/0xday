Where this is going: AI agents, quantum computers, post-quantum cryptography, memory-safe code. Predictions are labeled as opinion, the dates and standards are not.

1simple2mid3complex4hands-on+ref

01 simple // the idea in plain words

Read this chapter differently. The earlier chapters are history. This one mixes facts (published standards, announced results) with opinion (what I think is likely). Opinions are marked. Check the sources, and expect parts of this page to age badly.

Quantum computers

A normal bit is 0 or 1. A qubit can be in a blend of both until you measure it, and qubits can be entangled. For a few specific problems, that lets a quantum computer take shortcuts no ordinary computer can. It does not make every program faster.

Two algorithms matter for security:

  • Shor's algorithm (1994). Factors large numbers and solves discrete logarithms efficiently. That breaks RSA, Diffie-Hellman and elliptic-curve cryptography, which protect almost all web traffic and signatures today.
  • Grover's algorithm (1996). Speeds up brute-force search by a square root. That roughly halves the strength of a symmetric key, so AES-256 stays fine.

Today's machines are small and noisy. Nobody has yet built one that can break real RSA keys, and it needs many thousands of error-corrected qubits, which is much harder than adding raw qubits. Nobody knows exactly when it will happen.

Post-quantum cryptography (PQC)

New algorithms that run on ordinary computers but are believed to resist quantum attacks. In August 2024 NIST published the first standards: ML-KEM (key exchange, from Kyber), ML-DSA (signatures, from Dilithium) and SLH-DSA (hash-based signatures, from SPHINCS+). More are in progress.

The threat to plan for now

Harvest now, decrypt later. An attacker records encrypted traffic today and decrypts it when a quantum computer exists. Data that must stay secret for 10 to 30 years is already at risk.

02 mid // how it's actually used

Other bets on where tech goes (labeled opinion)

  • Opinion: AI agents become normal coworkers. Tools that read, write code, run commands and call APIs. The security job shifts toward permissions, audit trails and review, and away from typing every command by hand.
  • Opinion: memory-safe languages gradually replace C and C++ in new code. Rust, Go, Swift and managed languages remove whole bug classes (see Heartbleed). In 2024 the White House and CISA publicly urged the shift. Old C code will be around for decades.
  • Fact-ish trend: passwordless login (passkeys, FIDO2) spreads. Phishing-resistant authentication beats every password policy.
  • Fact-ish trend: software supply-chain controls (SBOMs, signed builds, provenance such as SLSA) become requirements for vendors.
  • Opinion: AI finds bugs faster than people fix them. Government and industry programs (such as DARPA's AI Cyber Challenge) test automatic bug finding and patching. Defenders and attackers both get the tools.
  • Hard to predict: neuromorphic chips, brain-computer interfaces, 6G, space networks. Treat any confident date as marketing.

The state of quantum, plainly

  • A "physical qubit" is noisy. A "logical qubit" is built from many physical ones with error correction.
  • Published resource estimates have fallen over time. A 2019 estimate (Gidney and Ekerå) put RSA-2048 at around twenty million noisy qubits running for hours. A 2025 update by Gidney cut that to under a million. Real machines are still orders of magnitude smaller.
  • In late 2024 Google reported error correction improving as it added qubits (its "Willow" chip), a necessary milestone, not a break of anything.

What to do about it (this is the defender part)

  1. Inventory where you use RSA, ECC and Diffie-Hellman: TLS, VPNs, SSH, code signing, certificates, stored data.
  2. Crypto agility: make algorithms swappable without rewriting systems.
  3. Prioritise long-lived secrets (medical, legal, state data) for early PQC.
  4. Hybrid mode: combine a classical and a PQC key exchange. Browsers and large providers already do this for TLS.
  5. Follow the NIST schedule: its 2024 draft guidance (IR 8547) proposes deprecating RSA and ECC around 2030 and disallowing them by 2035.

03 complex // what the pros do

Mosca's inequality

Let X = how many years the data must stay secret. Y = how many years your migration to PQC will take. Z = years until a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists. If X + Y > Z, the data is already exposed to harvest-now-decrypt-later. You do not need to know Z precisely; the point is that X and Y are *your* numbers and are usually larger than people think.

What the NIST algorithms are built on

  • Lattice-based (ML-KEM, ML-DSA): hard problems about finding short vectors in high-dimensional grids (Learning With Errors). Fast, but key and signature sizes are larger than ECC.
  • Hash-based (SLH-DSA): security reduces to hash functions only. Conservative, big signatures.
  • Code-based (HQC, selected by NIST in 2025 as a backup KEM): error-correcting codes.
  • A lesson from the process: SIKE, a finalist-stage candidate, was broken in 2022 on a laptop by classical means (Castryck and Decru). New math needs years of public attack before trust.

Symmetric and hashing

Use 256-bit symmetric keys and SHA-256 or better; Grover costs a square root, not everything. AES-128 is probably fine in practice but the margin is smaller.

Open problems and honest uncertainty

  • Whether large-scale fault-tolerant quantum computers arrive in 10, 20 or 50 years: experts disagree.
  • Whether lattice assumptions hold up.
  • Whether AI changes the offense/defense balance, and for how long.
  • Side-channels and bugs in *implementations* will break PQC deployments before the math does, as with every earlier algorithm.

Sources to read

NIST FIPS 203, 204, 205 (2024); NIST IR 8547 draft; Shor (1994); Grover (1996); Gidney and Ekerå (2019) and Gidney (2025) resource estimates; Mosca, *Cybersecurity in an era with quantum computers* (2018); CISA/NSA/NIST joint factsheet on quantum readiness.
