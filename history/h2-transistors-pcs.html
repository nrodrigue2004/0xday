~/history/h2-transistors-pcs

The transistor shrank the computer from a room to a desk. Along the way came Unix, C, the microprocessor and the PC, and the first ideas about computer security.

1simple2mid3complex4hands-on+ref

01 simple // the idea in plain words

  • 1947, the transistor. Bardeen, Brattain and Shockley at Bell Labs built a solid-state switch. Smaller, cooler and more reliable than a vacuum tube.
  • 1958 to 1959, the integrated circuit. Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce put many transistors on one chip.
  • 1965, Moore's law. Gordon Moore observed that the number of transistors per chip was doubling about every year or two. It was an observation, not a law, and it held for decades.
  • 1969, Unix. Ken Thompson and Dennis Ritchie at Bell Labs. 1972, the C language. Unix was rewritten in C, which made it portable. Linux, macOS, Android and most servers descend from this line.
  • 1970, the relational model. Edgar Codd's idea became SQL databases.
  • 1971, the Intel 4004. A whole processor on one chip.
  • 1975 to 1984, the home computer. Altair 8800 (1975), Apple I (1976), the IBM PC (1981, running Microsoft's DOS), and the Macintosh (1984) with a graphical interface.

Security started to matter once machines were shared and connected:

  • 1971, Creeper. A harmless program that moved between machines on ARPANET printing "I'm the creeper, catch me if you can". Reaper was written to delete it.
  • 1976 to 1977, public-key crypto. Diffie and Hellman, then RSA (Rivest, Shamir, Adleman): two people can agree on secrets without ever meeting.
  • 1983 to 1984, "computer virus". Fred Cohen demonstrated one; Ken Thompson's Turing lecture, *Reflections on Trusting Trust*, showed a compiler could carry a hidden backdoor.

02 mid // how it's actually used

Why C and Unix shaped security

C gives you direct memory access and no bounds checking. That made Unix small and fast, and it is the reason a whole class of bugs exists: buffer overflows, use-after-free, and integer overflows. The Morris worm (1988) and Heartbleed (2014) are both C bugs, 26 years apart. See Morris worm and Heartbleed.

Unix ideas that survived

  • Everything is a file; small tools do one job; pipes connect them (ps | grep | wc).
  • Users, groups and permission bits (rwx) with a privileged root. Still the base of Linux permissions.
  • Text configuration, a shell, and scripts. Your homelab runs on this.

The first security thinking

  • 1970s Multics, a time-sharing system built with security in mind, and the Anderson report (1972) for the US Air Force, which framed the idea of a reference monitor: a small piece that mediates every access.
  • Rings and privilege levels in hardware: user mode vs kernel mode.
  • Orange Book (1983, TCSEC): the first formal criteria for rating how trustworthy a system is.

The PC era

The IBM PC and DOS had no users or permissions at all: whoever sat at the keyboard owned the machine. Floppy disks carried the first PC viruses: Elk Cloner (1982, an Apple II prank by a teenager), Brain (1986, widely called the first PC virus).

03 complex // what the pros do

Trusting trust

Thompson's 1984 lecture describes a compiler modified to (1) insert a backdoor when it compiles the login program and (2) insert that same modification when it compiles a new compiler. Delete the malicious source and the binary compiler still reproduces it. The point: you cannot fully trust code you did not build from scratch, including the tools. Modern defenses: reproducible builds, diverse double-compiling, signed releases, and SBOMs. The 2020 SolarWinds and 2024 XZ Utils incidents are supply-chain cousins of this idea.

Why Moore's law ended up in security

Cheap, fast chips made brute force cheap (password cracking, hashing), and made side channels possible: speculative execution tricks in the 2018 Spectre and Meltdown bugs came from performance optimisations. Speed and safety trade off.

Public-key cryptography, briefly

Diffie-Hellman (1976) lets two parties build a shared key over a public channel. RSA (1977) is based on the difficulty of factoring a product of two large primes. Both are the subject of the quantum threat in the future chapter. British GCHQ had found equivalent ideas earlier (Cocks, Ellis, Williamson, early 1970s), but kept them secret until 1997.

Operating system lineage (rough)

  • Unix (1969) -> BSD (1977) -> FreeBSD, OpenBSD, macOS/iOS kernels
  • Unix ideas -> Linux (1991) -> Android, most of the cloud
  • CP/M -> DOS (1981) -> Windows (1985); NT kernel (1993) is what Windows uses today

04 hands-on // sandbox shell

Scenario (fictional, simulated): you inherit a Unix box from 1979 (a text archive of its /etc/passwd and a log). ~/etc/passwd lists accounts, and the old rule was that an account with an empty password field needs no password at all. Find the username of the account that has no password and has a shell. Submit the username, e.g. submit root.
