~/cases/wannacry

A stolen NSA exploit plus ransomware equals a worm that hit hospitals, factories and railways in a weekend. Told at concept level: what the bug was, how the worm spread, the accidental kill switch, and how to avoid being the next headline.

1simple2mid3complex4hands-on+ref

01 simple // the idea in plain words

The story

On Friday 12 May 2017, a ransomware program called WannaCry began spreading by itself between Windows computers. It encrypted files and displayed a demand for roughly $300 in bitcoin, rising to $600 after a few days. Within a day it had hit about 230,000 computers in some 150 countries, including parts of Britain's NHS, where ambulances were diverted and operations cancelled, plus a Spanish telecom, FedEx and a car maker's plants.

It spread because of a flaw in an old, rarely-needed Windows file-sharing protocol, SMBv1. An exploit for that flaw, codenamed EternalBlue, had been developed by the US NSA. In April 2017 a group calling itself the Shadow Brokers published it online. Microsoft had already patched the flaw in March 2017 (bulletin MS17-010). Machines that had applied the patch were safe. Millions had not.

A young researcher, Marcus Hutchins (MalwareTech), noticed the malware tried to contact a nonsense web address and registered it for about ten dollars. The malware treated "this address resolves" as "stop", which slowed the first wave. It was a kill switch, probably intended as a way to detect sandboxes. Later variants removed it.

US and UK governments attributed WannaCry to the Lazarus Group, linked to North Korea.

Timeline

  • Mid 2016 to 2017: the Shadow Brokers appear and leak tools claimed to belong to the Equation Group (linked by researchers to the NSA).
  • 14 Mar 2017: Microsoft issues MS17-010.
  • 14 Apr 2017: the Shadow Brokers release the Windows tools, including EternalBlue.
  • 12 May 2017: WannaCry outbreak begins; the kill switch is found by the evening.
  • 13 to 15 May: Microsoft ships emergency patches even for unsupported Windows XP.

02 mid // how it's actually used

How it worked (concept level)

WannaCry was three things in one:

  1. A worm component. Scan for machines with TCP port 445 open (SMB). Try the SMB flaw. If it works, run the malware on the target and repeat. It scanned the local network and random internet addresses.
  2. A backdoor implant (DoublePulsar, also from the leak) installed on compromised machines, which other attackers later reused.
  3. A ransomware payload. Generate a key for each file, encrypt files with it, protect that key with a public key from the attacker, and show the ransom note.

The bug, in one paragraph

SMBv1 handles client requests in a kernel driver (srv.sys). A mistake in how a size value was converted, and then used to allocate and copy data, allowed a specially built request to overwrite kernel memory, and gave remote code execution as SYSTEM with no login. The details are public in the Microsoft bulletin and many analyses. This page does not reproduce the exploit; what matters for defenders is the class: an unauthenticated, network-reachable parser in the kernel, which is the worst combination.

If you want to read the actual mechanism, read the original research write-ups, not a PoC. Understanding the bug class (integer errors leading to heap overflow) is enough to patch, detect and design around it.

What stopped it, and what would have

Installing MS17-010 (two months earlier)stops the exploit entirely

Detection ideas

  • Network: many hosts suddenly scanning port 445; SMB sessions to many different targets; DNS lookups of the kill-switch name (a defender's tell, and a reason not to block that name).
  • Endpoint: a process creating files with the .WNCRY extension; shadow-copy deletion commands (vssadmin delete shadows); mass file renames.
  • Vulnerability scanning: Nessus, OpenVAS and nmap's smb-vuln-ms17-010 script (use on your own hosts) show who is unpatched.

03 complex // what the pros do

Why hospitals

Many health systems ran Windows XP/7 on medical devices that vendors had not certified for updates, on flat networks where file shares reached everything. The UK National Audit Office later found that at least 81 of 236 trusts were disrupted, and that the NHS was not specifically targeted: it was collateral damage of an untargeted worm. A basic fix existed and had been recommended by the NHS months earlier.

The leak as policy problem

The case reopened an old debate: should a government keep software flaws secret to use them, or disclose them for patching? Microsoft's president Brad Smith compared it to the military losing missiles. NSA reportedly told Microsoft about the flaw shortly before the leak, which is why a patch existed. Reasonable people disagree on the policy; the defender's lesson is simpler: assume any secret exploit will leak eventually.

Related incident: NotPetya (June 2017)

Another EternalBlue-based tool, plus credential theft (Mimikatz-style), spread via a poisoned update to Ukrainian tax software. It looked like ransomware but was destructive, with no workable recovery. Costs ran into billions (Maersk, Merck, FedEx/TNT). It shows how quickly the same exploit gets repurposed.

Defensive lessons

  • Patch SLAs for critical remote bugs: days, not months.
  • Retire legacy protocols (SMBv1, Telnet) once nothing needs them. Audit with Get-SmbServerConfiguration on Windows or Nmap's smb-protocols script.
  • Segment. Workstations rarely need to reach each other on 445.
  • Backups must be offline or immutable, and restore-tested.
  • Know your unpatchable assets and wrap them: isolate, restrict, monitor.
  • Do not block a kill-switch domain in your own DNS: it can make the malware run.
