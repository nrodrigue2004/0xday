Risk management is how a company decides what to protect and how much to spend. Learn assets, threats and vulnerabilities, qualitative and quantitative scoring, the ALE formula, treatment options and the risk register.

01 simple // the idea in plain words

Risk is the chance that something bad happens and how much it would hurt. Security cannot remove all risk, so the job is to find the biggest risks and decide what to do about them with a limited budget.

The building blocks

  • Asset: something worth protecting (customer database, laptops, reputation, a website)
  • Threat: something that can cause harm (ransomware gang, flood, careless employee)
  • Vulnerability: a weakness the threat can use (unpatched server, no backup)
  • Likelihood: how probable
  • Impact: how bad (money, downtime, legal trouble, safety)

Risk = Likelihood x Impact. A big impact that almost never happens and a small impact that happens daily can both matter.

Four ways to respond

  1. Mitigate: reduce it with a control (patch, MFA, backup)
  2. Transfer: shift the cost (cyber insurance, outsourcing to a provider; responsibility still stays with you)
  3. Accept: knowingly live with it because the cost of fixing is higher than the loss
  4. Avoid: stop doing the risky thing (retire the service)

What remains after your controls is residual risk. What exists before any controls is inherent risk.

Quick scoring (qualitative)

Rate likelihood 1 to 5 and impact 1 to 5, multiply. 1-5 low, 6-12 medium, 13-25 high. Simple, fast, and good enough to rank a list. Use the risk worksheet to try it.

02 mid // how it's actually used

The risk process

  1. Identify assets and their value and owner
  2. Identify threats and vulnerabilities (scans, interviews, past incidents, threat intelligence)
  3. Analyze likelihood and impact
  4. Evaluate against the organization's risk appetite (how much risk leadership will take) and risk tolerance (the acceptable variation around it)
  5. Treat with one of the four responses
  6. Monitor and review: risks change when systems, threats or business change

Quantitative: the formulas to know

TermMeaningFormula
AVAsset valuedollars
EFExposure factor: percent of the asset lost in one event0 to 1
SLESingle loss expectancyAV x EF
AROAnnualized rate of occurrenceevents per year
ALEAnnualized loss expectancySLE x ARO

Example: a server worth $200,000, a flood destroys 25% of it per event (SLE = $50,000), and a flood is expected once every 4 years (ARO 0.25). ALE = $12,500 a year.

Control justification: a control is worth buying if the drop in ALE is greater than its yearly cost. If the ALE falls from $12,500 to $2,500 and the control costs $4,000 a year, the net benefit is $6,000.

Qualitative versus quantitative

Quantitative gives numbers executives like, but the inputs are often guesses. Qualitative is faster and honest about uncertainty. Many teams use a heat map (likelihood across, impact down) and add dollar figures only for the top risks. FAIR is a structured quantitative model.

The risk register

A table of every known risk: ID, description, asset, threat, vulnerability, likelihood, impact, score, owner, treatment, status, review date. Each risk has one owner who can make decisions about it. The register is a living document reviewed at least quarterly.

Related terms

BIA (business impact analysis) finds critical processes and how long they can be down: RTO (recovery time objective), RPO (recovery point objective, how much data loss is acceptable), MTTR, MTBF. Third-party risk covers vendors; supply chain risk covers what they depend on.

03 complex // what the pros do

Making risk statements useful

A good statement is specific: "Because the payments API lacks rate limiting (vulnerability), an attacker could run credential stuffing (threat) and take over customer accounts, causing fraud losses and notification costs (impact)." Vague entries like "hackers" cannot be owned or fixed.

Dealing with uncertainty

  • Use ranges, not single numbers (low, likely, high) and a Monte Carlo simulation if you can
  • Anchor estimates on data: incident history, industry reports (Verizon DBIR, IBM Cost of a Data Breach), insurance quotes
  • Document assumptions so the estimate can be challenged and improved
  • Beware precision theatre: ALE of $12,483 is not more accurate than "about $12k"

Risk acceptance done properly

Acceptance is a business decision: written, with an owner at the right level, a time limit and a review date. "We never got to it" is not acceptance, it is neglect. Exceptions to policy should have the same discipline.

Risk appetite in practice

Leadership sets thresholds: for example, no risk scored High stays open more than 30 days without an approved plan; no single event may exceed $1M. Reports to the board use a few key risk indicators, such as time to patch critical vulnerabilities, percent of systems with MFA, and number of overdue risks.

Common mistakes

Counting only technical risk and missing process, people and vendor risk. Rating by gut and never revisiting. Treating compliance as risk management (passing an audit does not mean you are safe). Hiding risks to avoid blame, which guarantees the surprise comes later.

Link to testing

Pentests, vulnerability scans and threat modeling feed the register. A finding from PEN300 becomes a risk entry with an owner and a treatment. Frameworks supply the control choices: see frameworks.
