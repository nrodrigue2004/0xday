Incident response (IR) is the structured way a team handles a security event. The exam expects the phases in order, the planning activities, and the basics of digital forensics.

### The process (NIST SP 800-61 style)

| Phase | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Preparation** | Plan, train, equip. Everything that has to exist before an incident |
| **2. Detection and analysis** | Notice something, confirm it is an incident, scope and prioritize it |
| **3. Containment** | Stop the spread: isolate hosts, disable accounts, block traffic |
| **4. Eradication** | Remove the cause: malware, backdoors, malicious accounts, close the vulnerability |
| **5. Recovery** | Restore systems from known-good sources and return to normal operations, with monitoring |
| **6. Lessons learned** | Post-incident review to improve the process |

> Memory aid: **P**repare, **D**etect/analyze, **C**ontain, **E**radicate, **R**ecover, **L**essons learned.

Many questions ask "what is the **next** step?" Contain before eradicating. Preserve evidence before wiping.

### Preparation

- **Incident response plan** with defined roles, authority and escalation. **Playbooks** and **runbooks** for common incident types.
- **Contact list** and **communication plan**: who to inform (management, legal, HR, PR, law enforcement, customers, regulators), with out-of-band channels because email may be compromised.
- **Incident response team**: IR manager, security analysts, IT/system owners, legal, HR, communications.
- **Go bag / jump kit**: laptops, forensic software, write blockers, cables, blank media, documentation, a camera.
- **Known-good images and backups** to rebuild from.
- **Training** and **exercises** before the incident; you cannot learn during one.
- **Monitoring and detection tools** in place: SIEM, EDR, IDS/IPS, logging.

### Detection and analysis

- Sources: IPS/IDS alerts, antivirus, SIEM correlation, log anomalies (unexpected admin changes, new accounts), network traffic spikes, user reports, threat intelligence.
- **Triage**: determine whether it's a real **incident** versus a normal **event**, then **prioritize** by impact on the business.
- **Sandbox** analysis of suspicious files shows what they do safely.
- Document everything with timestamps from the start.
- **Root cause analysis** (RCA) identifies how it happened so the same hole is closed.

### Containment, eradication, recovery

- **Containment**: isolate or **quarantine** affected systems, disable compromised accounts, block malicious IPs. Short-term (stop the bleeding) and long-term (clean network). Weigh evidence preservation against speed.
- **Eradication**: remove malware, patch the exploited vulnerability, reset credentials, rebuild from known-good images where trust is lost.
- **Recovery**: restore from clean backups, re-enable services in stages, monitor closely for reinfection, verify integrity.
- **Reconstitution** returns systems to normal operation.

### Lessons learned (post-incident)

A meeting with everyone involved: what happened, the **timeline**, how well the plan and tools worked, what indicators were missed, and what to change. Feed the results back into the plan, monitoring and training. Produce a report.

### Incident planning exercises

- **Tabletop exercise**: discussion of a scenario around a table. Low cost, no systems touched.
- **Walkthrough**: step through the plan to verify accuracy.
- **Simulation**: a more realistic drill, possibly with technical injects.
- **Full test / functional exercise**: the team performs real actions.
- **Root cause analysis** and **threat hunting** (proactively searching for undetected compromise) support the process.
- **Stakeholder management**: keep leaders, legal and communications informed; **retention** and **escalation** rules; **notification** requirements for regulators and affected people.

### Digital forensics

Forensics collects and analyzes evidence in a way that holds up in court and in internal investigations.

- **Legal hold**: a legal instruction to preserve relevant data (electronically stored information, ESI). Disposal is suspended.
- **Chain of custody**: documented record of who handled the evidence, when, where and why, from collection to presentation. Gaps can make evidence inadmissible. Use hashes and signed logs to prove it wasn't altered.
- **Acquisition**: copy evidence without changing it. Use a **write blocker** on disks and work on **forensic images** (bit-for-bit copies), not the original. Hash original and copy and compare.
- **Order of volatility**: collect the most volatile data first, as it disappears fastest:
  1. CPU registers and cache
  2. RAM, routing/ARP tables, process table
  3. Temporary files and swap
  4. Disk
  5. Remote logs and monitoring data
  6. Physical configuration, topology
  7. Archival media and backups
- **Sources**: disk, memory, firmware, network traffic, logs, snapshots of VMs, mobile devices, cloud. Live acquisition may be needed for encrypted systems (a powered-off system loses the keys).
- **Preservation**: keep original data pristine; analyze copies; store securely.
- **Reporting**: summary, acquisition details, findings, methods, and conclusions that a third party can reproduce.
- **E-discovery**: collecting, preparing, reviewing and producing electronic documents for legal proceedings.
- **Admissibility**: evidence must be authentic, complete and properly handled.
- **Timeline analysis**, **time offsets** (note clock differences), and **data carving** (recover files from raw disk).
- **Provenance** shows where evidence came from; **hashes** show it's unchanged.

### More detail from the lessons

**Process and preparation**

- Example incidents: a user clicks an attachment and malware runs; a DDoS from botnets overwhelms the WAN link; stolen data held for ransom; or software that lets outsiders in. The NIST guide is **SP 800-61 Revision 2, Computer Security Incident Handling Guide** (the lesson's phases: preparation; detection and analysis; containment, eradication and recovery; post-incident activity).
- **Preparation** items: an up-to-date **communication/contact list**; an **incident go bag** (laptops with specialized software, removable media, forensic software, a digital imaging system for photos and video); documentation and **network diagrams**; **security baselines** and **file hashes** of critical files; **known-good OS and application images**; and policies and procedures everyone follows.
- **Detection** is hard because attacks arrive constantly and most are automated scripts. Sources: web server logs (a vulnerability scan shows up), a **patch calendar** (watch for attacks on unpatched systems), occasional direct contact from attackers, **IPS alerts** (buffer overflow attempt), antivirus reports, configuration changes made by an attacker, and a large **traffic increase** (exfiltration). Stop an attack quickly rather than watching it.
- **Sandbox** analysis shows what malware changes in the OS, but malware may detect a VM with limited connectivity and **delete itself** or behave differently.
- **Recovery** means removing bad software and replacing it with known good: remove the malware or **reimage**, disable breached or attacker-created accounts, fix the original vulnerability, restore from known-good backups or reinstall from original media, then lock things down.
- **Post-incident meeting** as soon as possible while memories are fresh: what happened, the **timeline**, how well the plan worked, whether indicators were missed (if so change monitoring), and what to change next time. Planning and training must occur before an incident (no on-the-job training). Large organizations with multiple IR teams may find it expensive, but it saves money after a big incident.

**Exercises and analysis**

- Test with **test systems**, limited time, and evaluate afterwards. Full-scale disaster events cost time, money and people, so start with a **tabletop**: sit around a table for a few hours, walk through one scenario (what first, what second) and watch how other departments respond at the same time.
- **Simulation** examples: phishing emails (see who clicks), calling the help desk to see if someone changes a password, or sending data out to see if monitoring catches it. A third-party phishing simulation also tests mail filters and anti-phishing systems, and users who click get more training.
- **Root cause analysis**: one incident has many small events (breach a vulnerability, move data, plant malware). Use logs and attacker artifacts to rebuild how they got in. There may be **multiple root causes**, and user mistakes (social engineering) are part of it.
- **Threat hunting**: find the vulnerability before attackers do, for example adjusting firewall rules, tracking newly announced vulnerabilities, and checking installed systems are patched. Automated tools can stop some attacks without you watching.

**Forensics in detail**

- Collection details are mostly beyond the exam, but **RFC 3227** covers guidelines for evidence collection and archiving. Evidence may be used in legal proceedings years later, so take extensive notes.
- **Legal hold**: a lawyer or legal entity sends a document describing data that must be preserved; it goes to the **data custodian**, who evaluates it. Data is collected into a separate **ESI (electronically stored information)** repository, which may require converting (for example a proprietary email format to text). You must keep it safe for the court.
- **Chain of custody**: keep data pristine, and track every person who accesses it. Physical analogy: evidence in a sealed bag, with each access recorded on the bag. Digital version uses **hashes and digital signatures**. Every piece of data from every source needs its own chain of custody.
- **Sources**: disk or memory, firmware, file system, other systems, servers, network devices, firewall logs, a VM **snapshot**, and easily overlooked places like log files, the recycle bin, temporary storage, browser bookmarks and saved logins.
- **Documentation/report**: a summary of the event, detailed steps from source to acquired data and the integrity checks, an **analysis** (factual description of structure and use), and a **conclusion**.
- **Preservation**: work on **copies** so the original is untouched (and mobile devices can be remotely wiped, so copy them). Prefer **live collection**, especially for systems with encryption that locks when powered off.
- **E-discovery**: collecting, preparing, reviewing, interpreting and producing electronic documents. It is about acquisition, with no analysis required. Often paired with forensics: you create a drive image, then the forensics team determines whether deleted data exists and recovers it.

### Exam traps

- **Contain before eradicate.** Don't wipe a system before capturing evidence unless policy says otherwise.
- **Chain of custody** is about *handling*; **hashing** proves *integrity*; both are needed.
- Capture **memory first** (most volatile).
- A **tabletop** is discussion only.
- **Lessons learned** happens *after* recovery.
- Legal hold stops routine deletion.
