Monitoring turns raw activity into alerts people can act on. This objective covers what to monitor, how logs are gathered and analyzed, and the tools that make it practical.

### What to monitor

- **Systems and computing resources**: logins and where from, service activity, backups, installed software and versions, patch status.
- **Applications**: availability, errors, unusual usage, performance.
- **Infrastructure**: firewalls, IPS, VPN connections (employees vs vendors vs guests), switches and routers, cloud services.
- **Network traffic**: volume and destinations. A large unexplained transfer can mean data exfiltration.

Monitoring is a **detective** control. Real-time monitoring also supports **reaction** (quarantine, disable an account).

### Monitoring activities

| Activity | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **Log aggregation** | Collect logs from many systems into one place, normalize formats (usually a SIEM) |
| **Scanning** | Regularly inventory devices and check versions, drivers, patches and vulnerabilities |
| **Reporting** | Build reports from the stored data: compliance, patch status, OS versions, trends, "how many systems are affected by today's new CVE?" (including forward-looking views like end-of-life dates) |
| **Archiving** | Retain logs for investigations, legal and regulatory needs. Attackers can sit undetected for months, so long retention matters |
| **Alerting** | Notify people when something actionable happens (spike in authentication failures, large transfer, new admin account) |
| **Response / quarantine** | Isolate the affected system to prevent further spread |
| **Alert tuning** | Adjust thresholds and rules to cut noise |

### Alert quality

- **False positive**: alert fires but nothing is wrong. Too many cause **alert fatigue**, and analysts start ignoring them.
- **False negative**: a real attack produces no alert. The dangerous one.
- **Alert tuning** is the continuous balance between the two. Set thresholds, whitelist known-good activity, add context, and review rules regularly.
- Alerts can be e-mailed, texted, paged, or sent to a **SOC** (security operations center) dashboard or ticketing system.

### Tools

- **SIEM (security information and event management)**: central log collection plus **correlation**, alerting, dashboards and reporting. Pulls from firewalls, servers, endpoints, IDS/IPS, applications. Correlates events across sources (e.g. failed VPN logins followed by a success from a new country). Supports forensic search and compliance reports.
- **SOAR (security orchestration, automation and response)**: automates response with playbooks (disable account, block IP, open a ticket). See 4.7.
- **SCAP (Security Content Automation Protocol)**: NIST standards that give tools a common language for vulnerabilities and configuration so scanners, patch systems and reporting tools can interoperate. Components include **CVE**, **CVSS**, **OVAL** and **XCCDF** checklists for compliance.
- **Benchmarks and baselines**: **CIS benchmarks**, vendor guides and DISA STIGs are used to measure configuration against a standard.
- **Agents and agentless monitoring**: software on the host vs remote polling (SNMP, WMI, API).
- **NetFlow / sFlow / IPFIX**: summaries of network conversations (who talked to whom, how much). Good for spotting exfiltration and scanning without capturing content.
- **Antivirus / EDR / XDR**: endpoint telemetry; **DLP** for data leaving; **vulnerability scanners** (Nessus, OpenVAS); **protocol analyzers** (Wireshark).
- **SNMP** (versions 1/2 use cleartext community strings; **SNMPv3** adds authentication and encryption) for device status; **SNMP traps** push alerts.
- **Syslog** for log forwarding.

### More detail from the lessons

**What gets monitored and why**

- Monitor authentications (logins from where, such as lots of logins from a country with no employees), services (type and amount of activity, backup completion, software versions to decide patching), applications (availability), and traffic volume (a sudden spike above the norm may mean exfiltration). Stay in contact with software vendors so you hear about security issues.
- Infrastructure examples: VPN users broken down into employees, vendors and guests; firewall and IPS spikes in attacks as a warning. Roll everything into **dashboards** for a glance at posture.
- A **SIEM** consolidates logs from firewalls, switches, servers and routers (different formats) into one database, allowing correlation (VPN authentication, then what was accessed), reports on application access, and data-transfer baselines with alerts when exceeded.

**Scanning and reporting**

- Devices constantly move (laptops, phones, tablets), so continuously scan for OS versions, driver versions, installed apps and log anomalies, and store results in a database for later reports. Make reports **actionable**: not just how many devices are compliant, but **which are not** and what is needed.
- When a new vulnerability drops, the database lets you instantly report how many systems are affected. **Ad hoc "what if" reports**: for example how many systems will be exposed when an OS reaches end of life in six months.

**Archiving**

- An **IBM security report from 2022** said it takes about **nine months** on average to identify and contain a breach, so keep long-term logs and backups. Some laws require collecting data for a set period.

**Alerts**

- Examples of alert triggers: more authentication errors (password spraying or brute force), or a large data transfer from the data center to an internet site. Deliver by **SMS**, **email**, or to a separate **SOC** alert group, not only a screen or morning report.
- Common reaction: **quarantine** the system so the attacker cannot reach others. Accuracy is a balancing act: **false positives** are inaccurate alerts, **false negatives** are events never logged or alerted. Tuning takes time, after which alerts reflect what is really happening.

**Tools in detail**

- **SCAP** (maintained by NIST, scap.nist.gov): a common language so a next-gen firewall, IPS and vulnerability scanner all call the same vulnerability the same thing. This lets tools automate the chain: scanner finds it, management system pushes the patch, no human needed.
- **Benchmarks**: the **Center for Internet Security (CIS)** has a library (cissecurity.org). Example: a mobile benchmark disables screenshots and screen recording, blocks voice calls while locked, and forces encrypted backups.
- **Agent vs agentless compliance checks**: checks run at connection or login. An **agent** is installed beforehand, always on, but needs ongoing updates of code and rules. **Agentless** runs on demand (for example at VPN login), executes in memory, verifies compliance and **removes itself**; no maintenance, but it only runs when triggered so must be run regularly.
- **SIEM** also supports correlation across VPN logins, application server activity and firewall blocks, plus **forensic** reports of what happened before an event.
- **Antivirus vs anti-malware**: the terms are effectively interchangeable today. **DLP** looks for and blocks sensitive data (Social Security numbers, medical info, card data) in real time; can run on endpoints or in cloud systems.
- **SNMP**: built into most devices. Metrics live in the **MIB (Management Information Base)** identified by **OIDs** (numeric object identifiers). Polling uses **UDP 161**: the management station asks a router how many bytes crossed an interface, waits (5 or 10 minutes), asks again, and graphs the changes. **SNMP traps** are proactive alerts from the agent to the station on **UDP 162**; example: send a trap when CRC errors rise by 5, and the station then fires alerts, SMS or automation.
- **NetFlow**: standard for traffic flow and application-use statistics. A **probe** (in a switch/router software or external, fed by a **SPAN** port or **tap**) sends metrics to a **collector** that shows top 10 conversations, top 10 endpoints, and traffic events.
- **Vulnerability scanner**: minimally invasive, no exploits, includes port scans and discovery of active hosts across an IP range. Scan internally, and also from outside to see what an attacker would see. Verify results because not everything is accurate. Sample critical finding: a server random-number-generator flaw allowing remote shell, and an unsupported Unix OS.

### Real-world rhythm

1. Collect logs and scan results.
2. SIEM normalizes, correlates and stores.
3. Rules and thresholds raise alerts.
4. Analysts investigate, tune rules, escalate to incident response.
5. Reports show trends and compliance.

### Exam traps

- **SIEM** = collection + correlation + alerting. **SOAR** = automated response.
- **SCAP** standardizes vulnerability/config data between tools; it isn't a scanner.
- **False negatives** are a bigger risk than false positives, but too many false positives cause alert fatigue.
- **NetFlow** shows traffic metadata, not packet contents.
- **SNMPv3** is the secure version.
