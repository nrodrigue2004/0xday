A **threat vector** is the path an attacker uses to get in. The **attack surface** is every place a vector could exist. This objective splits into technical vectors (software, networks, devices) and human vectors (social engineering).

### Common threat vectors

| Vector | What it means | Key defenses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Message-based** | Email, SMS, instant messages, collaboration chat carry links, attachments and scams. Email is the biggest one | Spam and attachment filtering, link rewriting, DMARC/DKIM/SPF, user awareness |
| **Image-based** | Malicious code or links hidden in image files (SVG with scripts, steganography) | Content inspection, don't auto-render untrusted images |
| **File-based** | Documents, PDFs, archives, macros, executables | Sandboxing, disable macros, attachment scanning, application control |
| **Voice call** | Vishing, spoofed caller ID, deepfake voice | Call-back verification, policy, awareness |
| **Removable device** | USB drives dropped in a parking lot, malicious cables, autorun malware | Disable USB ports or autorun, USB data blockers, endpoint control |
| **Vulnerable software** | Unpatched code. **Client-based** (agent installed) or **agentless** (scanned remotely) | Patching, vulnerability scanning |
| **Unsupported systems and applications** | No vendor patches (end of life). Becomes a permanent hole | Replace, isolate, compensating controls |
| **Unsecure network** | Wireless (open Wi-Fi, weak WPA, rogue AP), wired (unprotected ports), Bluetooth (bluejacking, bluesnarfing) | Encryption, 802.1X, NAC, disable unused ports |
| **Open service ports** | Every listening port is an entry point | Close unused ports, host firewall, scan regularly |
| **Default credentials** | admin/admin on routers, cameras, IoT | Change at install, password policy |
| **Supply chain** | Attack through a trusted vendor, MSP or supplier | Vendor assessment, contracts, monitoring |

**Supply chain pieces to recognize:** a **managed service provider (MSP)** has remote access to many customers, so compromising one MSP can reach all of them. A **vendor** supplies hardware or software. A **supplier** provides parts and components. Tainted hardware, counterfeit parts and poisoned updates are all supply chain attacks.

### Human vectors and social engineering

Social engineering manipulates people rather than systems. Attackers rely on psychological levers: **authority, urgency, scarcity, social proof, familiarity/liking, trust, fear and consensus**.

#### Phishing family

- **Phishing**: fraudulent email pretending to be a trusted sender, tricking you into clicking, opening or entering credentials. Clues: wrong or look-alike domain, mismatched link text, generic greeting, urgency, bad grammar (though modern ones are polished), unexpected attachment.
- **Spear phishing**: aimed at a specific person or group with personal details.
- **Whaling**: spear phishing aimed at senior executives.
- **Vishing**: voice phishing by phone, often with spoofed caller ID.
- **Smishing**: phishing by SMS text message.
- **Pharming**: redirects victims to a fake site by corrupting DNS or the hosts file.
- **Typosquatting** (URL hijacking): registers a mistyped domain (`exampel.com`) to catch typos. Related: **brand impersonation** (a site or email using a company's branding).
- **Business email compromise (BEC)**: a spoofed or hijacked executive or vendor email asks staff to wire money or change payment details. Defended with out-of-band verification.

#### Impersonation and pretexting

- **Impersonation**: pretending to be someone else (IT support, auditor, delivery person, executive).
- **Pretexting**: an invented scenario that justifies the request ("I'm from payroll and need to confirm your bank details").
- **Identity fraud**: using someone's stolen identity for gain.
- **Eliciting information**: casual conversation that draws out details without the target noticing.

Defense: policies for verifying identity, call-back procedures, escalation, and a culture where people can say "let me verify that".

#### Watering hole attack

The attacker compromises a website that the target group already visits (an industry forum, a supplier portal) and waits. Visitors get infected with no click on a bad link. Defense: patching, web filtering, browser isolation, endpoint protection, monitoring for odd behavior after visiting trusted sites. This is also why you can't rely on "only visit trusted sites".

#### Other social engineering

- **Misinformation / disinformation**: false information, spread by mistake or on purpose, to influence behavior or reputation.
- **Dumpster diving**: reading discarded paper for data. Shred documents.
- **Shoulder surfing**: watching a screen or keypad. Privacy screens, awareness of surroundings.
- **Tailgating / piggybacking**: following someone through a secured door. Access control vestibules, badge checks.
- **Baiting**: leaving something tempting (infected USB drive) for a victim to pick up.
- **Quid pro quo**: offering a service or benefit in return for information or access.
- **Influence campaigns**: coordinated misinformation (often state-backed) to shape opinion.
- **Hoax**: a false warning (a fake virus alert) to cause panic or unneeded actions.

### More detail from the lessons

**Vectors, with the lesson's examples**

- A threat vector (attack vector) is how an attacker gets in. Attackers hunt for unknown vectors as well as known ones.
- **Messaging**: email links and attachments, **SMS** texts, and instant or direct messages that let the attacker talk to you one on one. Typical lures are fake invoices for services never rendered and cryptocurrency scams (steal a wallet or sell coin that does not exist). A fake "USPS package suspended, incorrect address" text from an odd sender is the standard example. Report junk instead of clicking.
- **Images**: **SVG** (Scalable Vector Graphics) is an **XML** file, not just a picture, so it can carry HTML or **JavaScript**. A few lines of XML can draw a triangle and also run a script (a cross-site scripting demo). Browsers usually block XSS, but a browser bug or a non-XSS script can slip through. Input validation on SVG helps, and some browsers let you disable image types.
- **Files**: executables run in memory, but also **PDFs** (text, images and scripting in one container), **compressed archives** (zip, rar hide one bad file among thousands), and **Office files with macros** that can gather data and send it out. **Browser extensions and add-ins** can themselves be malicious.
- **Voice**: vishing, **spam over IP** (automated VoIP spam), **war dialing** (hunting for unpublished numbers), and DoS through messaging systems.
- **Removable media**: a $10 USB drive can bypass millions in firewalls and IPS, especially against an **air-gapped** network. Drop drives in the parking lot and wait. A modified USB can present itself as a **keyboard** and type commands on its own. It also makes it easy to copy large amounts of data out.
- **Vulnerable software**: keep updating (monthly Microsoft updates plus every other product). **Agentless** web-based systems have a central server: infect it and every client that logs in gets a fresh infected instance.
- **Unsupported systems**: no patches = large risk. The classic case is an old OS on an old PC under a desk that IT does not even know exists. Fix by keeping an **up-to-date inventory** and scanning the network regularly.
- **Unsecure networks**: use WPA3 instead of WEP, WPA or WPA2, scan for open or **rogue wireless access points**, and turn on **802.1X** for wired and wireless. Bluetooth can be used for recon or exploited via weak implementations.
- **Open ports**: a web server uses TCP 80 and 443. Each new service needs its own port, so more services means more exposure and more misconfiguration chances. Use port-based or application-aware firewalls to expose only the one service needed.
- **Default credentials**: admin/admin on routers and access points. Many devices now force a change at first login. Sites such as routerpasswords.com list defaults for thousands of devices, so attackers can look them up.
- **Supply chain**: tampering can happen at manufacturing (maker unaware) or afterward by a third party. An **MSP** is a high-value target. The **Target 2013** breach came through an **HVAC contractor** network, then to the point-of-sale systems to steal card numbers. **Counterfeit hardware**: fake Cisco Catalyst switches in 2020, spotted because they could not update software properly.

**Phishing details**

- Phishing is social engineering through mail, text or other channels to make you give up private data. Check where links really point, and watch for off fonts and spacing, a deadline ("you will be blocked"), and a sender whose domain does not fit the service (an iCloud sender for a Rackspace notice).
- Best practice: **never click a link in an email**. A fake login page can closely copy the real one, sometimes even reusing the same email image.
- Stolen email access is powerful: send mail as you, search for financial info, and **use password reset** on other sites (the reset goes to the attacker-controlled mailbox).
- **Typosquatting** example from the lesson: professormessor.com instead of professormesser.com. **Pretexting** is outright lying with a made-up story (a "Visa" call about a failed electric bill payment). **Vishing** spoofs caller ID. **Smishing** is SMS phishing. Other scams to know: fake check scam, phone verification code scam.

**Impersonation details**

- Scam call samples: a "Microsoft Windows" urgent checkup, a "US Treasury enforcement action", a "0% rate for your excellent payment history". Impersonation is pretending to be someone you are not, to build trust: claim to be the **help desk**, a **VP of finance or manufacturing** (rank), or bury you in technical jargon.
- Goal is **eliciting information** through a story, never a direct request for your bank number.
- **Identity fraud**: open credit cards in your name mailed to their address (shows up as nonpayment on your credit report), open bank accounts in your name to stash money and take loans, or **tax fraud** by filing your return first and collecting your refund.
- Prevention: do not volunteer information, remember help desks never need your password, never give personal data (address, SSN, birthday) by phone or email, and **verify by calling a public number** yourself. Verification should be normal procedure in your organization.

**Watering hole details**

- When employees will not plug in parking-lot USBs or click email links, the attacker poisons a site you will visit later (a local sandwich shop's ordering site). It needs research on which third-party sites staff use plus a vulnerability on that site, or emailing attachments to that third party to get into their network and then yours.
- **January 2017 example**: the Polish Financial Supervision Authority, the Mexican National Banking and Securities Commission and a state-owned bank in Uruguay had malicious **JavaScript** added, served **only to IP addresses of particular financial organizations**; everyone else saw a normal site. The outcome was never made public. Symantec antivirus did detect and block the code on visitors who had it.
- Defense is **defense in depth**: antivirus, firewall and IPS stacked (that is why firewalls and IPS ship together). The firewall may pass the traffic, but the IPS recognizes the malicious content.

**Misinformation and brand impersonation**

- Misinformation and disinformation are **factually incorrect**, not just opinion, usually meant to divide or confuse groups. **Influence campaigns** are often run by nation states on social media or even in ads, to persuade or to distract from damaging truths.
- Steps: create many **fake accounts**, post content from one, **amplify** with likes, shares and follows, the platform's algorithm shows it to real users, who share it, and eventually mass media reports on it.
- **Brand impersonation**: build hundreds or thousands of sites using a famous brand (Coca-Cola, McDonald's), get them indexed by Google, and search results redirect victims to a "you won" or "download this software" page that delivers adware, tracking or data exfiltration.

### Exam traps

- **Spear phishing** = targeted. **Whaling** = targeted at executives. **Vishing** = voice. **Smishing** = SMS.
- **Watering hole** hits a site the *targets use*, not the targets directly.
- **Typosquatting** = a deliberately mistyped domain. **Pharming** = DNS redirect with a correct URL typed.
- **Tailgating** can be unknowing (someone follows you through); **piggybacking** often has the authorized person's consent. The exam usually treats them together.
- **Default credentials** and **open ports** are configuration vectors, not software bugs.
- The attack surface shrinks when you remove unused services, ports, accounts and software.
