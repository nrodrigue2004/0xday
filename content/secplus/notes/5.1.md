Governance is how an organization sets, communicates and enforces security expectations. This objective is a hierarchy: policies say *what* and *why*, standards say *how much*, procedures say *how exactly*.

### Documents and what each is

| Document | Purpose | Detail level | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Policy** | High-level statement of management intent and rules; approved by leadership | Broad, rarely changes | "All laptops must use full-disk encryption" |
| **Standard** | Specific, measurable requirement that implements a policy | Mandatory specifics | "AES-256, managed through BitLocker with TPM" |
| **Procedure** | Step-by-step instructions to carry out a task | Detailed, changes often | "How to enable BitLocker and escrow the recovery key" |
| **Guideline** | Recommended practice, not mandatory | Advice | "Prefer passphrases of 4+ words" |

> Order from top to bottom: **policy → standard → procedure**. Policies are the *why*, procedures the *how*.

### Common policies

- **Acceptable use policy (AUP)**: what users may and may not do with company systems and data.
- **Information security policies**: overall program rules.
- **Business continuity** and **disaster recovery**: how operations continue and recover.
- **Incident response** policy and plan.
- **Software development life cycle (SDLC)** policy: security built into development.
- **Change management** policy (see 1.3).
- **Access control / identity** policies, **password** policy, **data classification and handling**, **remote access/BYOD**, **clean desk**, **retention** and **privacy**.

### Standards

Specify what must be used or achieved:

- **Password** standards (length, complexity, history, lockout).
- **Access control** standards (who can approve, how long reviews take).
- **Physical security** standards.
- **Encryption** standards (algorithms, key lengths).
- Based on frameworks and regulations: NIST, ISO/IEC 27001, CIS Controls, PCI DSS.

### Procedures

Documented, repeatable steps so everyone does it the same way:

- **Onboarding and offboarding** (provision accounts, collect devices, disable access).
- **Playbooks** for incident types.
- **Change management** procedure and approval flow.
- **Patching**, **backup and restore**, **account recovery**.
- Procedures must be **tested** and **kept current**.

### External considerations

Governance is shaped by outside forces:

- **Regulatory**: laws and rules that apply to your industry or location (HIPAA, GDPR, PCI DSS, GLBA, SOX).
- **Legal**: contracts, liability, litigation, data protection.
- **Industry** requirements and standards.
- **Local, regional, national and global** differences, such as data residency.

### Monitoring and revision

Policies must be reviewed regularly, especially after incidents, audits, regulatory changes or business changes. Track exceptions and keep approval records.

### Governance structure and data roles

- **Boards and committees**: oversight and strategy (a security steering committee or board).
- **Government entities** and regulators.
- **Centralized vs decentralized** governance.

**Data roles and responsibilities** (from 3.3 and here):

| Role | Responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| **Data owner** | Senior person accountable for a dataset; decides classification and who can access it |
| **Data steward** | Manages data quality, labels and metadata |
| **Data custodian / processor** (technical) | Implements storage, backup, access controls |
| **Data controller** | Decides why and how personal data is processed |
| **Data processor** | Handles personal data on the controller's behalf |
| **Data protection officer (DPO)** | Advises on and monitors privacy compliance |
| **Data subject** | The individual the data is about |

### More detail from the lessons

- **Policies** state what to do and why; the technical controls from 1.1 are how they are enforced. Policies also answer scenario questions (virus found, home VPN access, vulnerability exploited) and list **roles and responsibilities**. They are only documents: the organization must enforce them. An **AUP** also protects the company legally if someone is dismissed for breaking it.
- **Business continuity** example: when card processing is down, retail staff take manual transactions and phone the card company to approve. It must be planned and tested before the event. A **disaster recovery plan** is continuity for widespread or long outages (natural, technical or human-caused) and covers an alternate location, data recovery, application restoration and staff availability there.
- **Incident roles**: incident response team, IT security management, compliance officers, technical staff and users who saw the event. **NIST SP 800-61 Rev 2** lifecycle: preparation, detection and analysis, containment, eradication and recovery, post-incident activity.
- **SDLC**: **Waterfall** is linear (requirements, development, testing, deployment, maintenance); **Agile** cycles design, develop, test, deploy and review constantly.
- **Change management** records frequency, duration, install process and a **fallback procedure**. Steps: scope (one server or many, one file or an OS), risk, formal plan, approval, backup plan, then documentation. A **change control board** approves and schedules.
- **Standards** come from ISO and NIST or are written in house. Password standards cover complexity, resets, change frequency, storage and approved managers; a standard may forbid local accounts on network gear and require LDAP to Active Directory. Access standards may require MAC instead of DAC, sign-off or training before access, and removal on a security issue, expiry, departure or contract end. Physical standards cover badges, electronic locks, biometrics, motion detection and visitor escorts, differing for employee, contractor and guest. Encryption standards fix the hash or algorithm per data state (use, transit, rest).
- **Procedures**: onboarding gives the handbook and AUP sign-off, accounts, permissions and devices. Offboarding decides what happens to hardware and data; **disable rather than delete** the account because deleting can lose decryption keys. **Playbooks** (breach investigation, ransomware recovery) can be automated in a **SOAR** platform so staff focus on harder work. Revisit playbooks when new tech or emerging threats appear.
- **Governance structure**: a board sets broad objectives; committees of subject matter experts do the work and report back for approval. Government bodies add legal, administrative and political concerns and often hold open meetings. **Centralized** means one group decides; **decentralized** lets those doing the work decide.
- **Regulation and legal**: **SOX** (2002, public company finances), **HIPAA** (health data storage, transfer and disclosure). Teams need a process to report illegal activity, respond to a **legal hold**, and meet breach disclosure deadlines that vary by region. Some countries require citizens' data to stay in-country. Industries differ: power generation is often air-gapped, healthcare needs heavy encryption with wide access. Scope grows from local to national (defense) to global companies facing many differing laws.
- **Data roles**: the **owner** is a senior person (the VP of sales for customer data, the treasurer for finances) ultimately responsible. The **controller** decides how data is used (a payroll department) and the **processor** acts on those instructions (the payroll company). The **custodian/steward** keeps data accurate, private and compliant, assigns sensitivity labels and ties them to access control.

### Exam traps

- A policy is **mandatory and high-level**; a guideline is **optional advice**.
- **Procedures** are the most detailed and change most often.
- The **data owner** is accountable; the **custodian** implements.
- Regulatory vs legal: regulations from agencies; legal covers contracts and court obligations. Questions often just want "external considerations".
