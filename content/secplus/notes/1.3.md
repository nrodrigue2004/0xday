Change management is the formal process for altering systems without breaking them or opening a hole. Uncontrolled changes cause outages and are a common root cause of security gaps, so the exam treats the process as a security control.

### Business process side

A good change request goes through the same stages every time:

1. **Request and ownership**: someone proposes the change and a named **owner** is responsible for it.
2. **Stakeholders**: everyone affected is identified and informed (application owners, help desk, other teams, sometimes customers).
3. **Impact analysis**: what could go wrong, what depends on this system, how much risk does it add, what is the benefit.
4. **Test results**: the change was tried in a sandbox or test environment first. A sandbox lets you try the change safely without touching production.
5. **Approval process**: a change advisory board or an approver signs off. Approvals leave a record of who decided and why.
6. **Backout plan**: the exact steps to undo the change if it fails. Written and tested before the change starts. A change without a backout plan should not be approved.
7. **Maintenance window**: the scheduled low-impact time (late night, weekend) when the change happens, so fewer users are disrupted.
8. **Standard operating procedure (SOP)**: the documented, repeatable way to do routine changes the same way every time.

> Exam pattern: "what should have been done before the change failed?" Look for testing, a backout plan, or an approval.

### Technical implications (what the change touches)

- **Allow lists and deny lists**: firewall rules, application control and URL filters change what is permitted. Allow lists permit only listed items (secure by default). Deny lists block listed items and allow everything else.
- **Restricted activities**: a change may be limited by scope, so you document exactly what is permitted and what is not (for example, "restart the web service only, do not touch the database").
- **Downtime**: planned outages must be scheduled and communicated.
- **Service restart** and **application restart**: some patches only take effect after a restart. Restarting can drop connections, interrupt jobs and expose startup problems.
- **Legacy applications**: old software may break with a new OS, library or security setting, or may not be patchable at all. Compensating controls (isolation, firewall rules) are common.
- **Dependencies**: services rely on other services, databases, libraries or accounts. Changing one thing can break another far away. Map dependencies during impact analysis.

### Documentation and version control

- **Update diagrams** (network, data flow) and **update policies and procedures** after every change so documentation matches reality. Stale diagrams hurt incident response and audits.
- **Version control** keeps every revision of configs, scripts and documents, shows who changed what and when, and lets you **roll back** to a known good version.
- Keep records of the change itself: ticket, approval, test results, date, who implemented it.

### Putting it together

| Stage | Question to ask |
| --- | --- |
| Before | Who owns it? Who is affected? What is the impact? Was it tested? Who approved? Is there a backout plan? |
| During | Is it in the maintenance window? Is it limited to the approved scope? |
| After | Did it work? Are diagrams, policies and version history updated? Were stakeholders told? |

### Exam traps

- A **backout plan** is about reversing a failed change. A **disaster recovery plan** is about recovering from a major failure. Do not mix them.
- Emergency changes still need documentation, even if approval is fast.
- Change management protects **availability** and **integrity** first.
