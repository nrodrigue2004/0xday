Vulnerability management is a repeating cycle: **find** weaknesses, **analyze** which matter, **fix or mitigate** them, **verify** the fix, and **report**.

### Identification methods

- **Vulnerability scanning**: automated tools probe systems for known weaknesses (missing patches, misconfigurations, open ports, default credentials). Scan types:
  - **Credentialed** (authenticated) scans log in and see much more (installed software, local settings). Fewer false positives.
  - **Non-credentialed** scans see only what an outsider sees.
  - **Intrusive** scans actively test and can disrupt systems; **non-intrusive** scans avoid that.
  - **Agent-based** vs **agentless**; **internal** vs **external**; **compliance** scans against a standard.
  - Use the **Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE)** list and the **NVD**; scanners rely on updated signature feeds.
- **Application security testing**: **static analysis (SAST)** of source code, **dynamic analysis (DAST)** of the running app, **fuzzing**, **package monitoring** for vulnerable libraries.
- **Penetration testing**: authorized attackers try to exploit weaknesses to prove real impact. Unlike a scan, it exploits. Types:
  - **Known environment (white box)**: full information. **Partially known (gray)**. **Unknown environment (black box)**: tester starts like an outsider.
  - **Rules of engagement** define scope, timing and limits; get **written authorization**.
  - **Reconnaissance** can be **passive** (OSINT, no direct contact) or **active** (probing the target).
  - Phases: planning, discovery/recon, attack/exploitation (including **lateral movement**, **privilege escalation**, **persistence**, **pivoting**), **cleanup**, **reporting**.
- **Responsible disclosure and bug bounty**: a program that invites researchers to report flaws privately for recognition or payment.
- **System and process audit**: checking configuration and procedures against policy.

### Threat intelligence

**Threat feeds** supply indicators and context so you know what to look for.

- **Open-source intelligence (OSINT)**: public sources (news, social media, vulnerability databases, public repositories, government bulletins).
- **Proprietary / third-party (commercial)** feeds: paid, curated, often more timely.
- **Information-sharing organizations**: ISACs and ISAOs where members share threats in an industry.
- **Dark web** monitoring: leaked credentials and chatter about your organization.
- Standards **STIX** (format) and **TAXII** (transport) automate sharing; **indicators of compromise** are the unit of exchange.
- Also used for: threat maps, **vulnerability databases**, public/private information sharing, and **tactics, techniques and procedures (TTPs)** analysis.

### Analyzing vulnerabilities

- **Confirm** the finding: a **false positive** is reported but not real; a **false negative** is a real flaw the scan missed (dangerous). Verify with logs, config checks or manual testing.
- **Prioritize and classify** by severity and context:
  - **CVSS** (Common Vulnerability Scoring System): 0 to 10 base score (Low 0.1-3.9, Medium 4.0-6.9, High 7.0-8.9, Critical 9.0-10.0), plus temporal and environmental adjustments.
  - **Exposure factor** and **environmental variables** (is it internet-facing? what data is on it?).
  - **Industry/organizational impact** (regulations, business criticality).
  - **Risk tolerance** (how much exposure the business accepts).
  - Whether an **exploit exists** or is being used in the wild raises priority.
- Treat **zero-days**, **legacy** systems and **unpatchable** devices as special cases.

### Remediation

- **Patching**: apply vendor fixes; test first, schedule through change management.
- **Insurance**: transfer part of the financial risk (does not fix the flaw).
- **Segmentation**: isolate the vulnerable system.
- **Compensating controls**: alternative protection when you can't fix (WAF rule, firewall block, extra monitoring).
- **Exceptions and exemptions**: formally accepted when remediation isn't possible or practical, with documented justification, approval and review date. This is risk **acceptance**.
- **Configuration changes** and **removing** the vulnerable software.

### Validation and reporting

- **Rescanning** after the fix to confirm it's gone.
- **Verification**: confirm the system still works and the fix held.
- **Audit** the process; maintain evidence.
- **Reporting**: communicate findings, risk, status and trends to technical staff and management. Include severity, affected assets, owner, due date and the plan.

### More detail from the lessons

**Scanning**

- A vulnerability scan checks whether an attack is **possible** and does not attack (otherwise it is a pen test). A **port scan** is the simplest example: open ports are not necessarily vulnerable but show where an attacker could look. Scan from outside, and also from **inside** to cover insider threats.
- Reports are huge and not always accurate, so review for **false positives**. Severity levels: critical, high, medium, low, informational. Sample findings: an OpenSSH/OpenSSL **random number generator weakness** (weak SSH host keys, critical) and **unsupported Unix OS detection** (a system you did not know was still running and cannot be patched). Verify findings, then run confirmed ones through **change control** to patch.
- **SAST** reviews source for buffer overflows and injections but misses implementation problems like authentication and cryptography. Sample output: `test.c` line 73, `SetSecurityDescriptorDacl`, never create a **NULL ACL**.
- **Fuzzing / dynamic analysis** (also fault injection, robustness testing) feeds random input and watches for crashes, server errors or exceptions. The first fuzzer came from a **1988** University of Wisconsin class project led by **Professor Barton Miller**. It is automated with hundreds or thousands of iterations. A free tool is CERT's **Basic Fuzzing Framework (BFF)**.
- **Package monitoring**: verify packages came from the manufacturer, not a third party, and test unknown packages in a **lab environment** before production.

**Threat intelligence**

- Know the threats and the **threat actors** (hacker groups, their tools). Use it to choose tools and training, and share across research and IT teams.
- **OSINT** sources: hacking-group discussion groups, social media, researchers, government hearings and reports, and public commercial data like financial reports. **Commercial/third-party services** see many organizations at once, spot trends and alert early.
- **Cyber Threat Alliance (CTA)**: members submit threat data in a standard format, the alliance validates and **scores** each submission for severity, and members use it. The **dark web** (an overlay network on the internet that needs special software) shows hacking groups, tools and techniques, and stores selling stolen data such as credit cards. Monitor it for your organization's name.

**Penetration testing**

- Pen tests are actual exploits and may be required at regular intervals. NIST SP 800-115 is the technical guide to security testing and assessment.
- **Rules of engagement**: when testing is allowed, test types (on-site physical breach, internal or external), hours (for example after 6 PM only), in-scope IP ranges, **out-of-scope** systems, **emergency contacts**, and how to handle sensitive data found. Exploits can crash systems (a buffer overflow for privilege escalation), so protect critical production systems.
- Techniques per system: password brute force, social engineering (can be by phone), database injection, buffer overflow.
- After the first access: **lateral movement**, **persistence** (a backdoor, a new account, changing passwords, since the original hole might be patched), **pivoting** (use the compromised host as a relay/proxy to reach other internal systems).
- **Responsible disclosure and bug bounty**: a researcher reports to the developer, who fixes, tests and publishes a patch; only afterward is the issue published in the **CVE** list, often weeks or months later. Bounties are funded by the developer.

**Analyzing**

- **False positive**: report says it exists but it does not. Low/informational findings that are real are not false positives. **False negative**: real flaw the scanner missed, which is worse. **Update scanner signatures before scanning** to reduce both; ask the vendor for updated signatures if needed.
- **CVSS** scores 0 to 10 (10 most critical) and appears in multiple versions (CVSS 2.0, 3.x). The **NVD (nvd.nist.gov)** syncs with the **CVE** list (cve.mitre.org). Vendors keep their own databases (Microsoft security bulletins). Scanners usually give the CVE; some findings are generic with no CVE, so research or verify.
- Scanners also cover applications (CVE-2020-1889, WhatsApp desktop bypass), web applications (CVE-2020-24981, UCMS 1.4.8 access control), and network devices (CVE-2020-2579, D-Link).
- **Exposure factor** is a percentage: a flaw that disables a service half the time is 50%; one with no patch on a public web server that fully disables the service is 100%. Combine with CVSS to prioritize.
- **Environmental variables**: public cloud versus isolated lab. **Industry impact**: **Tallahassee Memorial Health, February 2023**, ransomware closed the hospital for two weeks (ER diversions, surgeries canceled); **March 2019 DDoS** on Salt Lake City and LA County power generators. Consider user numbers and type, internal vs external, whether the app is critical or revenue-generating, and exploit ease.
- **Risk tolerance** decides patch order and how much testing is acceptable before deploying. A widely affecting, easily exploited flaw means low tolerance and rushed testing.

**Remediation details**

- Patches arrive on a schedule (weekly/monthly) or **out of cycle** for severe flaws or zero-days.
- **Cyber insurance** pays after events (revenue loss, data recovery, phishing losses, lawsuits) but excludes things like intentional acts and some fund transfers. Ransomware has increased uptake.
- **Segmentation**: VLANs or separate switches (air gap: customer A and B on different switches). Different VLANs only talk through a router. **NGFWs** show application-level flows between segments (spotting SSH leaving a server).
- When you cannot patch: **disable the vulnerable service**, **revoke access** to the app, use the **edge firewall** policy, router **ACLs**, or a software firewall on the server. Patching is best; these are **compensating controls**.
- **Exceptions/exemptions** are decided by a security or change committee, not one person. Example: a flaw exploitable only locally on a server inside a data center may be reasonable to leave unpatched until the conflict is resolved.
- **Validation**: scan after patching to confirm the fix and find missed systems; audit samples manually; use **reporting** (total vulnerabilities, patched vs unpatched, new threats over time, patch errors, exceptions and exemptions, systems left to patch).

### Exam traps

- **Credentialed scans** give more accurate results than non-credentialed.
- A **vulnerability scan** finds weaknesses; a **penetration test** exploits them.
- **False negative** (missed vulnerability) is more dangerous than a false positive.
- **CVE** identifies a specific flaw; **CVSS** rates how severe it is.
- Cyber **insurance** transfers financial risk; it doesn't remediate.
- Always **rescan** to validate after remediation.
