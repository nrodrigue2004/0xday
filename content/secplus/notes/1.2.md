Objective 1.2 is the vocabulary the rest of the exam leans on: the CIA triad, non-repudiation, AAA, gap analysis, zero trust, physical security and deception technology.

### The CIA triad

| Goal | Question it answers | How you provide it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confidentiality | Can only the right people read it? | Encryption, access controls, multifactor authentication |
| Integrity | Is it exactly what was sent, unmodified? | Hashing, digital signatures, certificates, non-repudiation |
| Availability | Can authorized people use it when needed? | Redundancy, fault tolerance, patching, backups |

- Sometimes written **AIC** so nobody confuses it with the intelligence agency.
- Confidentiality example: an encrypted message looks like noise to anyone in the middle. Access control example: marketing staff can edit marketing decks but cannot open the finance share.
- Integrity example: the sender publishes a hash next to a download. You hash your copy. A mismatch means it changed or was corrupted.
- Availability example: two power supplies in a server so that one failing does not take it down. Unpatched systems are less stable and more exploitable, so patching is also an availability control.
- A trade-off is built in: stronger confidentiality (more checks) can reduce availability (slower or more failed access).

### Non-repudiation

Non-repudiation means a sender cannot credibly deny that they sent or approved something. It needs two proofs:

- **Proof of integrity**: the content did not change. A **hash** (message digest, fingerprint) gives this. Change one character in an 8 MB file and the hash is completely different, even though the file size is identical.
- **Proof of origin** (authentication of the source): it came from that person. A **digital signature** gives this.

A hash alone proves the data is unchanged but not who produced it. Signatures add the "who".

**How a digital signature works (Alice sends to Bob):**

1. Alice hashes her message.
2. She encrypts that hash with her **private key**. The result is the signature.
3. She sends the message and signature together.
4. Bob decrypts the signature with Alice's **public key**, which gives the original hash.
5. Bob hashes the message he received himself.
6. If both hashes match, the message is unchanged and only Alice's private key could have produced the signature.

> Key rule: you **sign with the private key** and **verify with the public key**. Encryption for confidentiality is the reverse: encrypt with the recipient's public key, decrypt with their private key.

### AAA: authentication, authorization, accounting

- **Identification**: you claim an identity (a username).
- **Authentication**: you prove it (password, MFA, certificate).
- **Authorization**: the system decides what that identity may do.
- **Accounting**: a record of who did what, when, for how long and how much (login and logout times, data volume).

**AAA server flow (VPN example):** a user logs in to a VPN concentrator or firewall. The concentrator holds no user database. It forwards the credentials to a central **AAA server** (RADIUS or TACACS+ in practice). The AAA server answers approved or denied, and the concentrator then allows or blocks the session.

**Authenticating a device**, which cannot type a password: issue it a **certificate** signed by your **certificate authority (CA)**. Anyone who trusts that CA can verify the certificate really came from it. A VPN concentrator or management platform uses this to confirm a laptop is company-owned.

**Authorization models scale access.** Setting permissions user by user does not scale. Put an abstraction between users and resources, for example a **group** or **role**: "Shipping and Receiving" has the rights it needs, and adding a hundred people to the group gives them all those rights. Common models (detailed in objective 4.6): role-based, attribute-based, rule-based, mandatory and discretionary access control.

### Gap analysis

A **gap analysis** compares where you are with where you want to be, then plans the route.

1. Pick a **baseline** (the target): for example NIST SP 800-171 Rev 2 (protecting controlled unclassified information in non-federal systems), ISO/IEC 27001 (information security management systems), or one you write yourself.
2. Evaluate **people** (training, experience, awareness of policy) and **processes** (do systems follow the written policy?).
3. Break broad areas into small requirements (access control splits into account management, privileged access, access reviews, and so on) and score each one.
4. Write the report: current state, target state, and the **path** to close each gap, which costs time, money, equipment and change control.
5. A common summary is a red/yellow/green grid by site and requirement. Fix red first for the biggest security improvement.

It can take weeks, months or years for a large organization.

### Zero trust

Traditional networks trust anything inside the firewall. **Zero trust** trusts nothing by default: every user, device and process must authenticate and be authorized for each resource, using MFA, encryption in transit and at rest, extra firewalls, permissions and policy.

The architecture splits into two planes:

| Plane | Job | Components |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Data plane** | Moves the actual traffic and enforces decisions | Subjects/systems, implicit trust zones, **policy enforcement point (PEP)** |
| **Control plane** | Decides what is allowed | **Adaptive identity**, threat scope reduction, **policy-driven access control**, **policy administrator**, **policy engine** |

- **Policy enforcement point**: the gatekeeper. Every request passes through it. It does not decide, it asks.
- **Policy decision point** = the **policy engine** (evaluates the request against policy and says grant, deny or revoke) plus the **policy administrator** (turns the decision into tokens or credentials and tells the PEP).
- **Adaptive identity**: authentication strength changes with context, such as source IP and geography, the relationship to the company (employee, contractor), physical location, connection type. A US-hosted resource requested from an unexpected country triggers extra proof.
- **Threat scope reduction**: limit how many entry points exist (only inside the building or through the VPN) so there is less to attack.
- **Policy-driven access control**: all the signals feed policy that picks the authentication needed.
- **Security zones** (trusted, untrusted, internal, external, VPN, per department) let you write rules between zones. Untrusted to trusted is denied by default. Traffic from the trusted zone to the internal zone can be treated as **implicitly trusted** along that path.
- Flow: subject (untrusted zone) to PEP, PEP asks policy administrator, policy administrator asks policy engine, answer returns, and if allowed the PEP opens access to the trusted zone and the enterprise resource.

Routers, switches and firewalls show the same split: ports forwarding frames are the data plane; the routing table, NAT config and firewall rules are the control plane. It applies to virtual and cloud devices too.

### Physical security

| Control | What it does | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bollards / barricades** | Block vehicles, let people through | Concrete posts, channel people to one entrance, can also be moats or bridges |
| **Access control vestibule** (formerly "mantrap") | Two-door room so only one door opens at a time | Variants: both unlocked until one opens; both locked and badge-controlled; one always locked. Stops tailgating, allows ID checks |
| **Fencing** | Perimeter barrier | Transparent or opaque, strong against bending, tall, razor wire on top |
| **Video surveillance (CCTV)** | Watch and record | Motion and object detection, facial and license plate recognition, networked to central storage |
| **Security guard** | Human judgment at the entrance | Often two or more for **two-person integrity** so one guard cannot bypass policy |
| **Access badge** | Photo ID worn visibly and tied to electronic locks | Every badge-in is logged centrally |
| **Lighting** | Dark areas help intruders | Correct angle matters for camera facial recognition |
| **Sensors** | Detect presence or movement | **Infrared** (heat, small areas, also night cameras), **pressure** (force change underfoot), **microwave** (large areas), **ultrasonic** (sound reflection, also collision detection) |

### Deception and disruption technology

- **Honeypot**: a decoy system that attracts attackers (usually automated tools) so you can watch their methods. Not part of production, so any traffic to it is suspect.
- **Honeynet**: many honeypots joined into a realistic network (servers, workstations, routers, firewalls) to keep attackers busy longer.
- **Honeyfile**: a bait file such as `passwords.txt` containing fake data. Nobody legitimate opens it, so an access alert means someone is snooping.
- **Honeytoken**: traceable fake data, such as fake API credentials left on a public share, fake email addresses, bogus database records, cookies or tracking pixels. When they show up elsewhere, you learn who took them.
- Attackers try to detect honeypots, so defenders keep making them more realistic. Honeypots are a detective control and a deterrent.

### More detail from the lessons

- **Integrity** can be strengthened with a digital signature (a hash encrypted with an asymmetric algorithm), and with **certificates** that identify devices or people. **Availability** comes from systems built to stay up, fault tolerance (a failed component is covered by another), and patching to keep systems stable and close security holes.
- **Hash demo**: Project Gutenberg's encyclopedia volume 1 is 8.1 MB. Changing one character leaves the size identical but produces a very different hash. A mismatch means re-download or run a diff to find the change. A hash gives integrity, not who sent it.
- **Signature demo**: Alice sends "You're hired, Bob." Her software hashes it, encrypts the hash with her private key, and sends both. Bob decrypts with Alice's public key, hashes the plaintext himself and compares; a match proves unchanged content and that Alice sent it. Anyone can verify the transaction, which is what makes it non-repudiation.
- **AAA with a VPN**: the concentrator holds no usernames or factors. It forwards the login to a central AAA server and allows access to the internal file server only after the server approves. Identification is the claim, authentication the proof, authorization the access, accounting the log of times and data volumes.
- **Device certificate chain**: create a certificate for the laptop on your CA and sign it; the CA's own certificate is signed by a root CA. At login, compare the device certificate with the trusted CA certificate. You do not store a password on field devices.
- **Why abstraction scales**: one shipping user needs a label rights, tracking, monthly reports and customer data. Mapping that per user fails with hundreds of people, so put them in a "Shipping and Receiving" group that carries those rights.
- **Gap analysis** timelines run weeks, months or years and involve many people. Baselines named: NIST SP 800-171 Rev 2 (protecting controlled unclassified information in nonfederal systems and organizations) and ISO/IEC 27001. Evaluate people (formal experience, training, knowledge of policy) and processes (do systems match the written policy). Break broad areas down: access control covers user registration and deregistration, access provisioning, privileged rights and access review. The report includes a path with time, money, equipment and change control. A red/yellow/green grid across, say, seven sites shows where to start: red first.
- **Zero trust** lessons: inside the firewall most networks are open, letting attackers and malware move freely. Zero trust adds MFA, encryption of stored and moving data, more permissions and firewalls. The planes apply to physical, virtual and cloud controls. A switch's interfaces are the data plane; its address settings and trunking configuration are the control plane.
- **Adaptive identity example**: a request for US-hosted data from a Chinese IP triggers extra checks, as do employee versus contractor status, full-time versus part-time, location and connection type. Limit entry points to people in the building or on the VPN. Zone rule example: untrusted to trusted is denied automatically, while trusted (corporate office) to internal (data center) is treated as implicitly trusted. The PEP may be several devices working together, and subjects can be users, processes or applications.
- **Physical**: vestibule variants are all doors unlocked until one opens, all locked with badge access, or one always locked. Bollards stop vehicles but let people pass, and bright colors signal a secure area. A moat with a bridge is also a barricade. Fences can be opaque or transparent, must resist bending and be high with razor wire. Cameras do motion detection, object detection, face reading and plate reading, with all video stored in one place. Two-person integrity means one guard cannot bypass policy. Badge use is logged in a central database. Light deters because intruders avoid being seen, and infrared cameras help in the dark.

### Exam traps

- Hash = integrity only. Hash + private-key signature = integrity + origin = **non-repudiation**.
- PEP enforces; policy engine decides; policy administrator relays and issues credentials.
- Control plane = configuration and decisions. Data plane = forwarding and enforcement.
- Gap analysis needs a **baseline** to compare against.
- Authentication proves identity; authorization is separate and comes after.
