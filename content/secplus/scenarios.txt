# Scenario cards. "@ objective | title" then S: scene, Q: question, + correct, - wrong, W: why.
@ 1.1 | The unpatchable controller
S: A factory PLC runs firmware the vendor will never patch. It has a known remote flaw. Production cannot stop.
Q: What do you deploy?
+ Isolate it on its own VLAN behind a firewall allowing only the engineering station (a compensating control)
- Add a warning banner to the login screen
- Reboot it nightly to clear any malware
- Replace the firewall logs with a shorter retention period
W: When the ideal fix (patching) is impossible, a compensating control reduces the risk another way. A banner is directive, a reboot does not fix the flaw.

@ 1.2 | Who did that?
S: An admin says they never deleted a customer database, but the delete came from their account. Logs show only the account name, and the password was shared among the team.
Q: Which concept is missing?
+ Non-repudiation, via unique accounts and signed or tamper-evident logs
- Confidentiality, via stronger disk encryption
- Availability, via a second database server
- Obscurity, via renaming the database
W: Shared credentials make it impossible to prove who acted. Unique IDs plus protected logs give accountability and non-repudiation.

@ 1.3 | Change on a Friday
S: A developer wants to push a firewall rule change to production at 4:45 pm Friday. There is no rollback plan.
Q: What does a sound change management process require first?
+ Documented impact analysis, approval, a test, and a backout plan
- Only verbal approval from the team lead
- Waiting until the change fails to see if anyone notices
- Disabling the monitoring to avoid false alarms
W: Change management covers approval, testing, scheduling, and a backout plan so a bad change can be reversed fast.

@ 1.4 | Storing hashed passwords
S: A breach exposes a table of user passwords hashed with unsalted MD5. Attackers crack most of them within hours using precomputed tables.
Q: What should the app have used?
+ A slow salted hash such as bcrypt, scrypt, or PBKDF2
- AES-128 with the key stored beside the table
- Base64 encoding with a longer string
- SHA-1 with no salt for speed
W: Salts defeat rainbow tables and slow hashing makes brute force costly. Encoding is not protection, and encryption with a nearby key is little better.

@ 2.1 | The CEO wire request
S: Finance gets an email from the CEO's lookalike domain demanding an urgent wire to a new vendor. The CEO is traveling and unreachable by email.
Q: What kind of attack is this and what is the best control?
+ Business email compromise; require out-of-band verification (call back on a known number) for payment changes
- Ransomware; restore from backup
- Watering hole; block the vendor site
- Tailgating; add a mantrap
W: BEC relies on impersonation and urgency. A callback process on a trusted number breaks it, since the attacker controls only the email channel.

@ 2.2 | Everyone gets the same page
S: Visitors to a trade-association site start downloading a fake browser update. The association's site was compromised, but only visitors from certain industries are served the payload.
Q: Which attack is this?
+ Watering hole
- Smishing
- Pharming against the visitors' DNS
- Bluejacking
W: A watering hole compromises a site that a target group already visits. Targeting by industry is a hint.

@ 2.3 | Prices set by the URL
S: A shopping site shows a price in the page URL (?price=49.99). A tester changes it to 0.01 and checks out successfully.
Q: What is the root problem?
+ The server trusts client-supplied data instead of validating it server-side
- The site uses HTTPS
- The tester used a VPN
- The database is not encrypted at rest
W: Never trust client input. Price and authorization checks must happen on the server. HTTPS only protects the transit, not the logic.

@ 2.4 | Spike of failed logins
S: Logs show 2,000 failed logins in an hour spread across 1,900 different accounts, each tried once or twice with the same common password.
Q: What attack is it, and what helps most?
+ Password spraying; MFA, plus alerting on one source or password hitting many accounts
- Brute force on one account; lock that account
- Rainbow table; add a salt
- Dictionary attack on the hash file; use bcrypt
W: Spraying avoids lockouts by trying few passwords across many accounts. MFA blunts it, and detection should correlate across accounts.

@ 2.5 | Plug in the stranger
S: A parking lot has 10 branded USB drives marked "Payroll 2026". Two are later seen in office computers, and an EDR alert fires.
Q: What control best prevents a repeat?
+ Block removable media by policy and train staff, with EDR or application allowlisting on top
- Remove the parking lot
- Encrypt every USB drive
- Rotate the Wi-Fi password
W: Baiting works by curiosity. Technical blocks plus awareness cover the human and the machine.

@ 3.1 | Customer data in a new cloud
S: A team puts a customer database in a cloud storage bucket and a scanner finds it publicly readable.
Q: Who is responsible for that misconfiguration?
+ The customer; under shared responsibility, access settings of your own data are yours
- The cloud provider, always
- Nobody, the default is safe
- The ISP
W: The provider secures the underlying infrastructure; the customer secures configuration, identities, and data in IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS to varying degrees.

@ 3.2 | Where does the web server go?
S: You must expose a public web server but keep the internal HR database unreachable from the internet.
Q: What is the sound layout?
+ Put the web server in a DMZ (screened subnet) and the database on an internal network, allowing only the web server to reach it on the DB port
- Place both on the same flat network behind NAT
- Put the database in the DMZ too for speed
- Open the database port to the internet with a strong password
W: A screened subnet limits the blast radius if the exposed host is compromised. Allow only the needed flow inward.

@ 3.3 | Phones that leave the building
S: An employee's laptop with customer data is stolen from a car.
Q: Which control would have made the theft a non-event?
+ Full-disk encryption with the key protected by TPM and a PIN
- A BIOS password only
- A screen saver timeout
- A cable lock at the office
W: With FDE the data is unreadable without the key. A BIOS password can be bypassed by removing the drive.

@ 3.4 | Recovery targets
S: The business can tolerate 4 hours of downtime and can afford to lose at most 15 minutes of data.
Q: What are the RTO and RPO?
+ RTO 4 hours, RPO 15 minutes
- RTO 15 minutes, RPO 4 hours
- Both 4 hours
- MTBF 4 hours, MTTR 15 minutes
W: RTO is how long to be back. RPO is how much data (time) you may lose, which sets backup frequency.

@ 4.1 | Hardening a new server
S: A fresh server arrives with default accounts, all ports open, and unneeded services enabled.
Q: What is the first hardening step?
+ Remove or disable unused accounts and services, close unneeded ports, apply a secure baseline
- Install a second antivirus
- Take it straight to production with monitoring
- Rename the server
W: Reduce the attack surface first, then patch and baseline. Default credentials are a classic hole.

@ 4.2 | Critical CVE, no patch yet
S: A scan finds a critical CVE on an internet-facing system. The vendor patch is two weeks out.
Q: What is the best interim action?
+ Apply a mitigation: virtual patch with WAF/IPS, restrict access, or disable the vulnerable feature, then track the patch
- Ignore it until the patch ships
- Delete the scan results
- Lower the CVSS score
W: Risk-based vulnerability management uses compensating mitigations while waiting. Exposure and exploitability set priority.

@ 4.3 | The odd outbound beacon
S: The SIEM shows a workstation sending small encrypted packets to the same external IP every 60 seconds for days.
Q: What does that pattern suggest?
+ Command-and-control beaconing from malware
- Normal NTP sync
- A DHCP lease renewal
- A backup job
W: Regular fixed-interval small outbound connections are a classic beacon. Investigate and isolate the host.

@ 4.4 | Alert fatigue
S: The SOC drowns in thousands of low-value alerts per day and analysts miss real incidents.
Q: What improves the situation most?
+ Tune detection rules and use SOAR playbooks to triage and enrich low-risk alerts
- Turn off the SIEM
- Hire a second intern to read every alert
- Raise every alert to critical
W: Tuning cuts false positives, and automation handles repetitive triage so analysts focus on real events.

@ 4.5 | Ransomware on the file server
S: Ransomware is encrypting a file server. Backups exist and appear intact.
Q: What do you do first?
+ Contain: isolate the host from the network, preserve evidence, then proceed to eradication and recovery
- Pay the ransom immediately
- Reformat the server before taking any images
- Notify the press
W: Containment stops the spread. Preserve evidence before wiping. Decisions about payment go through leadership and legal.

@ 4.6 | Disk image for the lawyers
S: You must collect evidence from a laptop for an investigation that may go to court.
Q: What preserves admissibility?
+ Make a forensic bit-for-bit copy with a hash, use a write blocker, and keep the chain of custody
- Copy the user folder by drag and drop
- Boot it and browse the files
- Ask the user to email you the files
W: Hashing proves the copy matches, a write blocker prevents changes, and chain of custody documents who held it.

@ 4.7 | Alert says leaver took data
S: DLP flags a departing employee uploading 4 GB of customer files to a personal cloud drive.
Q: What is the best response?
+ Preserve logs and evidence, involve HR and legal, and revoke access per policy
- Confront the employee in the hallway
- Delete the cloud drive account
- Ignore it as normal activity
W: Insider cases need legal and HR coordination plus preserved evidence. Do not tip off or tamper.

@ 4.8 | Account takeover
S: A user's mailbox forwards everything to an unknown address and they log in from two continents within an hour.
Q: Which are the best response steps?
+ Reset credentials, revoke sessions and tokens, remove forwarding rules, enable MFA, and review the scope
- Only change the user's display name
- Disable email for the whole company
- Wait for a second login
W: Reset plus revoking active sessions and tokens ensures the attacker is kicked out. Look for persistence like inbox rules.

@ 5.1 | The vendor with the keys
S: A small HVAC vendor with remote access to your network is breached, and attackers pivot into your environment.
Q: Which control best limits this?
+ Third-party risk management: least-privilege, segmented, monitored vendor access with MFA and contract security requirements
- Give the vendor a shared admin account
- Avoid contracts to reduce paperwork
- Trust their word
W: Supplier access is a major risk. TPRM, least privilege, segmentation, and audit rights reduce it.

@ 5.2 | Risk math
S: A server has an asset value of $200,000. One flood would destroy 50% of it. Floods occur about once every five years.
Q: What is the ALE?
+ $20,000 (SLE $100,000 x ARO 0.2)
- $100,000
- $200,000
- $50,000
W: SLE = asset value x exposure factor = $100,000. ARO = 0.2. ALE = SLE x ARO = $20,000.

@ 5.3 | Accept, avoid, transfer, mitigate
S: Leadership buys cyber insurance for ransomware losses instead of adding more controls.
Q: Which risk strategy is that?
+ Transfer
- Accept
- Avoid
- Mitigate
W: Insurance moves the financial impact to a third party but does not remove the risk.

@ 5.4 | Who gets the breach notice?
S: A company holding EU residents' personal data discovers a breach.
Q: Which regulation sets a 72-hour deadline to notify the supervisory authority?
+ GDPR
- SOX
- PCI DSS
- FERPA
W: GDPR requires notifying the authority within 72 hours of becoming aware of a reportable breach.

@ 5.5 | Prove it to the auditor
S: A customer wants evidence your controls work over a period of time, from an independent party.
Q: Which report fits best?
+ SOC 2 Type II
- Internal memo
- A self-assessment checklist
- SOC 2 Type I
W: Type II tests operating effectiveness over a period by an independent auditor. Type I is a point-in-time design review.

@ 5.6 | Teaching the humans
S: Phishing simulations show 30% of staff click. Leadership asks for a fix.
Q: Which program is most effective?
+ Recurring role-based awareness training, simulated phishing with feedback, and an easy way to report
- One annual slide deck
- Punishing everyone who clicks
- Blocking all email
W: Ongoing training and an easy reporting path build a culture. Punishment discourages reporting.
