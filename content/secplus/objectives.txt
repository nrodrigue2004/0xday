## 1.1 Compare and contrast various types of security controls
labs: system-hardening-windows-linux, firewalls-pfsense
- Four categories: technical (firewalls, encryption), managerial (policies, risk assessments), operational (guards, awareness training: done by people), physical (locks, fences, badges).
- Control types describe what a control does: preventive, deterrent, detective, corrective, compensating, directive.
- One control can be several types: a camera is physical, and deters and detects.
- Compensating controls are the substitute when the ideal control isn't possible (isolate an unpatchable server).
- Directive controls tell people what to do (policy, signage) but don't enforce anything.
Q: Which control category covers a security policy or a risk assessment?
A: Managerial (administrative) controls.
Q: A guard checking badges at the door is which control category?
A: Operational (it's carried out by people), and it's also physical in effect.
Q: What control type is an IDS that alerts on suspicious traffic?
A: Detective: it identifies an event but doesn't stop it.
Q: A legacy server can't be patched, so you put it on an isolated VLAN. What type of control is the VLAN?
A: Compensating: an alternative that reduces risk when the primary control isn't possible.
Q: Restoring a system from backup after ransomware is which control type?
A: Corrective.
Q: A "Authorized personnel only" sign is which control type?
A: Directive (it may also deter).
Q: Firewall rules and encryption are which control category?
A: Technical.
Q: Name the four control categories.
A: Technical, managerial (administrative), operational, and physical.
Q: A splash screen warning that unauthorized use is prohibited is which type and category?
A: Deterrent, technical.
Q: Reviewing login reports every week is which type and category?
A: Detective, managerial.
Q: Why are directive controls considered weak?
A: They only tell people what to do. They depend on people deciding correctly and complying, so nothing enforces them.
Q: What is the difference between a corrective and a compensating control?
A: Corrective acts after an event to reverse or limit damage. Compensating is a substitute used when the ideal control isn't available (for example, a firewall rule while waiting for a patch).
Q: Separation of duties is usually which control type?
A: Compensating (managerial category): no single person has complete control.
Q: A backup generator during a power outage is which type and category?
A: Compensating, physical.
Q: A motion detector is which type and category?
A: Detective, physical.
Q: Which control type discourages an attacker but doesn't actually block access?
A: Deterrent.
Q: Calling law enforcement after a break-in is which type and category?
A: Corrective, operational.

## 1.2 Summarize fundamental security concepts
labs: cryptography-applied, active-directory
- CIA triad: confidentiality, integrity, availability. Non-repudiation means you can't deny an action you took (digital signatures).
- AAA: authentication (prove who you are), authorization (what you can do), accounting (log what you did).
- Zero trust: never trust by network location. Control plane (policy engine, policy administrator) decides; data plane (policy enforcement point) enforces.
- Gap analysis compares where you are to where you want to be (a framework or baseline).
- Physical security: bollards, access control vestibules (mantraps), fencing, lighting, sensors (infrared, pressure, microwave, ultrasonic).
- Deception tech: honeypots, honeynets, honeyfiles, honeytokens.
Q: What does non-repudiation provide?
A: Proof that a specific party performed an action, so they can't deny it (usually via digital signatures).
Q: In zero trust, which component actually allows or blocks the traffic?
A: The policy enforcement point (data plane).
Q: In zero trust, what does the policy engine do?
A: Decides whether to grant access, based on policy and signals; the policy administrator then tells the enforcement point.
Q: What is a honeytoken?
A: Fake data (credentials, API key, record) that alerts you when someone uses it.
Q: What is an access control vestibule?
A: Two-door entry where only one door opens at a time, preventing tailgating (also called a mantrap).
Q: What does accounting mean in AAA?
A: Recording what users did: logs of logins, resources used, data transferred.
Q: What is a gap analysis?
A: A comparison of the current security posture against a target baseline or framework to find what's missing.
Q: Which key signs a digital signature, and which key verifies it?
A: The sender's private key signs. The sender's public key verifies.
Q: A hash proves integrity. What extra proof does a digital signature add?
A: Proof of origin (who created it), which together give non-repudiation.
Q: In AAA, what does accounting record?
A: Who did what, and when: logins, logouts, resources used, data volume.
Q: What is adaptive identity?
A: Authentication that adjusts to context (location, IP, device, relationship to the company) and asks for stronger proof when something looks unusual.
Q: Which zero trust component only enforces and never decides?
A: The policy enforcement point (PEP), which sits in the data plane.
Q: What two parts make up the policy decision point?
A: The policy engine (decides) and the policy administrator (issues tokens/credentials and tells the PEP).
Q: What is threat scope reduction?
A: Limiting the number of entry points into the network so there is less to attack.
Q: What does a gap analysis need before it starts?
A: A baseline to compare current state against, such as NIST SP 800-171 or ISO/IEC 27001.
Q: What is the purpose of an access control vestibule?
A: To stop tailgating by letting only one door open at a time so identity can be checked before entry.
Q: Which sensor suits a large open area: infrared or microwave?
A: Microwave. Infrared suits smaller areas.
Q: What is two-person integrity?
A: Two or more people (such as guards) are required so one person can't bypass policy alone.
Q: What is the difference between a honeyfile and a honeytoken?
A: A honeyfile is a decoy file that alerts when opened. A honeytoken is traceable fake data (credentials, emails, records) that reveals where it was copied or used.
Q: What is a honeynet?
A: A network of honeypots built to look like a real environment to keep attackers busy and observed.
Q: Which part of a router is the control plane: the routing table or packet forwarding?
A: The routing table (configuration). Forwarding packets is the data plane.
Q: How does a certificate authenticate a device?
A: The CA signs a certificate issued to the device. Anyone trusting the CA can verify the signature and confirm the device is genuine.

## 1.3 Explain the importance of change management processes and the impact to security
labs: git-github
- Change management: request, impact analysis, approval (change advisory board), test, schedule a maintenance window, backout plan, document.
- Stakeholders and owners must sign off; the backout plan is how you undo a failed change.
- Technical impacts: allow/deny lists, restarts, downtime, legacy apps, dependencies.
- Update diagrams, policies and version control after every change.
Q: What is a backout plan?
A: The documented steps to roll back a change if it fails.
Q: Who typically approves changes in a formal process?
A: The change advisory board (CAB), with the change owner and stakeholders.
Q: Why schedule changes in a maintenance window?
A: To limit the business impact of downtime and restarts.
Q: What should happen to network diagrams and documentation after a change?
A: They must be updated so documentation matches reality.
Q: Why does version control matter for change management?
A: It tracks what changed, when and by whom, and makes rollback possible.
Q: What must exist before approving a change, in case it fails?
A: A tested backout plan (steps to undo the change).
Q: What does impact analysis look at?
A: What the change could break or expose, what depends on the system, risk added, and expected benefit.
Q: What is a sandbox used for in change management?
A: Testing the change safely before it touches production.
Q: Why do dependencies matter in change management?
A: Changing one system can break others that rely on it, so they must be mapped before the change.
Q: Why must diagrams and policies be updated after a change?
A: So documentation matches reality, which helps incident response, audits and later changes.
Q: What does version control give you?
A: A history of every revision, who made it, and the ability to roll back to a known good version.
Q: What is the difference between an allow list and a deny list?
A: Allow list permits only what is listed (default deny). Deny list blocks only what is listed (default allow).
Q: Why is a legacy application a change-management concern?
A: It may break when the OS or libraries change, or it may not be patchable, which calls for compensating controls.

## 1.4 Explain the importance of using appropriate cryptographic solutions
labs: cryptography-applied, password-cracking-john
- Symmetric (AES, one shared key, fast) vs asymmetric (RSA/ECC, public+private keys, used for key exchange and signatures).
- Hashing (SHA-256) is one-way, for integrity. Salting stops rainbow tables; key stretching (bcrypt, PBKDF2) slows brute force.
- PKI: CA signs certificates; CRL and OCSP check revocation; a CSR requests a cert. Wildcard certs cover *.domain.
- Encryption levels: full-disk, partition, volume, file, database, record. Transport: TLS, VPN (IPsec).
- Hardware: TPM (on the motherboard), HSM (dedicated appliance), secure enclave, key management system.
- Obfuscation: steganography, tokenization, data masking. Blockchain is a distributed, append-only ledger.
Q: What's the main difference between symmetric and asymmetric encryption?
A: Symmetric uses one shared key (fast, bulk data); asymmetric uses a public/private key pair (key exchange, signatures).
Q: What does a salt protect against?
A: Precomputed attacks like rainbow tables, and identical passwords producing identical hashes.
Q: What is key stretching?
A: Running a password through a slow hash many times (bcrypt, PBKDF2) to make brute force expensive.
Q: OCSP vs CRL?
A: Both check certificate revocation; CRL is a downloaded list, OCSP asks the CA about one cert in real time.
Q: TPM vs HSM?
A: TPM is a chip on one device's motherboard; an HSM is a dedicated, often networked, appliance for managing many keys.
Q: What is tokenization?
A: Replacing sensitive data (like a card number) with a non-sensitive token that maps back only via a secure vault.
Q: Which key do you use to verify someone's digital signature?
A: Their public key.
Q: What does a digital signature give you?
A: Integrity, authentication and non-repudiation.
Q: What is the main advantage of symmetric over asymmetric encryption?
A: Speed. It's much faster and suited to bulk data.
Q: What is the main weakness of symmetric encryption?
A: Both sides need the same secret key, so sharing it safely is hard.
Q: How does TLS combine symmetric and asymmetric crypto?
A: Asymmetric crypto exchanges a symmetric session key, then symmetric encryption protects the data.
Q: What does perfect forward secrecy protect against?
A: Stealing a server's long-term private key later and decrypting previously recorded sessions. Ephemeral keys per session make that impossible.
Q: TPM or HSM: which is a chip on a single device's motherboard?
A: TPM. An HSM is a dedicated, tamper-resistant device or service for protecting keys for many systems.
Q: What is steganography?
A: Hiding a message inside another file or medium so its existence isn't obvious.
Q: What does a salt do?
A: A random value added before hashing so identical passwords get different hashes and rainbow tables fail.
Q: What does key stretching do?
A: Repeats hashing many times to make each password guess slower.
Q: Why are MD5 and SHA-1 no longer trusted for security?
A: Practical collision attacks exist (different inputs with the same hash).
Q: What file format holds both a certificate and its private key, protected by a password?
A: PFX/PKCS#12 (P12).
Q: What is OCSP stapling?
A: The server attaches a recent, CA-signed revocation status to the TLS handshake so clients don't have to query the CA.
Q: Which key do you use to verify a digital signature?
A: The signer's public key.
Q: What is a wildcard certificate and what is its risk?
A: One certificate covering all subdomains (*.example.com). If its private key is stolen, every subdomain is exposed.
Q: What is a CSR?
A: Certificate signing request: your public key and identity details sent to a CA to obtain a certificate.
Q: What is key escrow?
A: A trusted third party holds a copy of a key so it can be recovered when the owner can't provide it.
Q: What makes a blockchain tamper-evident?
A: Each block contains the hash of the previous block, so changing an old block changes every later hash.
Q: Which algorithm gives similar strength to RSA with much shorter keys?
A: ECC (elliptic curve cryptography).

## 2.1 Compare and contrast common threat actors and motivations
- Actors: nation-state (APT, huge resources), unskilled attacker (script kiddie), hacktivist, insider threat, organized crime, shadow IT.
- Attributes: internal vs external, resources/funding, sophistication.
- Motivations: data exfiltration, espionage, service disruption, blackmail, financial gain, philosophical/political beliefs, ethical, revenge, disruption/chaos, war.
Q: What does APT stand for and who is usually behind one?
A: Advanced persistent threat, usually a nation-state group with lots of resources that stays hidden for a long time.
Q: An employee installs an unapproved cloud app to get work done. What threat is that?
A: Shadow IT.
Q: What usually motivates organized crime groups?
A: Financial gain (ransomware, fraud, data theft for sale).
Q: What motivates a hacktivist?
A: Philosophical or political beliefs.
Q: Why are insider threats dangerous?
A: They already have legitimate access and knowledge of the systems.
Q: Which threat actor is typically responsible for an APT?
A: A nation state: long-term, well-funded, sophisticated intrusion.
Q: Which attribute of a threat actor is the most likely clue in an exam scenario?
A: Motivation (why they attack), followed by internal/external, resources and sophistication.
Q: An employee passed over for promotion deletes a shared drive. Which actor and motivation?
A: Insider threat, revenge.
Q: A department buys its own cloud service on a credit card, bypassing IT. Which actor?
A: Shadow IT: not malicious but creates unmanaged risk.
Q: A group defaces a company website to protest its policies. Which actor?
A: Hacktivist (philosophical/political motivation).
Q: Which actor is mainly motivated by money and may run customer support for ransomware victims?
A: Organized crime.
Q: What is a script kiddie?
A: An unskilled attacker who runs others' tools without understanding them.
Q: What is the difference between a white hat and gray hat hacker?
A: White hat is authorized and ethical. Gray hat acts without authorization but not maliciously and may disclose findings publicly.
Q: Name two defenses against insider threats.
A: Least privilege and separation of duties (also monitoring, vetting at hire, prompt offboarding).
Q: What was Stuxnet?
A: A worm attributed to nation states that sabotaged nuclear centrifuges, a classic example of a sophisticated nation-state attack.

## 2.2 Explain common threat vectors and attack surfaces
labs: phishing-analysis, recon-nmap-metasploit
- Message-based vectors: email, SMS (smishing), instant messaging. Also image-based and file-based (malicious attachments).
- Voice calls (vishing), removable devices (USB drops), vulnerable software (client-based vs agentless), unsupported systems.
- Unsecure networks: wireless, wired, Bluetooth. Open service ports, default credentials, supply chain (MSPs, vendors, suppliers).
- Human vectors: phishing, vishing, smishing, misinformation/disinformation, impersonation, business email compromise, pretexting, watering hole, brand impersonation, typosquatting.
Q: What is a watering hole attack?
A: Compromising a website the target group already visits, so they get infected there.
Q: What is typosquatting?
A: Registering domains that are misspellings of real ones (e.g. g00gle.com) to catch mistyped visits.
Q: What is pretexting?
A: Inventing a believable scenario (a pretext) to trick someone into giving up information.
Q: What is business email compromise (BEC)?
A: Using a compromised or spoofed executive/vendor email account to trick staff into payments or data transfers.
Q: Why are default credentials a threat vector?
A: They're published online, so attackers try them first on any new device.
Q: What is smishing?
A: Phishing via SMS text messages.
Q: What is the difference between phishing, spear phishing and whaling?
A: Phishing is broad. Spear phishing targets specific people. Whaling targets senior executives.
Q: What is vishing? Smishing?
A: Vishing is phishing by voice call. Smishing is phishing by SMS text.
Q: How does pharming differ from phishing?
A: Pharming redirects users to a fake site by corrupting DNS or hosts data, even when they type the correct address.
Q: Why is an MSP a supply chain risk?
A: It has remote access to many customers, so compromising one MSP can reach all of them.
Q: Name three message-based threat vectors.
A: Email, SMS, and instant messaging/collaboration chat.
Q: What is the difference between tailgating and shoulder surfing?
A: Tailgating is following someone through a secured door. Shoulder surfing is watching someone's screen or keypad.
Q: What is baiting?
A: Leaving something tempting, such as an infected USB drive, for a victim to pick up and use.
Q: Why are unsupported (end-of-life) systems a threat vector?
A: They no longer get patches, so known vulnerabilities stay open forever.
Q: What is an attack surface?
A: All the places an attacker could try to get in. Reduced by removing unused services, ports, accounts and software.
Q: Which social engineering principle is used when a message says "Act within 1 hour or your account is closed"?
A: Urgency (also fear and authority).

## 2.3 Explain various types of vulnerabilities
labs: binary-exploitation, databases-and-sql, typescript-javascript
- Application: memory injection, buffer overflow, race conditions (TOCTOU), malicious updates.
- Web: SQL injection, cross-site scripting (XSS). OS-based, hardware (firmware, end-of-life, legacy).
- Virtualization: VM escape, resource reuse. Cloud-specific, supply chain (service/hardware/software providers).
- Cryptographic weaknesses, misconfiguration, mobile (sideloading, jailbreaking), zero-day.
Q: What is a TOCTOU vulnerability?
A: Time-of-check to time-of-use race condition: the resource changes between when it's checked and when it's used.
Q: What is a buffer overflow?
A: Writing more data into a buffer than it holds, overwriting adjacent memory (possibly a return address).
Q: What is VM escape?
A: Breaking out of a virtual machine to reach the hypervisor or other VMs.
Q: SQL injection vs XSS: what does each target?
A: SQLi targets the database behind the app; XSS injects script that runs in other users' browsers.
Q: What is a zero-day?
A: A vulnerability the vendor doesn't know about or hasn't patched yet.
Q: What is sideloading?
A: Installing mobile apps from outside the official app store.
Q: What is the best defense against SQL injection?
A: Parameterized queries (prepared statements), with input validation and least-privilege database accounts.
Q: Which type of XSS affects every visitor to a page?
A: Stored (persistent) XSS, because the script is saved on the server.
Q: What is TOCTOU?
A: Time-of-check to time-of-use: a race condition where the state changes between checking something and using it.
Q: Name two OS-level protections that make buffer overflows harder to exploit.
A: ASLR (randomized memory layout) and DEP/NX (data pages can't execute). Stack canaries also help.
Q: What is a VM escape?
A: Code in a guest VM breaking out to the hypervisor or other VMs.
Q: What is VM sprawl?
A: Unmanaged, forgotten VMs that aren't patched or monitored.
Q: What is a zero-day vulnerability?
A: A flaw unknown to the vendor (no patch yet) that attackers may already exploit.
Q: What is jailbreaking/rooting?
A: Removing OS restrictions on a phone, which also removes built-in security protections.
Q: Which mitigation checks a software update is genuine?
A: Verify its digital signature or hash from a trusted source.
Q: In the shared responsibility model, who secures a customer's data in the cloud?
A: The customer. The provider secures the underlying cloud infrastructure.
Q: Name three misconfiguration vulnerabilities.
A: Default credentials, open/unneeded ports or services, and overly permissive file or bucket access.
Q: What makes firmware vulnerabilities hard to deal with?
A: Firmware is rarely patched and often not covered by normal OS patching or scanning tools.
Q: Which vulnerability lets a script run in another user's browser in the context of a trusted site?
A: Cross-site scripting (XSS).
Q: Which vulnerability is memory injection?
A: Placing malicious code into a running process's memory so it runs under that process's identity.

## 2.4 Given a scenario, analyze indicators of malicious activity
labs: log-analysis, malware-analysis, network-traffic-analysis, password-cracking-john
- Malware: ransomware, trojan, worm (self-spreading), spyware, bloatware, virus, keylogger, logic bomb, rootkit.
- Physical: brute force, RFID cloning. Network: DDoS (amplified, reflected), DNS attacks, wireless, on-path, credential replay, malicious code.
- Application: injection, buffer overflow, replay, privilege escalation, forgery (CSRF/SSRF), directory traversal.
- Crypto: downgrade, collision, birthday. Password: spraying (one password, many accounts) vs brute force.
- Indicators: account lockout, concurrent session usage, blocked content, impossible travel, resource consumption/inaccessibility, out-of-cycle logging, missing logs, published/documented.
Q: Password spraying vs brute force?
A: Spraying tries a few common passwords across many accounts (avoids lockouts); brute force tries many passwords on one account.
Q: What is "impossible travel"?
A: Logins from two locations too far apart for the time between them, a sign of stolen credentials.
Q: What makes a worm different from a virus?
A: A worm spreads by itself over the network; a virus needs a host file and user action.
Q: What is a logic bomb?
A: Malicious code that triggers when a condition is met (a date, an account being deleted).
Q: What does `../../etc/passwd` in a URL indicate?
A: A directory (path) traversal attempt.
Q: What is a downgrade attack?
A: Forcing a connection to use an older, weaker protocol or cipher (e.g. SSL instead of TLS).
Q: What is a reflected/amplified DDoS?
A: Spoofing the victim's IP in small requests to servers (DNS, NTP) that send much larger replies to the victim.
Q: Missing logs or gaps in logging are an indicator of what?
A: An attacker covering their tracks.
Q: What is the difference between a virus and a worm?
A: A virus needs a host file and user action to spread. A worm self-replicates across networks without user action.
Q: What makes a rootkit dangerous?
A: It hides deep in the OS or firmware, hiding itself and other malware while giving persistent privileged access.
Q: What is fileless malware and how is it caught?
A: Malware that runs in memory using built-in tools like PowerShell. Caught by behavior-based detection (EDR) rather than file signatures.
Q: What is password spraying?
A: Trying a few common passwords against many accounts to avoid lockouts.
Q: What defeats a rainbow table attack?
A: Salting the passwords before hashing.
Q: What is credential stuffing?
A: Reusing username/password pairs from breaches against other sites.
Q: What is an evil twin?
A: A rogue access point that copies a legitimate network's SSID to trick users into connecting.
Q: What does a deauthentication attack do?
A: Sends forged management frames to disconnect Wi-Fi clients. 802.11w (protected management frames) mitigates it.
Q: What is a replay attack and one defense?
A: Resending captured valid data to gain access. Defense: nonces, timestamps, one-time session tokens.
Q: What is an on-path attack?
A: An attacker positioned between two parties who can read or alter traffic (formerly man-in-the-middle).
Q: What is DNS cache poisoning?
A: Inserting false DNS records into a resolver's cache so users go to the attacker's address. DNSSEC helps prevent it.
Q: What is impossible travel?
A: Logins from locations too far apart for the time between them. A common indicator of compromise.
Q: Why alert on missing logs?
A: Attackers delete logs to hide activity, so gaps can indicate compromise.
Q: What is the difference between CSRF and XSS?
A: XSS runs attacker script in a victim's browser on a trusted site. CSRF makes a logged-in browser send an unintended request.
Q: What is SSRF?
A: Server-side request forgery: tricking the server into making requests to internal systems or cloud metadata.
Q: What is a birthday attack?
A: A collision attack that exploits birthday-paradox math, needing far fewer tries than brute force.
Q: What is amplification in a DDoS?
A: A small request triggers a much larger response sent to the victim (for example DNS or NTP).
Q: What is RFID cloning?
A: Copying the data from an access card or fob to make a working duplicate.
Q: What is cryptojacking?
A: Using a victim's computing power to mine cryptocurrency, shown by high CPU use and slowness.
Q: What is directory traversal?
A: Using ../ sequences to read files outside the intended folder.
Q: How does an attacker use an account lockout to get a password reset?
A: Locks the account deliberately, then calls the help desk impersonating the user to get a reset.

## 2.5 Explain the purpose of mitigation techniques used to secure the enterprise
labs: system-hardening-windows-linux, firewalls-pfsense, group-policy
- Segmentation, access control (ACLs, permissions), application allow lists, isolation, patching, encryption, monitoring.
- Least privilege; configuration enforcement; decommissioning.
- Hardening: install endpoint protection, host-based firewall, HIPS, disable ports/protocols, change default passwords, remove unnecessary software.
Q: What is an application allow list?
A: Only approved applications can run; everything else is blocked.
Q: What does least privilege mean?
A: Users and services get only the access they need for their job, nothing more.
Q: What is HIPS?
A: Host-based intrusion prevention system: watches a single host and blocks malicious activity.
Q: Why does network segmentation reduce risk?
A: It limits lateral movement and blast radius when one segment is compromised.
Q: Name three quick hardening steps for a new server.
A: Change default passwords, disable unused ports/services, remove unneeded software (also patch and enable a host firewall).
Q: What does segmentation limit?
A: Lateral movement and the spread of an infection or intruder between network zones.
Q: Application allow list or deny list: which is stronger?
A: Allow list. Only approved programs run, so unknown malware is blocked.
Q: A legacy system can't be patched. Name two mitigations.
A: Isolate it (VLAN/segmentation) and restrict access with ACLs (compensating controls).
Q: What is hardening?
A: Reducing the attack surface by removing or disabling everything not needed and locking down the rest.
Q: Name three hardening steps for a new server.
A: Change default credentials, disable unused ports/services, patch, apply a secure baseline, enable a host firewall.
Q: Why disable unused physical switch ports?
A: Prevents someone plugging in an unauthorized device.
Q: What does a host-based firewall do?
A: Filters traffic to and from a single host, allowing only needed ports and apps.
Q: What is configuration enforcement?
A: Automatically checking and correcting systems so they stay on their secure baseline.
Q: What is least privilege?
A: Giving users and systems only the access they need, only for as long as they need it.
Q: Why decommission unused systems and accounts?
A: They are unmanaged attack surface that rarely gets patched or monitored.

## 3.1 Compare and contrast security implications of different architecture models
labs: virtualization-proxmox, networking-tcp-ip-dhcp-nat-subnets
- Cloud: responsibility matrix (shared responsibility), hybrid, third-party vendors. IaC, serverless, microservices.
- Network infrastructure: physical isolation (air-gapped), logical segmentation, SDN.
- On-premises, centralized vs decentralized, containerization, virtualization, IoT, ICS/SCADA, RTOS, embedded systems, high availability.
- Considerations: availability, resilience, cost, responsiveness, scalability, ease of deployment, risk transference, ease of recovery, patch availability, inability to patch, power, compute.
Q: What is the shared responsibility model?
A: The cloud provider secures the cloud itself; the customer secures what they put in it. The split depends on IaaS/PaaS/SaaS.
Q: What is an air-gapped network?
A: One physically isolated with no connection to other networks.
Q: What is SDN?
A: Software-defined networking: the control plane is separated from the data plane and managed centrally by software.
Q: What is Infrastructure as Code (IaC)?
A: Defining infrastructure in code files, so it's repeatable, versioned and reviewable.
Q: Why are ICS/SCADA systems hard to secure?
A: They run critical physical processes, are often legacy, can't be patched or rebooted easily, and need availability above all.
Q: Containers vs VMs?
A: Containers share the host OS kernel (light, fast); VMs each run a full OS on a hypervisor (stronger isolation).
Q: In IaaS, who is responsible for patching the guest operating system?
A: The customer. The provider handles hardware, hypervisor and facility.
Q: In SaaS, what does the customer still always secure?
A: Their data, user access and configuration.
Q: What is an air gap?
A: A system physically isolated with no network connection to other networks.
Q: What is infrastructure as code (IaC)?
A: Defining infrastructure in versioned code so deployments are repeatable and reviewable.
Q: How do containers differ from VMs in isolation?
A: Containers share the host OS kernel and are less isolated; each VM has its own kernel and is more isolated.
Q: What is the main security concern with ICS/SCADA and IoT devices?
A: They are hard or impossible to patch and often have weak defaults, so isolate them from other networks.
Q: Can you transfer accountability for your data to a cloud provider?
A: No. You can transfer some risk, but you stay accountable for your data.
Q: What is serverless computing?
A: Running code without managing servers. The provider handles scaling and patching, while you secure the code, permissions and data.
Q: What is a microservices architecture?
A: An app built from small independent services that communicate via APIs.
Q: What does "inability to patch" call for?
A: Compensating controls such as isolation and access restrictions.

## 3.2 Given a scenario, apply security principles to secure enterprise infrastructure
labs: firewalls-pfsense, ids-snort, vpn-tailscale, networking-tcp-ip-dhcp-nat-subnets
- Infrastructure: device placement, security zones, attack surface, connectivity, failure modes (fail-open vs fail-closed).
- Device attributes: active vs passive, inline vs tap/monitor.
- Devices: jump server, proxy server, IPS/IDS, load balancer, sensors, port security (802.1X, EAP), firewalls (WAF, UTM, NGFW, layer 4/7).
- Secure communication: VPN, remote access, tunneling (TLS, IPsec), SD-WAN, SASE.
Q: Fail-open vs fail-closed?
A: Fail-open lets traffic through when the device fails (keeps availability); fail-closed blocks all traffic (keeps security).
Q: What is a jump server?
A: A hardened host admins connect to first, then from it into a protected network segment.
Q: What does 802.1X do?
A: Port-based network access control: a device must authenticate (via EAP and usually RADIUS) before the switch port allows traffic.
Q: IDS vs IPS placement?
A: An IDS is usually passive on a tap/span port; an IPS sits inline so it can block.
Q: What is a WAF?
A: Web application firewall: inspects HTTP traffic for attacks like SQLi and XSS.
Q: What is SASE?
A: Secure access service edge: cloud-delivered networking and security (SD-WAN plus firewall, CASB, zero-trust access) close to users.
Q: Layer 4 vs layer 7 firewall?
A: Layer 4 filters by IP/port/protocol; layer 7 (NGFW) understands applications and content.
Q: What does fail-closed mean?
A: When the device fails it blocks traffic, favoring security over availability.
Q: What does fail-open mean?
A: When the device fails it allows traffic, favoring availability over security.
Q: What is the difference between an inline and a tap/monitor deployment?
A: Inline traffic passes through the device so it can block; tap/monitor sees a copy and can only alert.
Q: Difference between IDS and IPS?
A: IDS detects and alerts (passive). IPS detects and blocks (inline).
Q: Forward proxy vs reverse proxy?
A: Forward proxy sits in front of clients for outbound web access. Reverse proxy sits in front of servers.
Q: What are the three roles in 802.1X?
A: Supplicant (client), authenticator (switch/AP), authentication server (RADIUS).
Q: Which EAP method uses certificates on both sides?
A: EAP-TLS.
Q: What does a stateful firewall track?
A: Connection state, so return traffic of an allowed session is automatically permitted.
Q: What is an implicit deny?
A: The final rule that blocks anything not explicitly allowed.
Q: What does a WAF protect?
A: Web applications at layer 7 against attacks like SQL injection and XSS.
Q: AH or ESP: which provides encryption?
A: ESP (AH provides integrity and authentication only).
Q: IPsec tunnel mode vs transport mode?
A: Tunnel mode encapsulates the whole original packet (gateway to gateway). Transport mode protects only the payload (host to host).
Q: Split tunnel vs full tunnel VPN?
A: Split tunnel sends only corporate traffic through the VPN. Full tunnel sends all traffic.
Q: What does DHCP snooping prevent?
A: Rogue DHCP servers handing out bad addresses.
Q: What does dynamic ARP inspection prevent?
A: ARP spoofing/poisoning.
Q: Signature-based vs anomaly-based detection?
A: Signature matches known patterns (misses new attacks). Anomaly flags deviations from normal (catches new ones, more false positives).

## 3.3 Compare and contrast concepts and strategies to protect data

- Classifications: sensitive, confidential, public, restricted, private, critical.

- Methods: geographic restrictions, encryption, hashing, masking, tokenization, obfuscation, segmentation, permission restrictions.
Q: What are the three states of data?
A: At rest (stored), in transit (moving over a network), in use (in memory being processed).
Q: What is data sovereignty?
A: Data is subject to the laws of the country where it's stored.
Q: Masking vs tokenization?
A: Masking hides part of the data (****1234); tokenization replaces it entirely with a token mapped in a vault.
Q: How do you protect data in transit?
A: Encrypt the connection (TLS, IPsec/VPN).
Q: What is geofencing/geographic restriction?
A: Limiting access to data or systems based on the user's location.
Q: Name the three states of data.
A: At rest, in transit (in motion), and in use.
Q: Which state of data is hardest to protect and why?
A: In use, because it must be decrypted to be processed.
Q: Who decides how data is classified?
A: The data owner.
Q: What does a data custodian do?
A: Implements the technical protection of data on behalf of the owner.
Q: Data controller vs data processor?
A: Controller decides why and how personal data is processed. Processor processes it on the controller's behalf.
Q: What is data masking?
A: Hiding part of a value (for example showing only the last 4 digits) while preserving its format.
Q: What is tokenization?
A: Replacing sensitive data with a meaningless token; the real data is kept in a secure vault.
Q: Name three regulated data types.
A: Health data (HIPAA), payment card data (PCI DSS), personal data of EU residents (GDPR).
Q: What does DLP do?
A: Detects and blocks sensitive data leaving the organization (email, USB, cloud uploads).
Q: What is a trade secret?
A: Information with business value that is protected by keeping it secret.
Q: What controls protect data at rest?
A: Encryption (disk, file, database), access controls and secure storage.

## 3.4 Explain the importance of resilience and recovery in security architecture
labs: virtualization-proxmox
- High availability: load balancing vs clustering. Site considerations: hot (ready now), warm (some gear), cold (empty space), geographic dispersion.
- Platform diversity, multi-cloud. Continuity of operations, capacity planning (people, technology, infrastructure).
- Testing: tabletop exercises, failover, simulation, parallel processing.
- Backups: onsite/offsite, frequency, encryption, snapshots, recovery, replication, journaling.
- Power: generators, UPS.
Q: Hot vs warm vs cold site?
A: Hot is fully equipped and ready almost immediately; warm has some equipment and needs setup; cold is space and power only.
Q: What's a tabletop exercise?
A: A discussion-based walkthrough of a scenario with the team, no systems touched.
Q: What does a UPS provide?
A: Short-term battery power to ride out outages or allow a clean shutdown until a generator starts.
Q: Snapshot vs backup?
A: A snapshot captures a VM/volume state at a point in time on the same storage; a backup is a separate copy that survives losing the original.
Q: Load balancing vs clustering?
A: Load balancing spreads requests across independent servers; clustering makes several servers act as one system with failover.
Q: Hot, warm and cold site: order by recovery speed.
A: Hot is fastest, then warm, then cold.
Q: What does RPO define?
A: The maximum acceptable data loss, measured in time (it drives backup frequency).
Q: What does RTO define?
A: The maximum acceptable time to restore a service.
Q: Which backup type is fastest to restore?
A: Full backup.
Q: What do you need to restore from incremental backups?
A: The last full backup plus every incremental since.
Q: What do you need to restore from a differential backup?
A: The last full backup plus the latest differential.
Q: What is the 3-2-1 backup rule?
A: 3 copies of data, on 2 different media types, with 1 copy offsite.
Q: Why are snapshots not a full backup substitute?
A: They usually sit on the same storage, so a storage failure or ransomware takes them too.
Q: What is a tabletop exercise?
A: A discussion-based walkthrough of an incident scenario to test plans without touching systems.
Q: What is a failover test?
A: Actually switching to the backup system or site to confirm it works.
Q: UPS vs generator?
A: A UPS gives short battery bridging and clean power; a generator provides longer-term power but takes time to start.
Q: What is platform diversity?
A: Using different operating systems, vendors or technologies so one flaw doesn't take down everything.
Q: Why are immutable or offline backups important?
A: Ransomware can't encrypt or delete them.
Q: What does MTBF measure?
A: Average time a repairable system operates between failures.

## 4.2 Explain the security implications of proper hardware, software, and data asset management

A: Acquisition/procurement, assignment/accounting, monitoring/tracking, and disposal/decommissioning.

A: So someone is accountable and so protection matches the asset's value.
Q: What is enumeration?
A: Discovering the devices and services present on a network, which also reveals unknown or shadow IT.

A: Degaussing.

A: Destroying the encryption key so encrypted data becomes unreadable.
Q: Which standard guides media sanitization?
A: NIST SP 800-88.
Q: What is a legal hold?
A: A requirement to preserve relevant data because of actual or likely litigation, so it must not be disposed.

A: To avoid counterfeit or tampered hardware and software (supply chain risk).

## 4.3 Explain various activities associated with vulnerability management

- Response: patching, insurance, segmentation, compensating controls, exceptions and exemptions.
- Validation: rescanning, audit, verification. Reporting.
Q: What is CVSS?
A: Common Vulnerability Scoring System: a 0-10 severity score for vulnerabilities.
Q: What is a CVE?
A: Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures: a unique public ID for a known vulnerability (e.g. CVE-2021-44228).
Q: What is a false positive in a vuln scan?
A: The scanner reports a vulnerability that isn't actually there.
Q: Credentialed vs non-credentialed scan?
A: Credentialed scans log in and see much more (missing patches, configs); non-credentialed show what an outsider sees.
Q: What should you do after patching a vulnerability?
A: Validate it: rescan to confirm the fix.
Q: What is an ISAC?
A: Information Sharing and Analysis Center: an industry group that shares threat intel among members.
Q: Vulnerability scan vs penetration test?
A: A scan finds weaknesses; a penetration test actually exploits them to prove impact.
Q: What is a false negative in a vulnerability scan?
A: A real vulnerability the scanner failed to report.
Q: What does CVSS provide?
A: A 0-10 severity score for vulnerabilities (Critical 9.0-10.0).
Q: CVE vs CVSS?
A: CVE is the ID of a specific vulnerability. CVSS rates its severity.
Q: What do rules of engagement define?
A: The scope, timing and limits of a penetration test.
Q: Known vs unknown environment test?
A: Known (white box) gives the tester full information. Unknown (black box) starts with none.
Q: Passive vs active reconnaissance?
A: Passive gathers public info without touching the target. Active probes the target directly.
Q: What are STIX and TAXII?
A: STIX is a standard format for threat intelligence; TAXII is the transport to exchange it.
Q: What is a bug bounty?
A: A program that rewards researchers for responsibly reporting vulnerabilities.
Q: When is a vulnerability exception or exemption used?
A: When remediation isn't possible or practical; it is documented, approved and reviewed (risk acceptance).
Q: What must happen after remediation?
A: Validate: rescan/verify the fix and confirm the system still works.
Q: What does insurance do for a vulnerability?
A: Transfers some financial risk; it doesn't fix the flaw.

## 4.6 Given a scenario, implement and maintain identity and access management
labs: active-directory, group-policy, windows-admin-powershell

Q: SAML vs OAuth?
A: SAML is XML-based authentication/SSO (often enterprise web apps); OAuth is authorization delegation (grant an app access to your data), often with OpenID Connect for login.
Q: RBAC vs ABAC?
A: RBAC grants access by job role; ABAC evaluates attributes (user, resource, environment) for fine-grained decisions.
Q: What is discretionary access control?
A: The owner of a resource decides who can access it (like NTFS permissions).
Q: What is just-in-time access?
A: Privileged rights granted only for a short window when needed, then removed automatically.
Q: What is federation?
A: Trusting identities from another organization or provider so users log in with their home credentials.
Q: Which MFA factor is a fingerprint?
A: Something you are (inherence).
Q: Why should de-provisioning be fast?
A: Leftover accounts of departed users are easy targets for attackers and insiders.
Q: What is the difference between OAuth and OpenID Connect?
A: OAuth is for authorization (delegated access). OpenID Connect adds authentication (identity) on top of it.
Q: What is SAML used for?
A: XML-based federation and single sign-on between an identity provider and service providers.
Q: Which access control model uses labels and clearance levels?
A: Mandatory access control (MAC).
Q: Which model lets the resource owner decide access?
A: Discretionary access control (DAC).
Q: Which access control model assigns permissions by job function?
A: Role-based access control (RBAC).
Q: Which model uses attributes such as department, location and time?
A: Attribute-based access control (ABAC).
Q: What are the authentication factor categories?
A: Something you know, have, are; somewhere you are; something you do.
Q: Are a password and a PIN multifactor?
A: No. Both are something you know, so it's a single factor.
Q: HOTP vs TOTP?
A: HOTP is counter-based. TOTP is time-based (codes expire, typically after 30 seconds).
Q: What does FAR measure? FRR?
A: FAR: how often an impostor is accepted. FRR: how often a legitimate user is rejected.
Q: What is crossover error rate (CER)?
A: The point where FAR equals FRR; lower means a more accurate biometric system.
Q: Why does Kerberos need synchronized clocks?
A: Tickets include timestamps and are rejected if the time difference is too large.
Q: What is just-in-time (JIT) permission?
A: Granting elevated access only when needed and for a limited time.
Q: What is privileged access management (PAM)?
A: Vaulting, controlling, recording and rotating admin credentials.
Q: What does TACACS+ do better than RADIUS?
A: It encrypts the whole payload and separates authentication, authorization and accounting.
Q: What does current NIST password guidance favor?
A: Length (passphrases), checking against breached/common lists, no forced periodic changes without evidence of compromise.
Q: What is permission creep?
A: Accumulating access over time as roles change, because old permissions are never removed.

## 4.8 Explain appropriate incident response activities

Q: What is a legal hold?
A: A requirement to preserve data that may be relevant to litigation, overriding normal deletion.

A: A post-incident review of what happened and what to improve, feeding back into preparation.

A: Preparation, detection and analysis, containment, eradication, recovery, lessons learned.
Q: Which comes first: containment or eradication?
A: Containment.

Q: What happens in the lessons learned phase?
A: A post-incident review of the timeline, what worked and what didn't, with changes to plans, monitoring and training.
Q: Why use out-of-band communication during an incident?
A: Normal channels such as email may be compromised.

Q: Which log shows domains a host looked up?
A: DNS log.
Q: What is metadata?
A: Data about data, such as email headers, file properties and timestamps.
Q: Packet capture vs NetFlow?
A: Packet capture holds the content; NetFlow holds only summary information about conversations.
Q: Why must device clocks be synchronized?
A: So events from different logs can be correlated accurately (NTP).
Q: Which source shows process creation and file changes on a host?
A: Endpoint logs (OS security logs, EDR).
Q: Which log would show blocked inbound connection attempts?
A: Firewall log.
Q: Why send logs to a central, protected store?
A: So attackers who compromise a host can't alter or delete the evidence.
Q: What does an IPS log show?
A: Signature matches and attacks that were detected or blocked.
Q: What does a vulnerability scan output provide in an investigation?
A: The weaknesses present at the time of the scan, which hints at how a system could have been compromised.

## 5.1 Summarize elements of effective security governance
- Guidelines, policies (AUP, information security, business continuity, disaster recovery, incident response, SDLC, change management).
- Standards (password, access control, physical, encryption) and procedures (change management, onboarding/offboarding, playbooks).
- External considerations: regulatory, legal, industry, local/regional/national/global.
- Monitoring and revision; governance structures (boards, committees, government entities, centralized/decentralized).
- Roles: owners, controllers, processors, custodians/stewards.
Q: Policy vs standard vs procedure?
A: Policy states what and why; a standard sets specific mandatory requirements; a procedure gives step-by-step how.
Q: What is an AUP?
A: Acceptable use policy: what users may and may not do with company systems.
Q: Data controller vs data processor?
A: The controller decides why and how personal data is processed; the processor handles it on the controller's behalf.
Q: What does a data custodian do?
A: Implements and maintains the controls on the data day to day (backups, permissions), as directed by the owner.
Q: What is a playbook?
A: A documented, repeatable set of steps for a specific situation (e.g. responding to phishing).
Q: Order policy, standard and procedure from highest-level to most detailed.
A: Policy, then standard, then procedure.
Q: Is a guideline mandatory?
A: No. It is recommended advice.
Q: Who is accountable for a dataset and decides its classification?
A: The data owner.
Q: What does a data steward do?
A: Manages data quality, labels and metadata.
Q: What does an acceptable use policy cover?
A: What users may and may not do with company systems and data.
Q: What is the difference between a data controller and a data processor?
A: Controller decides why and how personal data is processed. Processor handles it on the controller's behalf.
Q: What does a DPO do?
A: Advises on and monitors privacy law compliance.
Q: Name four external considerations that shape governance.
A: Regulatory, legal, industry standards, and local/national/global requirements.
Q: Why review policies regularly?
A: Threats, technology, laws and the business change, and incidents or audits reveal gaps.
Q: What is an onboarding/offboarding procedure for?
A: Consistently provisioning access when people join and removing it when they leave.

## 5.2 Explain elements of the risk management process
- Risk identification, assessment (ad hoc, recurring, one-time, continuous), analysis: qualitative vs quantitative.
- SLE = asset value x exposure factor. ALE = SLE x ARO.
- Risk register: key risk indicators, risk owners, thresholds. Risk tolerance, appetite (expansionary, conservative, neutral).
- Strategies: transfer, accept (exemption, exception), avoid, mitigate.
- Business impact analysis: RTO, RPO, MTTR, MTBF.
Q: How do you calculate ALE?
A: ALE = SLE x ARO (single loss expectancy times annualized rate of occurrence).
Q: A $100,000 server, 40% damaged per incident, twice a year. ALE?
A: SLE = 100,000 x 0.4 = 40,000; ALE = 40,000 x 2 = $80,000.
Q: RTO vs RPO?
A: RTO is how long until the system must be back; RPO is how much data loss (time since last backup) is acceptable.
Q: Buying cyber insurance is which risk strategy?
A: Transfer.
Q: What's a risk register?
A: A list of identified risks with their owner, likelihood, impact, and response.
Q: MTBF vs MTTR?
A: Mean time between failures (reliability) vs mean time to repair (how fast you fix it).
Q: What is the formula for SLE?
A: SLE = asset value x exposure factor.
Q: What is the formula for ALE?
A: ALE = SLE x ARO.
Q: A $200,000 asset loses 25% in one event. What is the SLE?
A: $50,000.
Q: The SLE is $50,000 and the event happens twice a year. What is the ALE?
A: $100,000.
Q: Name the four risk responses.
A: Avoid, mitigate, transfer, accept.
Q: Buying cyber insurance is which risk response?
A: Transfer.
Q: What is residual risk?
A: Risk remaining after controls are applied.
Q: What is risk appetite vs risk tolerance?
A: Appetite is the amount of risk an organization is willing to pursue. Tolerance is the acceptable variation around it.
Q: Qualitative vs quantitative risk analysis?
A: Qualitative uses descriptive ratings (high/medium/low). Quantitative uses numbers and money.
Q: What is a risk register?
A: A list of identified risks with owners, ratings, controls and status.
Q: What does a BIA produce?
A: Critical functions, dependencies and recovery metrics such as RTO and RPO.
Q: When is a control cost-effective?
A: When the control costs less than the loss it prevents (compare to ALE).

## 5.3 Explain the processes associated with third-party risk assessment and management
- Vendor assessment: penetration testing, right-to-audit clause, evidence of internal audits, independent assessments, supply chain analysis.
- Vendor selection: due diligence, conflict of interest. Monitoring and questionnaires, rules of engagement.
- Agreements: SLA, MOA, MOU, MSA, WO/SOW, NDA, BPA.
Q: What is an SLA?
A: Service level agreement: the measurable service a vendor guarantees (uptime, response time).
Q: MOU vs MOA?
A: An MOU is a non-binding statement of intent; an MOA is more formal and spells out responsibilities.
Q: What is an MSA?
A: Master service agreement: the overall terms for future work, with details in SOWs/work orders.
Q: What is a right-to-audit clause?
A: Contract language letting you audit the vendor's security.
Q: What does an NDA do?
A: Legally binds a party to keep shared information confidential.
Q: SLA vs SOW?
A: An SLA defines service levels (uptime, response times). A SOW defines a project's scope and deliverables.
Q: What is an NDA for?
A: Legally protecting confidential information from disclosure.
Q: What is due diligence in vendor selection?
A: Investigating a vendor's security, finances and reputation before signing.
Q: What is a BPA?
A: Business partners agreement: defines how partners operate together, including shared responsibilities and exit terms.
Q: What is a fourth party?
A: A vendor's own supplier or subcontractor.
Q: What belongs in vendor offboarding?
A: Revoking access and confirming return or destruction of data.
Q: What is a SOC 2 report?
A: An independent auditor's report on a service provider's controls (security, availability, confidentiality, and more).

## 5.4 Summarize elements of effective security compliance
- Compliance reporting (internal, external). Consequences of non-compliance: fines, sanctions, reputational damage, loss of license, contractual impacts.
- Compliance monitoring: due diligence/care, attestation and acknowledgement, internal/external, automation.
- Privacy: legal implications, data subject, controller vs processor, ownership, data inventory and retention, right to be forgotten.
Q: What is the right to be forgotten?
A: A data subject's right (e.g. under GDPR) to have their personal data erased.
Q: Due diligence vs due care?
A: Due diligence is investigating and understanding the risks; due care is acting reasonably to protect against them.
Q: Name two consequences of non-compliance.
A: Fines and reputational damage (also sanctions, loss of license, contract impacts).
Q: What is a data subject?
A: The person the personal data is about.
Q: Which law protects US health information?
A: HIPAA.
Q: Which standard governs card payment data?
A: PCI DSS (an industry standard, not a law).
Q: Which regulation protects EU residents' personal data?
A: GDPR.
Q: Name consequences of non-compliance.
A: Fines, sanctions, loss of license, reputational damage, lost contracts.
Q: What is attestation?
A: A formal confirmation that requirements are met.
Q: What is a privacy impact assessment?
A: An evaluation of the privacy risks of a project or system handling personal data.
Q: What is the GDPR breach notification window?
A: 72 hours to notify the regulator after becoming aware.
Q: What is data minimization?
A: Collecting and keeping only the personal data you actually need.
Q: What is SOX concerned with?
A: Integrity of financial reporting for US public companies.

## 5.5 Explain types and purposes of audits and assessments
labs: recon-nmap-metasploit
- Attestation; internal (compliance, audit committee, self-assessments) and external (regulatory, examinations, assessment, independent third-party audit).
- Penetration testing: physical, offensive, defensive, integrated; known, partially known, unknown environment; reconnaissance (passive, active).
Q: Known vs unknown environment pen test?
A: Known (white box): testers get full information; unknown (black box): no prior information, like an outside attacker.
Q: Passive vs active reconnaissance?
A: Passive gathers info without touching the target (OSINT); active interacts with it (port scans).
Q: What's the purpose of rules of engagement in a pen test?
A: Defining scope, timing, allowed techniques and contacts, so testing is authorized and safe.
Q: What is attestation?
A: A formal statement (often by an auditor) that controls or reports are accurate.
Q: Internal vs external audit?
A: Internal is done by your own staff for readiness and improvement. External is independent and gives credible assurance to others.
Q: Known, partially known and unknown environment tests?
A: White box (full information), gray box (some), black box (none).
Q: Red, blue, purple and white teams?
A: Red attacks, blue defends, purple combines both to improve defenses, white oversees and referees.
Q: What are rules of engagement?
A: The agreed scope, timing, methods and limits for a penetration test.
Q: Audit vs penetration test?
A: An audit compares controls to a standard. A penetration test tries to break in.
Q: What is an attestation in an audit context?
A: A formal statement or report that controls meet requirements.
Q: Why might a regulator examine you?
A: To check compliance with laws and industry regulations.

## 5.6 Given a scenario, implement security awareness practices
labs: phishing-analysis
- Phishing campaigns: recognizing attempts, responding to reported suspicious messages.
- Anomalous behavior recognition: risky, unexpected, unintentional.
- User guidance and training: policy/handbooks, situational awareness, insider threat, password management, removable media and cables, social engineering, operational security, hybrid/remote work.
- Reporting and monitoring: initial, recurring. Development and execution.
Q: Why run internal phishing simulations?
A: To train users to recognize and report phishing, and to measure improvement over time.
Q: What should a user do with a suspicious email?
A: Report it using the official process (report button/security team) without clicking links or attachments.
Q: Why warn users about unknown USB cables and drives?
A: They can carry malware or act as malicious keyboards (e.g. a USB Rubber Ducky or O.MG cable).
Q: What is operational security (OPSEC) for employees?
A: Not leaking sensitive details through social media, conversation or carelessness.
Q: What is the purpose of phishing simulations?
A: To measure and train users to recognize phishing, not to punish them.
Q: Name two phishing metrics to track.
A: Click rate and report rate.
Q: Why make reporting easy and blame-free?
A: Early reports give the security team warning and encourage more reporting.
Q: What is role-based training?
A: Training tailored to job risks, such as admins, executives, developers or HR.
Q: Name three things to teach about situational awareness.
A: Watch for shoulder surfers, unattended devices and tailgaters; be careful on public Wi-Fi.
Q: How often should awareness training happen?
A: At onboarding and on a recurring schedule, with reminders in between.
Q: Which control category does an awareness program belong to?
A: Operational.
Q: How do you measure training effectiveness?
A: Completion rates, quiz scores, simulation results and incident trends.
