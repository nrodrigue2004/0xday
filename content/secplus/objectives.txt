## 1.1 Compare and contrast various types of security controls
labs: system-hardening-windows-linux, firewalls-pfsense
- Four categories: technical (firewalls, encryption), managerial (policies, risk assessments), operational (guards, awareness training: done by people), physical (locks, fences, badges).
- Control types describe what a control does: preventive, deterrent, detective, corrective, compensating, directive.
- One control can be several types: a camera is physical, and deters and detects.
- Compensating controls are the substitute when the ideal control isn't possible (isolate an unpatchable server).
- Directive controls tell people what to do (policy, signage) but don't enforce anything.
Q: Which control category covers a security policy or a risk assessment?
A: Managerial (administrative) controls.
Q: A guard checking badges at the door is which control category?
A: Operational (it's carried out by people), and it's also physical in effect.
Q: What control type is an IDS that alerts on suspicious traffic?
A: Detective: it identifies an event but doesn't stop it.
Q: A legacy server can't be patched, so you put it on an isolated VLAN. What type of control is the VLAN?
A: Compensating: an alternative that reduces risk when the primary control isn't possible.
Q: Restoring a system from backup after ransomware is which control type?
A: Corrective.
Q: A "Authorized personnel only" sign is which control type?
A: Directive (it may also deter).
Q: Firewall rules and encryption are which control category?
A: Technical.

## 1.2 Summarize fundamental security concepts
labs: cryptography-applied, active-directory
- CIA triad: confidentiality, integrity, availability. Non-repudiation means you can't deny an action you took (digital signatures).
- AAA: authentication (prove who you are), authorization (what you can do), accounting (log what you did).
- Zero trust: never trust by network location. Control plane (policy engine, policy administrator) decides; data plane (policy enforcement point) enforces.
- Gap analysis compares where you are to where you want to be (a framework or baseline).
- Physical security: bollards, access control vestibules (mantraps), fencing, lighting, sensors (infrared, pressure, microwave, ultrasonic).
- Deception tech: honeypots, honeynets, honeyfiles, honeytokens.
Q: What does non-repudiation provide?
A: Proof that a specific party performed an action, so they can't deny it (usually via digital signatures).
Q: In zero trust, which component actually allows or blocks the traffic?
A: The policy enforcement point (data plane).
Q: In zero trust, what does the policy engine do?
A: Decides whether to grant access, based on policy and signals; the policy administrator then tells the enforcement point.
Q: What is a honeytoken?
A: Fake data (credentials, API key, record) that alerts you when someone uses it.
Q: What is an access control vestibule?
A: Two-door entry where only one door opens at a time, preventing tailgating (also called a mantrap).
Q: What does accounting mean in AAA?
A: Recording what users did: logs of logins, resources used, data transferred.
Q: What is a gap analysis?
A: A comparison of the current security posture against a target baseline or framework to find what's missing.

## 1.3 Explain the importance of change management processes and the impact to security
labs: git-github
- Change management: request, impact analysis, approval (change advisory board), test, schedule a maintenance window, backout plan, document.
- Stakeholders and owners must sign off; the backout plan is how you undo a failed change.
- Technical impacts: allow/deny lists, restarts, downtime, legacy apps, dependencies.
- Update diagrams, policies and version control after every change.
Q: What is a backout plan?
A: The documented steps to roll back a change if it fails.
Q: Who typically approves changes in a formal process?
A: The change advisory board (CAB), with the change owner and stakeholders.
Q: Why schedule changes in a maintenance window?
A: To limit the business impact of downtime and restarts.
Q: What should happen to network diagrams and documentation after a change?
A: They must be updated so documentation matches reality.
Q: Why does version control matter for change management?
A: It tracks what changed, when and by whom, and makes rollback possible.

## 1.4 Explain the importance of using appropriate cryptographic solutions
labs: cryptography-applied, password-cracking-john
- Symmetric (AES, one shared key, fast) vs asymmetric (RSA/ECC, public+private keys, used for key exchange and signatures).
- Hashing (SHA-256) is one-way, for integrity. Salting stops rainbow tables; key stretching (bcrypt, PBKDF2) slows brute force.
- PKI: CA signs certificates; CRL and OCSP check revocation; a CSR requests a cert. Wildcard certs cover *.domain.
- Encryption levels: full-disk, partition, volume, file, database, record. Transport: TLS, VPN (IPsec).
- Hardware: TPM (on the motherboard), HSM (dedicated appliance), secure enclave, key management system.
- Obfuscation: steganography, tokenization, data masking. Blockchain is a distributed, append-only ledger.
Q: What's the main difference between symmetric and asymmetric encryption?
A: Symmetric uses one shared key (fast, bulk data); asymmetric uses a public/private key pair (key exchange, signatures).
Q: What does a salt protect against?
A: Precomputed attacks like rainbow tables, and identical passwords producing identical hashes.
Q: What is key stretching?
A: Running a password through a slow hash many times (bcrypt, PBKDF2) to make brute force expensive.
Q: OCSP vs CRL?
A: Both check certificate revocation; CRL is a downloaded list, OCSP asks the CA about one cert in real time.
Q: TPM vs HSM?
A: TPM is a chip on one device's motherboard; an HSM is a dedicated, often networked, appliance for managing many keys.
Q: What is tokenization?
A: Replacing sensitive data (like a card number) with a non-sensitive token that maps back only via a secure vault.
Q: Which key do you use to verify someone's digital signature?
A: Their public key.
Q: What does a digital signature give you?
A: Integrity, authentication and non-repudiation.

## 2.1 Compare and contrast common threat actors and motivations

- Attributes: internal vs external, resources/funding, sophistication.
- Motivations: data exfiltration, espionage, service disruption, blackmail, financial gain, philosophical/political beliefs, ethical, revenge, disruption/chaos, war.
Q: What does APT stand for and who is usually behind one?
A: Advanced persistent threat, usually a nation-state group with lots of resources that stays hidden for a long time.
Q: An employee installs an unapproved cloud app to get work done. What threat is that?
A: Shadow IT.
Q: What usually motivates organized crime groups?
A: Financial gain (ransomware, fraud, data theft for sale).
Q: What motivates a hacktivist?
A: Philosophical or political beliefs.
Q: Why are insider threats dangerous?
A: They already have legitimate access and knowledge of the systems.

## 2.2 Explain common threat vectors and attack surfaces
labs: phishing-analysis, recon-nmap-metasploit
- Message-based vectors: email, SMS (smishing), instant messaging. Also image-based and file-based (malicious attachments).
- Voice calls (vishing), removable devices (USB drops), vulnerable software (client-based vs agentless), unsupported systems.
- Unsecure networks: wireless, wired, Bluetooth. Open service ports, default credentials, supply chain (MSPs, vendors, suppliers).
- Human vectors: phishing, vishing, smishing, misinformation/disinformation, impersonation, business email compromise, pretexting, watering hole, brand impersonation, typosquatting.
Q: What is a watering hole attack?
A: Compromising a website the target group already visits, so they get infected there.
Q: What is typosquatting?
A: Registering domains that are misspellings of real ones (e.g. g00gle.com) to catch mistyped visits.
Q: What is pretexting?
A: Inventing a believable scenario (a pretext) to trick someone into giving up information.
Q: What is business email compromise (BEC)?
A: Using a compromised or spoofed executive/vendor email account to trick staff into payments or data transfers.
Q: Why are default credentials a threat vector?
A: They're published online, so attackers try them first on any new device.
Q: What is smishing?
A: Phishing via SMS text messages.

## 2.3 Explain various types of vulnerabilities
labs: binary-exploitation, databases-and-sql, typescript-javascript
- Application: memory injection, buffer overflow, race conditions (TOCTOU), malicious updates.
- Web: SQL injection, cross-site scripting (XSS). OS-based, hardware (firmware, end-of-life, legacy).
- Virtualization: VM escape, resource reuse. Cloud-specific, supply chain (service/hardware/software providers).
- Cryptographic weaknesses, misconfiguration, mobile (sideloading, jailbreaking), zero-day.
Q: What is a TOCTOU vulnerability?
A: Time-of-check to time-of-use race condition: the resource changes between when it's checked and when it's used.
Q: What is a buffer overflow?
A: Writing more data into a buffer than it holds, overwriting adjacent memory (possibly a return address).
Q: What is VM escape?
A: Breaking out of a virtual machine to reach the hypervisor or other VMs.
Q: SQL injection vs XSS: what does each target?
A: SQLi targets the database behind the app; XSS injects script that runs in other users' browsers.
Q: What is a zero-day?
A: A vulnerability the vendor doesn't know about or hasn't patched yet.
Q: What is sideloading?
A: Installing mobile apps from outside the official app store.

## 2.4 Given a scenario, analyze indicators of malicious activity
labs: log-analysis, malware-analysis, network-traffic-analysis, password-cracking-john
- Malware: ransomware, trojan, worm (self-spreading), spyware, bloatware, virus, keylogger, logic bomb, rootkit.
- Physical: brute force, RFID cloning. Network: DDoS (amplified, reflected), DNS attacks, wireless, on-path, credential replay, malicious code.
- Application: injection, buffer overflow, replay, privilege escalation, forgery (CSRF/SSRF), directory traversal.
- Crypto: downgrade, collision, birthday. Password: spraying (one password, many accounts) vs brute force.
- Indicators: account lockout, concurrent session usage, blocked content, impossible travel, resource consumption/inaccessibility, out-of-cycle logging, missing logs, published/documented.
Q: Password spraying vs brute force?
A: Spraying tries a few common passwords across many accounts (avoids lockouts); brute force tries many passwords on one account.
Q: What is "impossible travel"?
A: Logins from two locations too far apart for the time between them, a sign of stolen credentials.
Q: What makes a worm different from a virus?
A: A worm spreads by itself over the network; a virus needs a host file and user action.
Q: What is a logic bomb?
A: Malicious code that triggers when a condition is met (a date, an account being deleted).
Q: What does `../../etc/passwd` in a URL indicate?
A: A directory (path) traversal attempt.
Q: What is a downgrade attack?
A: Forcing a connection to use an older, weaker protocol or cipher (e.g. SSL instead of TLS).
Q: What is a reflected/amplified DDoS?
A: Spoofing the victim's IP in small requests to servers (DNS, NTP) that send much larger replies to the victim.
Q: Missing logs or gaps in logging are an indicator of what?
A: An attacker covering their tracks.

## 2.5 Explain the purpose of mitigation techniques used to secure the enterprise
labs: system-hardening-windows-linux, firewalls-pfsense, group-policy
- Segmentation, access control (ACLs, permissions), application allow lists, isolation, patching, encryption, monitoring.
- Least privilege; configuration enforcement; decommissioning.
- Hardening: install endpoint protection, host-based firewall, HIPS, disable ports/protocols, change default passwords, remove unnecessary software.
Q: What is an application allow list?
A: Only approved applications can run; everything else is blocked.
Q: What does least privilege mean?
A: Users and services get only the access they need for their job, nothing more.
Q: What is HIPS?
A: Host-based intrusion prevention system: watches a single host and blocks malicious activity.
Q: Why does network segmentation reduce risk?
A: It limits lateral movement and blast radius when one segment is compromised.
Q: Name three quick hardening steps for a new server.
A: Change default passwords, disable unused ports/services, remove unneeded software (also patch and enable a host firewall).

## 3.1 Compare and contrast security implications of different architecture models
labs: virtualization-proxmox, networking-tcp-ip-dhcp-nat-subnets
- Cloud: responsibility matrix (shared responsibility), hybrid, third-party vendors. IaC, serverless, microservices.
- Network infrastructure: physical isolation (air-gapped), logical segmentation, SDN.
- On-premises, centralized vs decentralized, containerization, virtualization, IoT, ICS/SCADA, RTOS, embedded systems, high availability.
- Considerations: availability, resilience, cost, responsiveness, scalability, ease of deployment, risk transference, ease of recovery, patch availability, inability to patch, power, compute.
Q: What is the shared responsibility model?
A: The cloud provider secures the cloud itself; the customer secures what they put in it. The split depends on IaaS/PaaS/SaaS.
Q: What is an air-gapped network?
A: One physically isolated with no connection to other networks.
Q: What is SDN?
A: Software-defined networking: the control plane is separated from the data plane and managed centrally by software.
Q: What is Infrastructure as Code (IaC)?
A: Defining infrastructure in code files, so it's repeatable, versioned and reviewable.
Q: Why are ICS/SCADA systems hard to secure?
A: They run critical physical processes, are often legacy, can't be patched or rebooted easily, and need availability above all.
Q: Containers vs VMs?
A: Containers share the host OS kernel (light, fast); VMs each run a full OS on a hypervisor (stronger isolation).

## 3.2 Given a scenario, apply security principles to secure enterprise infrastructure
labs: firewalls-pfsense, ids-snort, vpn-tailscale, networking-tcp-ip-dhcp-nat-subnets
- Infrastructure: device placement, security zones, attack surface, connectivity, failure modes (fail-open vs fail-closed).
- Device attributes: active vs passive, inline vs tap/monitor.
- Devices: jump server, proxy server, IPS/IDS, load balancer, sensors, port security (802.1X, EAP), firewalls (WAF, UTM, NGFW, layer 4/7).
- Secure communication: VPN, remote access, tunneling (TLS, IPsec), SD-WAN, SASE.
Q: Fail-open vs fail-closed?
A: Fail-open lets traffic through when the device fails (keeps availability); fail-closed blocks all traffic (keeps security).
Q: What is a jump server?
A: A hardened host admins connect to first, then from it into a protected network segment.
Q: What does 802.1X do?
A: Port-based network access control: a device must authenticate (via EAP and usually RADIUS) before the switch port allows traffic.
Q: IDS vs IPS placement?
A: An IDS is usually passive on a tap/span port; an IPS sits inline so it can block.
Q: What is a WAF?
A: Web application firewall: inspects HTTP traffic for attacks like SQLi and XSS.
Q: What is SASE?
A: Secure access service edge: cloud-delivered networking and security (SD-WAN plus firewall, CASB, zero-trust access) close to users.
Q: Layer 4 vs layer 7 firewall?
A: Layer 4 filters by IP/port/protocol; layer 7 (NGFW) understands applications and content.

## 3.3 Compare and contrast concepts and strategies to protect data
labs: cryptography-applied, secure-data-sanitization
- Data types: regulated, trade secret, intellectual property, legal, financial, human/non-human readable.
- Classifications: sensitive, confidential, public, restricted, private, critical.
- Data states: at rest, in transit, in use. Data sovereignty and geolocation.
- Methods: geographic restrictions, encryption, hashing, masking, tokenization, obfuscation, segmentation, permission restrictions.
Q: What are the three states of data?
A: At rest (stored), in transit (moving over a network), in use (in memory being processed).
Q: What is data sovereignty?
A: Data is subject to the laws of the country where it's stored.
Q: Masking vs tokenization?
A: Masking hides part of the data (****1234); tokenization replaces it entirely with a token mapped in a vault.
Q: How do you protect data in transit?
A: Encrypt the connection (TLS, IPsec/VPN).
Q: What is geofencing/geographic restriction?
A: Limiting access to data or systems based on the user's location.

## 3.4 Explain the importance of resilience and recovery in security architecture
labs: virtualization-proxmox
- High availability: load balancing vs clustering. Site considerations: hot (ready now), warm (some gear), cold (empty space), geographic dispersion.
- Platform diversity, multi-cloud. Continuity of operations, capacity planning (people, technology, infrastructure).
- Testing: tabletop exercises, failover, simulation, parallel processing.
- Backups: onsite/offsite, frequency, encryption, snapshots, recovery, replication, journaling.
- Power: generators, UPS.
Q: Hot vs warm vs cold site?
A: Hot is fully equipped and ready almost immediately; warm has some equipment and needs setup; cold is space and power only.
Q: What's a tabletop exercise?
A: A discussion-based walkthrough of a scenario with the team, no systems touched.
Q: What does a UPS provide?
A: Short-term battery power to ride out outages or allow a clean shutdown until a generator starts.
Q: Snapshot vs backup?
A: A snapshot captures a VM/volume state at a point in time on the same storage; a backup is a separate copy that survives losing the original.
Q: Load balancing vs clustering?
A: Load balancing spreads requests across independent servers; clustering makes several servers act as one system with failover.

## 4.1 Given a scenario, apply common security techniques to computing resources
labs: system-hardening-windows-linux, group-policy, windows-admin-powershell
- Secure baselines: establish, deploy, maintain. Hardening targets: mobile, workstations, switches, routers, cloud, servers, ICS/SCADA, embedded, RTOS, IoT.
- Wireless: site surveys and heat maps; WPA3, RADIUS, AAA. Cryptographic protocols and authentication protocols.
- Application security: input validation, secure cookies, static code analysis, code signing. Sandboxing. Monitoring.
- Mobile solutions: MDM; deployment models BYOD, COPE, CYOD; connection methods cellular, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth.
Q: What is a secure baseline?
A: A documented, approved secure configuration that systems are built from and checked against.
Q: BYOD vs COPE vs CYOD?
A: BYOD: employee's own device. COPE: company-owned, personally enabled. CYOD: choose from an approved list of company devices.
Q: What does WPA3 improve over WPA2?
A: SAE (Dragonfly) replaces the PSK handshake, stopping offline dictionary attacks, and adds forward secrecy.
Q: What is input validation?
A: Checking that user input matches expected type, length and format before using it.
Q: What is code signing?
A: Signing software with a certificate so users can verify who published it and that it wasn't modified.
Q: What does a wireless site survey produce?
A: A heat map of signal coverage, used to place access points and find rogue APs.

## 4.2 Explain the security implications of proper hardware, software, and data asset management
labs: secure-data-sanitization, hardware-diagnostics-and-repair
- Acquisition/procurement, assignment/accounting (ownership, classification), monitoring/asset tracking (inventory, enumeration).
- Disposal/decommissioning: sanitization, destruction, certification, data retention.
Q: Why does asset inventory matter for security?
A: You can't protect, patch or monitor what you don't know you have.
Q: Sanitization vs destruction?
A: Sanitization wipes data so the media can be reused; destruction physically ruins the media (shred, degauss, incinerate).
Q: What is a certificate of destruction?
A: A document from a disposal vendor proving the media was destroyed.
Q: Does degaussing work on SSDs?
A: No, it only affects magnetic media. SSDs need crypto erase / secure erase or physical destruction.
Q: What is data retention?
A: Keeping data for a required length of time (legal, regulatory) and disposing of it afterwards.

## 4.3 Explain various activities associated with vulnerability management
labs: recon-nmap-metasploit, linux-server-admin-ubuntu
- Identification: vulnerability scans, application security (static/dynamic analysis, package monitoring), threat feeds (OSINT, proprietary, ISAC, dark web), penetration testing, bug bounty, system/process audits.
- Analysis: confirmation (false positive/negative), prioritize, CVSS, CVE, classification, exposure factor, environmental variables, impact, risk tolerance.
- Response: patching, insurance, segmentation, compensating controls, exceptions and exemptions.
- Validation: rescanning, audit, verification. Reporting.
Q: What is CVSS?
A: Common Vulnerability Scoring System: a 0-10 severity score for vulnerabilities.
Q: What is a CVE?
A: Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures: a unique public ID for a known vulnerability (e.g. CVE-2021-44228).
Q: What is a false positive in a vuln scan?
A: The scanner reports a vulnerability that isn't actually there.
Q: Credentialed vs non-credentialed scan?
A: Credentialed scans log in and see much more (missing patches, configs); non-credentialed show what an outsider sees.
Q: What should you do after patching a vulnerability?
A: Validate it: rescan to confirm the fix.
Q: What is an ISAC?
A: Information Sharing and Analysis Center: an industry group that shares threat intel among members.

## 4.4 Explain security alerting and monitoring concepts and tools
labs: siem-security-onion, log-analysis, ids-snort
- Monitor systems, applications and infrastructure. Activities: log aggregation, alerting, scanning, reporting, archiving, alert response (quarantine, alert tuning).
- Tools: SCAP, benchmarks, agents/agentless, SIEM, antivirus, DLP, SNMP traps, NetFlow, vulnerability scanners.
Q: What does a SIEM do?
A: Collects and correlates logs from many sources, alerts on suspicious patterns, and stores them for investigation.
Q: What is alert tuning?
A: Adjusting rules and thresholds to cut false positives without missing real events.
Q: What is NetFlow?
A: Metadata about network flows (who talked to whom, ports, bytes, time), not full packet content.
Q: What is SCAP?
A: Security Content Automation Protocol: standards for automated config and vulnerability checks.
Q: What is DLP?
A: Data loss prevention: detects and blocks sensitive data leaving where it shouldn't.
Q: What is an SNMP trap?
A: An alert a network device sends to the management station when something happens.

## 4.5 Given a scenario, modify enterprise capabilities to enhance security
labs: firewalls-pfsense, ids-snort, phishing-analysis
- Firewall: rules, access lists, ports/protocols, screened subnets. IDS/IPS: trends, signatures.
- Web filter: agent-based, centralized proxy, URL scanning, content categorization, block rules, reputation.
- OS security: Group Policy, SELinux. Secure protocols, DNS filtering, email security (DMARC, DKIM, SPF, gateway).
- File integrity monitoring, DLP, NAC, EDR/XDR, user behavior analytics.
Q: What does SPF do?
A: Lists which mail servers may send for a domain (DNS TXT record).
Q: What does DKIM do?
A: Adds a cryptographic signature to email so receivers can verify it wasn't altered and came from the domain.
Q: What does DMARC do?
A: Tells receivers what to do when SPF/DKIM fail (none, quarantine, reject) and where to send reports.
Q: What is a screened subnet?
A: A DMZ: a network segment for public-facing services, separated from the internal network.
Q: What is file integrity monitoring?
A: Alerting when important files change, by comparing hashes to a known-good baseline.
Q: What does EDR add beyond antivirus?
A: Continuous endpoint telemetry, behavior detection, investigation and automated response.
Q: What is SELinux?
A: Mandatory access control for Linux: policy decides what processes can access, even for root.

## 4.6 Given a scenario, implement and maintain identity and access management
labs: active-directory, group-policy, windows-admin-powershell
- Provisioning/de-provisioning, permission assignments, identity proofing, federation, SSO (LDAP, OAuth, SAML), interoperability, attestation.
- Access models: mandatory, discretionary, role-based, rule-based, attribute-based, time-of-day restrictions, least privilege.
- MFA: something you know, have, are, somewhere you are. Hard/soft tokens, security keys, biometrics.
- Password concepts: length, complexity, reuse, expiration, age; password managers; passwordless.
- Privileged access management: just-in-time permissions, password vaulting, ephemeral credentials.
Q: SAML vs OAuth?
A: SAML is XML-based authentication/SSO (often enterprise web apps); OAuth is authorization delegation (grant an app access to your data), often with OpenID Connect for login.
Q: RBAC vs ABAC?
A: RBAC grants access by job role; ABAC evaluates attributes (user, resource, environment) for fine-grained decisions.
Q: What is discretionary access control?
A: The owner of a resource decides who can access it (like NTFS permissions).
Q: What is just-in-time access?
A: Privileged rights granted only for a short window when needed, then removed automatically.
Q: What is federation?
A: Trusting identities from another organization or provider so users log in with their home credentials.
Q: Which MFA factor is a fingerprint?
A: Something you are (inherence).
Q: Why should de-provisioning be fast?
A: Leftover accounts of departed users are easy targets for attackers and insiders.

## 4.7 Explain the importance of automation and orchestration related to secure operations
labs: bash-scripting, python, windows-admin-powershell

Q: Name two security benefits of automation.
A: Consistent enforcement of baselines and faster reaction time (also scaling and fewer human errors).
Q: What is a guard rail in automation?

Q: Name a risk of automation.
A: It can become a single point of failure, or add complexity and technical debt.
Q: What is SOAR?
A: Security orchestration, automation and response: playbooks that automate incident handling across tools.

## 4.8 Explain appropriate incident response activities
labs: incident-response, digital-forensics, malware-analysis
- Process: preparation, detection, analysis, containment, eradication, recovery, lessons learned.
- Training and testing: tabletop exercise, simulation.
- Root cause analysis, threat hunting.
- Digital forensics: legal hold, chain of custody, acquisition, reporting, preservation, e-discovery.
Q: What are the incident response phases (CompTIA order)?
A: Preparation, detection, analysis, containment, eradication, recovery, lessons learned.
Q: What is chain of custody?
A: A record of who handled evidence, when and why, from collection to court.
Q: What is a legal hold?
A: A requirement to preserve data that may be relevant to litigation, overriding normal deletion.
Q: Containment vs eradication?
A: Containment stops the spread (isolate hosts); eradication removes the cause (malware, accounts, vulnerabilities).
Q: What is threat hunting?
A: Proactively searching for attackers already in the environment, before alerts fire.
Q: What happens in lessons learned?
A: A post-incident review of what happened and what to improve, feeding back into preparation.

labs: log-analysis, network-traffic-analysis, siem-security-onion

## 5.1 Summarize elements of effective security governance
- Guidelines, policies (AUP, information security, business continuity, disaster recovery, incident response, SDLC, change management).
- Standards (password, access control, physical, encryption) and procedures (change management, onboarding/offboarding, playbooks).
- External considerations: regulatory, legal, industry, local/regional/national/global.
- Monitoring and revision; governance structures (boards, committees, government entities, centralized/decentralized).
- Roles: owners, controllers, processors, custodians/stewards.
Q: Policy vs standard vs procedure?
A: Policy states what and why; a standard sets specific mandatory requirements; a procedure gives step-by-step how.
Q: What is an AUP?
A: Acceptable use policy: what users may and may not do with company systems.
Q: Data controller vs data processor?
A: The controller decides why and how personal data is processed; the processor handles it on the controller's behalf.
Q: What does a data custodian do?
A: Implements and maintains the controls on the data day to day (backups, permissions), as directed by the owner.
Q: What is a playbook?
A: A documented, repeatable set of steps for a specific situation (e.g. responding to phishing).

## 5.2 Explain elements of the risk management process
- Risk identification, assessment (ad hoc, recurring, one-time, continuous), analysis: qualitative vs quantitative.
- SLE = asset value x exposure factor. ALE = SLE x ARO.
- Risk register: key risk indicators, risk owners, thresholds. Risk tolerance, appetite (expansionary, conservative, neutral).
- Strategies: transfer, accept (exemption, exception), avoid, mitigate.
- Business impact analysis: RTO, RPO, MTTR, MTBF.
Q: How do you calculate ALE?
A: ALE = SLE x ARO (single loss expectancy times annualized rate of occurrence).
Q: A $100,000 server, 40% damaged per incident, twice a year. ALE?
A: SLE = 100,000 x 0.4 = 40,000; ALE = 40,000 x 2 = $80,000.
Q: RTO vs RPO?
A: RTO is how long until the system must be back; RPO is how much data loss (time since last backup) is acceptable.
Q: Buying cyber insurance is which risk strategy?
A: Transfer.
Q: What's a risk register?
A: A list of identified risks with their owner, likelihood, impact, and response.
Q: MTBF vs MTTR?
A: Mean time between failures (reliability) vs mean time to repair (how fast you fix it).

## 5.3 Explain the processes associated with third-party risk assessment and management
- Vendor assessment: penetration testing, right-to-audit clause, evidence of internal audits, independent assessments, supply chain analysis.
- Vendor selection: due diligence, conflict of interest. Monitoring and questionnaires, rules of engagement.
- Agreements: SLA, MOA, MOU, MSA, WO/SOW, NDA, BPA.
Q: What is an SLA?
A: Service level agreement: the measurable service a vendor guarantees (uptime, response time).
Q: MOU vs MOA?
A: An MOU is a non-binding statement of intent; an MOA is more formal and spells out responsibilities.
Q: What is an MSA?
A: Master service agreement: the overall terms for future work, with details in SOWs/work orders.
Q: What is a right-to-audit clause?
A: Contract language letting you audit the vendor's security.
Q: What does an NDA do?
A: Legally binds a party to keep shared information confidential.

## 5.4 Summarize elements of effective security compliance
- Compliance reporting (internal, external). Consequences of non-compliance: fines, sanctions, reputational damage, loss of license, contractual impacts.
- Compliance monitoring: due diligence/care, attestation and acknowledgement, internal/external, automation.
- Privacy: legal implications, data subject, controller vs processor, ownership, data inventory and retention, right to be forgotten.
Q: What is the right to be forgotten?
A: A data subject's right (e.g. under GDPR) to have their personal data erased.
Q: Due diligence vs due care?
A: Due diligence is investigating and understanding the risks; due care is acting reasonably to protect against them.
Q: Name two consequences of non-compliance.
A: Fines and reputational damage (also sanctions, loss of license, contract impacts).
Q: What is a data subject?
A: The person the personal data is about.

## 5.5 Explain types and purposes of audits and assessments
labs: recon-nmap-metasploit
- Attestation; internal (compliance, audit committee, self-assessments) and external (regulatory, examinations, assessment, independent third-party audit).
- Penetration testing: physical, offensive, defensive, integrated; known, partially known, unknown environment; reconnaissance (passive, active).
Q: Known vs unknown environment pen test?
A: Known (white box): testers get full information; unknown (black box): no prior information, like an outside attacker.
Q: Passive vs active reconnaissance?
A: Passive gathers info without touching the target (OSINT); active interacts with it (port scans).
Q: What's the purpose of rules of engagement in a pen test?
A: Defining scope, timing, allowed techniques and contacts, so testing is authorized and safe.
Q: What is attestation?
A: A formal statement (often by an auditor) that controls or reports are accurate.

## 5.6 Given a scenario, implement security awareness practices
labs: phishing-analysis
- Phishing campaigns: recognizing attempts, responding to reported suspicious messages.
- Anomalous behavior recognition: risky, unexpected, unintentional.
- User guidance and training: policy/handbooks, situational awareness, insider threat, password management, removable media and cables, social engineering, operational security, hybrid/remote work.
- Reporting and monitoring: initial, recurring. Development and execution.
Q: Why run internal phishing simulations?
A: To train users to recognize and report phishing, and to measure improvement over time.
Q: What should a user do with a suspicious email?
A: Report it using the official process (report button/security team) without clicking links or attachments.
Q: Why warn users about unknown USB cables and drives?
A: They can carry malware or act as malicious keyboards (e.g. a USB Rubber Ducky or O.MG cable).
Q: What is operational security (OPSEC) for employees?
A: Not leaking sensitive details through social media, conversation or carelessness.
