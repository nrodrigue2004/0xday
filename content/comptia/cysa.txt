## 1.1 Explain concepts related to system and network architecture in security operations
labs: log-analysis, system-hardening-windows-linux
@notes
An analyst can only investigate what was logged, so architecture questions are mostly "where does the evidence come from and can I trust it".

### Logging
- **Ingestion**: agents, syslog forwarders or API pulls ship logs to a central store (a SIEM). Normalize fields (timestamp, host, user, src/dst IP) or correlation breaks.
- **Time sync**: every device syncs to NTP. Two hosts five minutes apart make a timeline impossible and can wreck Kerberos too. Store in UTC.
- **Integrity and security**: send logs off the box immediately, over TLS, write-once if possible. An attacker with admin deletes local logs first (Windows event 1102 means the Security log was cleared).
- **Retention**: set by compliance and by dwell time. Intrusions often sit unnoticed for months, so hot storage for weeks and cold storage for a year or more is common.

### Operating system
- **Hardening**: remove unused services and software, patch, least privilege, disable default accounts, host firewall, secure baselines (CIS).
- **Critical files**: Windows has the registry hives, SAM, `System32`, startup folders and `Windows\Temp`. Linux has `/etc/passwd`, `/etc/shadow`, `/etc/sudoers`, `/var/log`, cron entries and `~/.ssh/authorized_keys`. Know what normal looks like so deviations stand out.
- **System processes**: `lsass.exe`, `svchost.exe`, `services.exe` and `explorer.exe` are common impersonation targets. Wrong parent process, wrong path or a misspelling (`svch0st`) are classic tells.

### Infrastructure
- **Cloud native**: services built for the cloud. Evidence lives in provider logs (CloudTrail, Azure Activity, VPC flow logs) and you cannot image a managed service.
- **Virtualization**: many VMs per host. Snapshots help forensics. VM escape is the rare worst case.
- **Containerization**: short-lived, so logs and artifacts vanish when the container does. Ship logs out, scan images, avoid privileged containers.
- **APIs**: the glue between tools and a major attack surface. Authenticate, rate-limit, log, and keep keys out of code.

### Devices and networks
- **Mobile and endpoint management** (MDM/UEM, EDR) lets you enforce policy, wipe lost devices and collect telemetry.
- **ZTNA**: no implicit trust from network location, verify each request on identity, device health and context.
- **SASE**: network and security services (SWG, CASB, ZTNA, firewall-as-a-service) delivered from the cloud edge for remote users and branches.
- **Hybrid cloud**: on-prem plus cloud means two sets of logs and identity to stitch together.

### Identity and data
- **PAM**: vault admin credentials, check them out per session, record the session, rotate afterwards. **Secrets management** keeps API keys and passwords in a vault, not in repos.
- **AuthN vs AuthZ**: MFA, SSO with SAML/OIDC, OAuth for delegated access, Kerberos in AD.
- **Encryption**: AES at rest, TLS in transit, hashing with salt for passwords. **Data protection**: classification, DLP, masking, tokenization, backups.

### Critical infrastructure
**OT** runs physical processes. **ICS** is the control system, **SCADA** supervises many remote sites. These favor availability and safety over confidentiality, run legacy protocols (Modbus, DNP3) with no authentication and cannot be patched casually. Segment them from IT, monitor passively, never active-scan a PLC carelessly.
@cards

A: Event correlation and timelines depend on consistent timestamps. Clock drift hides or reorders attacker steps.
Q: Why forward logs off the host immediately?
A: Attackers with admin rights clear local logs. A remote copy preserves integrity.

A: 1102 (System log cleared is 104).
Q: Why is process path and parent relevant to spotting malware?
A: Malware imitates names like svchost.exe. The legit one runs from System32 under services.exe. Wrong path or parent is suspicious.
Q: What does SASE bundle?
A: Cloud-delivered networking plus security: SWG, CASB, ZTNA, FWaaS.
Q: What does PAM add that a plain admin account does not?
A: Vaulting, per-use checkout, session recording and rotation of privileged credentials.
Q: Priority order in OT versus IT?
A: OT puts availability and safety first; IT usually puts confidentiality first.
Q: Biggest evidence problem with containers?
A: They are ephemeral. Logs and artifacts disappear unless exported.

## 1.2 Given a scenario, analyze indicators of potential malicious activity
labs: network-traffic-analysis, phishing-analysis, log-analysis
@notes
The job here is pattern recognition: a single odd event is noise, several in a chain are an incident.

### Network indicators
- **Rogue devices**: unknown MACs, a rogue AP or DHCP server. Compare against the asset inventory.
- **Enumeration**: many connection attempts across hosts or ports in a short time (port scans, ping sweeps, SMB or LDAP queries from a workstation).
- **Anomalous activity**: beaconing (fixed-interval small callbacks, often with jitter), large outbound transfers, DNS with long random subdomains (tunneling or DGA), traffic at 3 a.m., unusual protocols.
- **Unexpected ports**: HTTP on 4444, SSH on 443, high ports to external hosts. Reverse shells often use common ports to slip through.

### Host indicators
- **Resource consumption**: sustained CPU and GPU use (cryptominer), disk churn (ransomware encrypting), memory spikes.
- **Unauthorized software** and **unauthorized configuration changes**: new services, scheduled tasks, run keys, disabled AV, edited hosts file, new local admin.
- **Suspicious or rogue processes**: odd parent-child pairs (Office spawning PowerShell, `cmd` under a browser).
- **LOLBins**: legitimate tools abused so nothing "malicious" is dropped: `certutil -urlcache`, `mshta`, `rundll32`, `regsvr32`, `bitsadmin`, PowerShell with `-enc`, `wmic`. Detection is behavioral, so hunt the command line.
- **File system changes**: new executables in temp or AppData, mass renames, changed extensions, deleted shadow copies (`vssadmin delete shadows`).
- **Data exfiltration**: archives staged then sent out, big uploads to cloud storage, DNS or ICMP tunneling.

### Application and cloud
- **Application**: service disruption, spikes of 500s, injection strings in logs (`' OR 1=1`, `../../`), new admin users, unusual API calls.
- **Cloud**: new access keys, resources created in odd regions (mining), public buckets, disabled logging, privilege escalation in IAM, API calls from unfamiliar IPs.

### Social engineering and identity
- **Typosquatting** (`rnicrosoft.com`), **URL shorteners** that hide the target, lookalike Unicode domains.
- **Impossible travel**: logins from two far-apart places faster than a plane could go. Verify first: VPNs and mobile carriers cause false positives.
- Other identity tells: MFA fatigue (burst of push prompts), new inbox rules forwarding mail, logins at odd hours, many failures then a success (spray or brute force), service accounts logging in interactively.
- **BEC**: attacker (or a lookalike domain) impersonates an exec or vendor to redirect payments. Look for reply-to mismatch, changed bank details, urgency, inbox rules that hide replies.

## 1.3 Given a scenario, use tools to determine malicious activity
labs: network-traffic-analysis, ids-snort, siem-security-onion, malware-analysis
@notes
Know what each tool is for and what its output looks like. Exam scenarios hand you a snippet and ask what it means.

### Decode and parse
- **CyberChef**: browser "recipe" tool for base64, hex, XOR, URL decoding, gunzip, regex extraction. Chain operations on the fly.

### Packets and network sensors
- **Wireshark**: GUI packet analysis. Display filters: `ip.addr == 10.0.0.5`, `tcp.port == 4444`, `http.request`, `dns`, `tcp.flags.syn == 1 && tcp.flags.ack == 0`. Follow TCP stream shows a whole conversation.
- **tcpdump**: CLI capture, BPF filters: `tcpdump -i eth0 -w cap.pcap host 10.0.0.5 and port 443`. `-nn` skips name resolution.
- **Snort / Suricata**: signature IDS/IPS. Rules look like `alert tcp $EXTERNAL_NET any -> $HOME_NET 22 (msg:"SSH brute"; flags:S; threshold:...; sid:1000001;)`. Suricata is multi-threaded and also writes EVE JSON.
- **Zeek**: not signatures. It turns traffic into structured logs (`conn.log`, `dns.log`, `http.log`, `ssl.log`, `files.log`), ideal for hunting.

### Logs and intelligence
- **SIEM**: collects, normalizes, correlates and alerts (Splunk, Elastic, Sentinel). Know queries by shape: filter, group, count, sort.
- **Threat intel platforms**: **OTX** (community pulses of IoCs), **MISP** (open-source sharing platform), **OpenCTI** (knowledge graph for CTI).

### Endpoint
- **EDR/XDR**: endpoint telemetry with response (isolate host, kill process). XDR also pulls in email, network and cloud. **MDM** manages mobile devices (wipe, policy).

### Reputation and file analysis
- **WHOIS**, **AbuseIPDB**, **GEO-IP** for domain/IP age, ownership, abuse reports and location. A domain registered three days ago is suspicious.
- **strings** finds readable text in binaries (URLs, commands). **VirusTotal** checks hashes/files against many engines (do not upload sensitive files, upload the hash). **YARA** rules match patterns in files or memory: `rule X { strings: $a="cmd.exe /c" condition: $a }`.
- **Sandboxes** (**Joe Sandbox**, **Cuckoo**) run a sample safely and report behavior (files, registry, network). Beware sandbox-aware malware that sleeps.
- **MXToolbox** checks mail records (MX, SPF, DKIM, DMARC) and blacklists.
- **UEBA** (for example OpenUBA) baselines user behavior and flags deviations.

### Patterns, formats, scripts
- **Regex**: `\d{3}-\d{2}-\d{4}` (SSN-like), `(?:\d{1,3}\.){3}\d{1,3}` (IPv4), `[A-Za-z0-9+/=]{40,}` (base64 blob).
- **Formats**: JSON (objects, key/value), XML (tags), YAML (indentation, config and rules), EVTX (Windows event log, read with Event Viewer or `Get-WinEvent`).
- **Scripting**: Python for parsing and API calls, PowerShell for Windows admin and hunting (`Get-WinEvent`, `Get-Process`), shell for quick `grep`/`awk` pipelines.

## 1.4 Explain threat intelligence and threat-hunting concepts
labs: incident-response
@notes
### Threat actors
- **APT**: well-funded, patient, targeted, persistent. Usually nation-state or state-linked, with custom tooling and long dwell time.
- **Insider threat**: current/former employee or contractor with legitimate access. Intentional or accidental. Detect with DLP, UEBA and access reviews.
- Others to separate: hacktivists (ideology), organized crime (money), script kiddies (low skill).

### TTPs and models
- **TTPs** describe how an adversary operates: tactic = goal, technique = method, procedure = the specific implementation.
- **MITRE ATT&CK** catalogs tactics and techniques (Initial Access, Execution, Persistence, Privilege Escalation, Defense Evasion, Credential Access, Discovery, Lateral Movement, Collection, C2, Exfiltration, Impact). **Heat maps** overlay your detection coverage or an actor's techniques on the matrix to show gaps.
- **Pyramid of Pain** ranks IoC types by how much it hurts the attacker when you block them. Bottom to top: hash values, IP addresses, domain names, network/host artifacts, tools, TTPs. Hashes are trivial to change, TTPs are painful.
- **Attribution** (who did it) is hard and rarely needed to defend. Do not over-invest.

### Confidence
Rate intel on **timeliness** (stale IPs lie), **relevance** (does it touch your sector/tech?) and **accuracy** (corroborated by multiple sources?). Low-confidence intel gets monitored, high-confidence gets blocked.

### Sources
- **OSINT**: public (news, social media, paste sites, WHOIS, Shodan, vendor blogs). **Closed-source**: paid feeds, government, private vendors. **Sharing**: ISACs/ISAOs, MISP communities, STIX/TAXII formats.

### Indicators of compromise
Collect, analyze (is it real, scope, context), apply (detections, blocks, retro-hunts). **Atomic** IoCs stand alone (IP, hash, domain). **Behavioral** IoCs describe actions (Office spawns PowerShell, then LSASS access). Atomic ages fast, behavioral lasts.

### Threat modeling and deception
- **STRIDE**: Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information disclosure, Denial of service, Elevation of privilege, a checklist to find threats per component.
- **Threat mapping** ties threats to your assets and attack paths. **Deception**: honeypots, honeytokens, decoy accounts. Any touch is a high-fidelity alert.

### Hunting
Proactive, hypothesis-driven search that assumes breach and does not wait for alerts. Flow: hypothesis (from intel or ATT&CK) → pick data → query → investigate → document → turn findings into detections.

## 1.5 Explain the importance of efficiency and process improvement in security operations
@notes
Analysts drown in alerts, so the goal is faster, more consistent, less burnout.

- **Standardize**: **playbooks** are step-by-step responses for a known scenario (phishing, ransomware). **Runbooks** are the operational procedures and are often automated. Both cut variance and onboarding time. Coordinate through clear roles, handover notes and ticketing.
- **Automation and orchestration**: **SOAR** takes alerts and runs workflows (enrich IP, quarantine email, disable account, open ticket). Automate the boring and high-volume steps and keep a human approval on destructive actions. **IaC** (Terraform, Ansible, CloudFormation) builds infrastructure from versioned code so environments are repeatable and auditable.
- **Data enrichment**: add context to raw alerts (asset owner, user risk, IP reputation, geo). **Rule and alert tuning** removes false-positive sources and adds exceptions without losing detection. **Dashboards** show trends so you can decide where to spend effort.
- **Integration**: **APIs** (programmatic access), **webhooks** (a system pushes an event when something happens), **plug-ins** (extend a tool). Prefer integrations over copy-paste between consoles.

> Danger: automating a bad process just makes mistakes faster. Test playbooks, log every automated action, keep an off switch.
@cards
Q: Playbook versus runbook?
A: A playbook is the response plan for a scenario; a runbook is the operational procedure, often executed automatically.
Q: What does SOAR do?
A: Orchestrates and automates security tasks across tools to respond faster.
Q: Why tune alert rules?
A: To cut false positives and alert fatigue while keeping real detections.
Q: Webhook definition?
A: An HTTP callback one system sends to another when an event occurs.
Q: Why use IaC for security?
A: Repeatable, reviewable, version-controlled builds; fewer manual misconfigurations.
Q: What should stay manual in automation?
A: High-impact, hard-to-reverse actions, or require an approval step.

## 2.1 Given a scenario, implement the appropriate vulnerability scanning method
labs: recon-nmap-metasploit
@notes
Scanning finds known weaknesses. It is not a pentest, and it lies in both directions, so you validate.

### Before you scan
- **Asset inventory**: you cannot scan what you do not know exists. Include shadow IT, cloud, containers and IoT.
- **Planning**: scheduling (off-peak, change windows), operations (notify owners, avoid critical processes), performance (bandwidth, scan throttling, fragile devices like OT or old printers), sensitivity levels (what data may a credentialed scan touch), segmentation (scanner placement so firewalls do not hide hosts), regulatory (PCI DSS requires quarterly external scans by an ASV).

### Scan types
- **Internal vs external**: internal sees what an insider sees, external what the internet sees.
- **Agent vs agentless**: agents on hosts report continuously, work for roaming laptops and need install and updates. Agentless scans over the network.
- **Credentialed vs non-credentialed**: credentialed logs in and sees missing patches, configs and local software, with far fewer false positives. Non-credentialed is the outsider view.
- **Passive vs active**: passive listens to traffic (safe for OT, slow, misses quiet hosts). Active sends probes (thorough, can disrupt).
- **Discovery**: mapping scans find hosts and topology; device fingerprinting guesses OS and services.
- **Baseline scanning**: compare configuration to a standard such as **CIS benchmarks**, **PCI DSS**, or the **ISO 27000** series.

> Exam habit: pick "credentialed" for accuracy, "passive" for fragile systems, "agent" for remote laptops, "external" for the attack surface view.
@cards
Q: Why use credentialed scans?
A: More accurate results with fewer false positives, and it can see missing patches and local configuration.
Q: Best scan type for a fragile ICS network?
A: Passive scanning, or very carefully controlled active scans.
Q: Agent-based scanning best fits what hosts?
A: Roaming laptops and remote endpoints that are not always on the network.
Q: What do CIS benchmarks provide?
A: Hardening baselines to compare configurations against.
Q: What is a mapping scan?
A: A discovery scan to find hosts and their layout.
Q: Why scan externally?
A: To see the attack surface as an internet attacker sees it.
Q: First prerequisite for good vulnerability management?
A: A complete asset inventory.

## 2.2 Given a scenario, analyze output from vulnerability assessment tools
labs: recon-nmap-metasploit
@notes
### Tools and their lane
- **Network scanning**: **Angry IP Scanner** (simple GUI sweep), **Masscan** (internet-scale, very fast port scanner).
- **Multipurpose**: **Nmap** (hosts, ports, services, OS, scripts via NSE), **Metasploit** (exploit framework used to validate), **Maltego** (link-analysis OSINT graphs), **Recon-ng** (modular OSINT framework).
- **Web app scanners**: **Burp Suite** (intercepting proxy), **ZAP** (free proxy and scanner), **Nikto** (quick web server checks).
- **Vulnerability scanners**: **Nessus**, **OpenVAS** (open source), **Nuclei** (template-based, fast, great for specific CVEs).
- **Cloud**: **ScoutSuite** and **Prowler** audit cloud accounts for misconfigurations, **Trivy** scans container images and IaC, **Checkov** scans IaC (Terraform etc.) before deployment.
- **Breach and attack simulation**: **Atomic Red Team** (small tests mapped to ATT&CK), **Caldera** (automated adversary emulation). They test whether your detections work.

### Reading output
- Nmap states: open, closed (reachable, nothing listening), filtered (firewall dropped it). `-sV` versions, `-sC` default scripts, `-O` OS guess, `-p-` all ports, `-sS` SYN scan.
- A finding has: title/plugin, CVE, severity (CVSS), host/port, evidence, solution. **Validate**: read the evidence, check the version and whether the vulnerable feature is on, then confirm with a second tool or manual test.
- Beware: banner says Apache 2.4.49 but a distro back-patched it (false positive), or a scan with no credentials missed a missing patch (false negative).

## 2.3 Given a scenario, analyze data to prioritize and mitigate vulnerabilities
@notes
You cannot fix everything. Prioritize on real risk.

### Criteria
- **Exploitability** (is there a public exploit, how easy), **active exploitation** (CISA KEV list, threat intel: highest priority), **asset value** and **impact** (crown jewels versus a test box), **patch availability**, **true/false positives and negatives** (TP real, FP false alarm, FN missed vuln, which is the scary one).
- **CVSS**: base score 0-10 from Attack Vector (network is worst), Attack Complexity, Privileges Required, User Interaction, Scope, then C/I/A impact. Severity bands: None 0, Low 0.1-3.9, Medium 4-6.9, High 7-8.9, Critical 9-10. Temporal and environmental metrics adjust for the real world. **EPSS** is a probability (0-1) that a CVE will be exploited in the next 30 days. Use CVSS for severity and EPSS for likelihood.
- **Context**: internet-facing (external) first, then internal, then isolated/air-gapped. A medium vuln on the perimeter can outrank a critical on an isolated lab box.

### Mitigation
- **Attack surface management**: continuously discover and shrink exposed assets and services.
- **Secure coding**: input validation, parameterized queries, output encoding, least privilege, secrets out of code.
- **Patching and configuration management**: test, stage, deploy, verify. **Exceptions**: documented, time-boxed, approved acceptance with an owner. **Compensating controls**: WAF rule, segmentation, virtual patching, extra monitoring when you cannot patch.
- **Validation**: rescan, retest or verify evidence afterward. A closed ticket is not a fixed vulnerability.
@cards
Q: Which CVSS attack vector is most dangerous?
A: Network (remotely exploitable).
Q: CVSS Critical range?
A: 9.0 to 10.0.
Q: What does EPSS estimate?
A: The probability a vulnerability will be exploited in the wild within 30 days.
Q: Which is worse, false positive or false negative?
A: False negative: a real vulnerability is missed and stays exposed.
Q: What is virtual patching?
A: A WAF/IPS rule that blocks exploitation when you can't patch the app.
Q: What must an exception include?
A: Business justification, owner, compensating controls and an expiry/review date.
Q: Why rescan after remediation?
A: To validate the fix actually worked.
Q: What is the CISA KEV catalog?
A: A list of vulnerabilities known to be exploited in the wild: patch first.

## 2.4 Explain concepts related to control types, risks, and vulnerability management
labs: system-hardening-windows-linux
@notes
- **Control types** (what it is): administrative (policy, training), technical (firewall, encryption), physical (locks, cameras). **Control functions** (what it does): preventative (stops), detective (spots), responsive (reacts), corrective (fixes or restores).
- **Risk**: **inherent** (before controls), **residual** (after controls), **risk appetite** (how much the organization will accept). Strategies: **accept**, **transfer** (insurance, vendor contract), **avoid** (stop the activity), **mitigate** (reduce with controls).
- **Policies, governance and SLOs**: policy sets the rule (patch criticals within 7 days), governance assigns owners and review, service-level objectives turn them into measurable targets.
- **Application security**: **SAST** reads source code without running it (early, many false positives). **DAST** attacks the running app (finds runtime and config flaws, later in the cycle). **SAMM** (OWASP Software Assurance Maturity Model) assesses and improves secure development practices.
- **Third-party risk**: vendors and libraries can carry flaws. **SCA** finds vulnerable open-source components, **SBOM** is the ingredient list of your software so you can quickly answer "are we affected by this CVE".
@cards
Q: Inherent vs residual risk?
A: Inherent is risk before controls; residual is what remains after controls.
Q: Name the four risk responses.
A: Accept, transfer, avoid, mitigate.
Q: SAST vs DAST?
A: SAST analyzes source/binaries statically; DAST tests the running application from outside.
Q: What is an SBOM?
A: A software bill of materials: an inventory of components and dependencies in software.
Q: What does SCA find?
A: Known vulnerabilities and license issues in third-party libraries.
Q: Buying cyber insurance is which risk strategy?
A: Transfer.
Q: Four control functions?
A: Preventative, detective, responsive, corrective.

## 3.1 Summarize concepts related to attack methodology frameworks
labs: incident-response
@notes
Frameworks give a shared vocabulary for describing and predicting attacks.

- **Cyber Kill Chain** (Lockheed Martin), seven linear phases: Reconnaissance → Weaponization → Delivery → Exploitation → Installation → Command and Control → Actions on Objectives. Defender idea: break any link and the attack fails. Weak at insiders and lateral movement because it is linear.
- **Diamond Model**: every event has four vertices: adversary, capability, infrastructure, victim. Pivot between them: find an attacker domain (infrastructure), then other victims hitting it. Also has meta-features such as timestamp, phase, result, direction, methodology.
- **MITRE ATT&CK**: matrix of tactics (columns, the why) and techniques/sub-techniques (the how, `T1566.001`), with real-world groups, software and detections. Use it for detection coverage gaps, hunt ideas and reporting. Not linear, not every technique is used.
- **Pick**: kill chain for explaining stage and where to block, diamond for linking intrusions and attribution, ATT&CK for detail and coverage.
@cards
Q: Seven kill chain phases?
A: Recon, weaponization, delivery, exploitation, installation, C2, actions on objectives.
Q: Diamond Model's four vertices?
A: Adversary, capability, infrastructure, victim.
Q: ATT&CK tactic vs technique?
A: Tactic is the goal (why); technique is how it's achieved.
Q: Which framework is best to map detection coverage gaps?
A: MITRE ATT&CK.
Q: Kill chain weakness?
A: Linear model; poor fit for insiders and lateral movement.
Q: What do analysts do with the diamond model?
A: Pivot between the vertices to connect intrusions.
@check

a) Reconnaissance
b) Delivery
c) Installation
d) C2
= b | Delivery is transmitting the weapon.

a) Kill chain
b) Diamond Model
c) STRIDE

= b | Pivoting from the infrastructure vertex.

## 3.2 Summarize the incident response process
labs: incident-response, digital-forensics
@notes
The phases in order:
1. **Preparation**: plan, team, tools, contacts, logging, backups, training. Nothing else works without it.
2. **Detection**: alerts, user reports, threat intel.
3. **Analysis**: confirm it's real, scope, severity, what systems and data, timeline.
4. **Containment**: stop the spread. Short-term (isolate host, disable account, block IP) then long-term (rebuild segments, temporary fixes). Preserve evidence first when you can, and weigh business impact.
5. **Eradication**: remove the cause: malware, persistence, bad accounts, patch the hole, reset credentials.
6. **Recovery**: restore from clean backups, rebuild, monitor closely, return to production in stages.
7. **Post-incident**: lessons learned meeting, after-action report, update plans and detections.

Traps: pulling the plug on a running host loses memory evidence. Eradicating before scoping tips off the attacker and misses persistence. Recovering before eradication re-infects. Order of volatility for evidence: CPU registers/cache, RAM, network state and processes, disk, remote logs, backups.
@cards
Q: IR phase order?
A: Preparation, detection, analysis, containment, eradication, recovery, post-incident.
Q: Containment vs eradication?
A: Containment stops spread; eradication removes the cause.
Q: Why not just power off a compromised host?
A: You lose volatile evidence like memory, processes and connections.
Q: Most volatile evidence?
A: CPU registers/cache, then RAM.
Q: Which phase produces lessons learned?
A: Post-incident activity.
Q: Which phase is mostly done before any incident?
A: Preparation.
Q: What happens if you recover before eradication?
A: The attacker's persistence reinfects the restored system.
@check

a) Write the report
b) Containment: isolate affected hosts and shares
c) Pay the ransom
d) Reimage everything
= b | Stop spread before cleanup.

a) Detection
b) Recovery
c) Preparation
d) Analysis
= b | Restore operations once the threat is removed.

@answer

## 3.3 Given a scenario, implement incident response techniques
labs: incident-response, digital-forensics, log-analysis
@notes
### Prepare
- **IR plan** (policy, roles, severity definitions, escalation) and **communication plan** (who is told what, when, by whom, out-of-band channels in case email is compromised). **Playbooks** per scenario. **Roles**: incident commander, analysts, comms/legal/PR, system owners. **Training**: **tabletop** (talk through a scenario) and **simulation** (hands-on exercise).

### Investigate
- **Log collection**, **correlation** (join events across sources), **augmentation and enrichment** (add reputation, asset owner, user). **Alerts and notifications** feed **triage**: is it real, how urgent.
- Build the **timeline** (first malicious event to now) and determine **severity and impact** (data types, systems, users, business function). **Prioritize** by impact and spread.

### Evidence
- **Chain of custody**: who held the evidence, when, where, with signatures. **Integrity validation**: hash (SHA-256) before and after copying. **Preservation**: write blockers, forensic images, store securely. **Legal hold**: suspend deletion of data that might be needed in litigation.

### Act
- **Isolate** affected targets (EDR network contain, VLAN, disable port). **Escalate** per severity. **Remediate and verify** (patch, rebuild, rescan, monitor). **Release from isolation** only after verification. **Restore** from known-good backups.
- **Root cause analysis** asks how it got in and why defenses missed it; **corrective actions** assign owners and deadlines.
@cards

A: Tabletop is a discussion walk-through; simulation is hands-on practice.
Q: Purpose of chain of custody?
A: Document who handled evidence to prove it wasn't altered, for legal use.
Q: How do you prove a disk image is unchanged?
A: Compare cryptographic hashes of the original and the copy.
Q: What is a legal hold?
A: Orders retention of potentially relevant data, overriding normal deletion.
Q: Log enrichment example?
A: Adding IP reputation or the asset owner to a raw alert.
Q: Why use out-of-band comms in IR?
A: Primary channels like email may be compromised.
Q: What does RCA produce?
A: Understanding of the cause and corrective actions to prevent recurrence.
Q: When is a host released from isolation?
A: After remediation is verified and monitoring is in place.

## 4.2 Explain the importance of security operations and incident response reporting and communication
@notes
- **Declaration and escalation**: define who can declare an incident, on what criteria, and who gets notified at each severity.
- **Executive summary**: one page: what happened, impact, current status, decisions needed. No jargon.
- **Communication plan**: stakeholders include **legal** (privilege, breach laws), **PR** (public statements), **regulators** (GDPR 72 hours, HIPAA, SEC and others), **law enforcement**, **customers**, employees. One spokesperson, no speculation, no blame. Use secure channels and know your role.
- **Operational awareness**: choose channels (ticket, chat, phone, out-of-band) and keep sensitive details to the need-to-know group.
- **Post-incident**: **after-action report** (timeline, actions, what worked), **lessons learned** (meeting soon after, blameless), **RCA**.
- **Handover**: shift notes: open incidents, pending actions, key contacts, so nothing is dropped.
- **Internal threat intel report**: tailored to your environment and adversaries.
- **Metrics**: alert volume, **false-positive rate**, **true-positive rate**, **MTTD** (detect), **MTTR** (respond or remediate), mean time to close, **phishing click rate**.
