## 1.1 Laptop Hardware
labs: hardware-diagnostics-and-repair
@notes
### Laptop Hardware
- Laptops are compact and repair difficulty varies by maker; use the manufacturer's **service manual** for keyboard, display and other swaps. You get fast with whichever brand your employer buys most.
- **Battery**: lithium-ion or lithium-polymer. No memory effect, but each charge cycle wears capacity, so old batteries get replaced. Some are modular (slide and lock out), others sit inside the case. Batteries are **model-specific**.
- **Keyboard**: the most used part, usually attached by a **ribbon cable** and easy to replace. Small laptops drop the numeric keypad and double up keys with **Fn** functions (brightness, help). Keycaps and the mechanics under them are fragile, so follow the maker's removal steps. A USB external keyboard works around a bad internal one.
- **Memory**: **SO-DIMM** (small outline DIMM) in a slot behind a panel. Some laptops **solder** memory to the motherboard, so you can't upgrade it.
- **Storage**: older **2.5 inch HDD** (spinning, SATA); **2.5 inch SATA SSD** (no moving parts, same form factor, big speed gain); **M.2 SSD** (tiny card, one screw, no cable). Open the back or one access cover, unscrew, slide out, replace.
- **HDD to SSD migration**: either install the OS fresh and move documents and apps (slow), or **clone/image** the drive with imaging software (many SSD makers include a tool). A **drive-to-drive** clone copies sector by sector and needs both drives connected at once; one image file can build many machines.
- **Wireless cards**: built into newer boards; older laptops use **Mini PCI / Mini PCI Express** cards for 802.11 (WLAN), Bluetooth (WPAN) and cellular (WWAN). Cards connect to **antenna wires**.
- **Biometrics**: needs OS support plus hardware. **Windows Hello** face (camera) or fingerprint reader; use with a password for two factors.
- **NFC**: tap a phone or watch to authenticate (useful in shared environments like hospitals and warehouses).

A: HDD (spinning) or SATA SSD; both use SATA.

A: Clone or image the drive to the SSD.

## 1.2 Laptop Displays
labs: hardware-diagnostics-and-repair
@notes
### Laptop Displays
- **LCD**: liquid crystals plus color filters lit by a **backlight**. Light, low power, cheap. Weakness: hard to get true black; a failed backlight is part of the panel.
- **LCD types**: **TN** (twisted nematic: fast response, poor viewing angles with color inversion; gaming), **IPS** (in-plane switching: best color and viewing angles, costs more; design work), **VA** (vertical alignment: middle ground, good color, slower response).
- **OLED**: organic light-emitting diode. **No backlight**; each pixel emits light. Thin, light, great color, common on phones and tablets.
- **Backlights**: older **CCFL** (cold cathode fluorescent lamp, needs AC from an **inverter**) vs **LED** (edge-lit or matrix, thinner, lower power, so people call it an "LED display"). A **flashlight test**: shine a light at a dark screen; if the image faintly shows, the **backlight or inverter** failed, not the panel. A CCFL laptop often just needs a new inverter.
- **Wi-Fi antennas** run up the display bezel (it is the highest part). Replacing a display means rerouting and reconnecting the antenna wires. The **webcam and microphone** also sit in the lid, with wires through the hinge.
- **Digitizer**: layer that converts touch or stylus input to digital signals (touch only, or pen plus touch on tablets and 2-in-1s).
@cards
Q: TN vs IPS vs VA?
A: TN fast but poor angles; IPS best color and angles; VA in between, slower than TN.
Q: What is OLED's key difference?
A: No backlight; the organic pixels emit their own light.
Q: CCFL vs LED backlight?
A: CCFL is fluorescent and needs an inverter (AC); LED is thinner and lower power.
Q: What does the inverter do?
A: Converts DC to AC for CCFL backlights.
Q: How to check for a failed backlight?
A: Shine a flashlight on the screen; a faint image means the backlight/inverter has failed.
Q: Where do laptop Wi-Fi antennas run?
A: Through the display bezel/lid.
Q: What is a digitizer?
A: The layer converting touch or stylus input into digital data.
Q: Why does a display swap require care besides video cable?
A: Antenna and webcam wires also route through the lid.

## 1.3 Mobile Device Configuration

### Connecting Mobile Devices
- Phones and tablets connect for internet, email, backup, identification and sync.
- **USB**: type A (host), **mini-B** (old), **micro-B** (common), and **USB-C** (reversible; can carry DisplayPort, HDMI, Thunderbolt signals). **Lightning**: Apple's 8-pin reversible proprietary connector with higher charge power than micro-USB. A tech's bag holds all the cable types.
- **Serial**: **DB-9 (DE-9)** with **RS-232** was the pre-USB standard; still used for console access to switches and routers. Use a **USB-to-DB9 adapter** on laptops.
- **NFC** (near-field communication): tiny data over very short range for payments, transfers and badges.
- **Bluetooth**: wireless audio and accessories (earbuds, headsets, car audio).
- **Wi-Fi hotspot**: phone shares its cellular connection over 802.11; the carrier may charge extra or limit it.

### Mobile Device Accessories
- **Touch pen / capacitive stylus**: works on any capacitive screen, replaces the finger. **Active stylus** (digital pen): talks to the device, senses **pressure**, buttons, usually tied to certain devices (Apple Pencil for iPad). **Drawing pad**: external digitizer plus active stylus for computers with no touch.
- **Trackpad**: built in on laptops; external ones are Bluetooth and support multi-finger gestures. Many laptops allow a Fn key to **disable the trackpad** to stop accidental touches.
- **Headsets**: USB, **3.5 mm TRRS** (tip-ring-ring-sleeve: left, right, mic, ground), Lightning, or Bluetooth.
- **Speakers**: battery Bluetooth speakers for better sound. **Webcam**: built-in cameras for video calls.
- **Docking station**: stays on the desk, laptop drops in; connects monitors, keyboard, printer, power; often a proprietary connector and may hold expansion cards. **Port replicator**: smaller, connects by USB (or similar), duplicates ports, no cards.

## 1.4 Mobile Device Connectivity
labs: hardware-diagnostics-and-repair
@notes
### Cellular Standards
- **Cellular** = coverage split into cells with antennas at the edges. 
- **2G**: **GSM** (about 90 percent of the world; AT&T and T-Mobile in the US; uses **SIM card**, time-division multiplexing) and **CDMA** (each call coded; Verizon and Sprint; carrier controls handsets). Both started circuit-switched voice and needed upgrades for data.
- **3G** (1998 onward): several Mb/s, enabled GPS, streaming, mobile TV.
- **4G LTE** (Long Term Evolution): one converged standard, up to about **150 Mb/s**; **LTE-A** (advanced) about **300 Mb/s**.
- **5G** (2020 onward): speeds from 100 to 900 Mb/s in practice, with a goal of about **10 Gb/s**; helps IoT with many devices and large uploads.
- **PRL** (preferred roaming list) tells a phone which towers to use; updated **OTA** (over the air) with the OS.
- **Hotspot** note: carrier plans may limit or charge for tethering.

### Mobile Device Connectivity
- **Bluetooth pairing**: enable Bluetooth on both, set the accessory to **discoverable**, pick it from the list, confirm or enter the **PIN**, test. Pair once; they reconnect automatically.
- **GPS**: over 30 satellites; a phone needs signals from at least **four** to compute latitude, longitude and altitude via timing differences. Phones also use Wi-Fi and cell towers to refine location.
- **MDM (mobile device management)**: central console for company and **BYOD** phones: enforce screen lock and PIN, require biometrics or 2FA, push email and apps, block forbidden apps, disable the camera, **partition** work from personal data, and **remote wipe** only company data.

### Mobile Device Configurations
- Phone numbers and texts work out of the box; email and cloud sync usually need setup.
- **Microsoft 365 / Exchange / Outlook.com**: sign in to sync mail, contacts, calendar, reminders and notes.
- **iCloud**: Apple devices; choose what to sync (mail, photos, contacts, calendar, backups); set whether it may use cellular or only Wi-Fi. Signing in on a new phone restores data.

## 2.1 Ports and Protocols
labs: networking-tcp-ip-dhcp-nat-subnets, computer-networks
@notes
### Introduction to IP
- **Encapsulation**: application data goes in a TCP or UDP segment, inside an IP packet, inside an Ethernet frame (header, payload, trailer). IP is the delivery truck: it moves a box between **IP addresses** and doesn't care what's inside. TCP and UDP sit at **OSI layer 4 (transport)** and add **multiplexing** with port numbers.
- **TCP** (transmission control protocol): **connection-oriented**, formal setup and teardown, **acknowledged "reliable" delivery**, reorders out-of-order data, **retransmits** lost data, **flow control**. Used by HTTPS and SSH.
- **UDP** (user datagram protocol): **connectionless**, no acknowledgment, no flow control. Fast, used for real-time traffic (voice, video) and short exchanges like **DHCP** and **TFTP**; the application handles any resends.
- **Ports**: the "room" in the house, 0 to 65,535, separate sets for TCP and UDP. **Well-known (non-ephemeral)** server ports are fixed (0 to 1023, for example 80 and 443). The client picks a random **ephemeral** source port (OS-assigned, typically 1,024 to 65,535) per conversation. A flow = server IP + protocol + server port + client IP + client port. Changing a port is not security; scanners find open ports.

### Common Network Ports
- **FTP** TCP **20** (data) and **21** (control): file transfer, login (or anonymous), list/add/delete/rename.
- **SSH** TCP **22** (encrypted terminal); **Telnet** TCP **23** (clear text, avoid).
- **SMTP** TCP **25**: server to server and client **sending** mail. **POP3** TCP **110**: download mail to one client. **IMAP4** TCP **143**: manage a mailbox synced across devices.
- **DNS** UDP/TCP **53**: names to IPs (UDP in normal queries).
- **DHCP** UDP **67** (server) and **68** (client): lease pool; **reservations** give fixed IPs.
- **HTTP** TCP **80**; **HTTPS** TCP **443**.
- **SMB/CIFS** (Windows file and print): **TCP 445** direct; older **NetBIOS over TCP/IP**: UDP **137** name service, TCP **139** sessions (also UDP 138 datagram).
- **SNMP** UDP **161** (queries), UDP **162** (**traps**, alerts to the manager). v1 and v2 clear text; **v3** adds authentication, integrity, encryption.
- **LDAP** TCP **389**: directory queries (Active Directory).
- **RDP** TCP **3389**: Windows remote desktop (whole desktop or a single app).

## 2.2 Network Devices
labs: computer-networks, networking-tcp-ip-dhcp-nat-subnets
@notes
### Network Devices
- **Router**: forwards between IP subnets using the destination **IP address** (layer 3); can join LAN, WAN, copper and fiber. A **layer 3 (multilayer) switch** adds routing to a switch. SOHO devices combine router, switch, AP, firewall.
- **Switch**: forwards by destination **MAC address** (layer 2), in hardware (**ASIC**) at high speed; can supply **PoE**. **Unmanaged**: plug and play, no VLANs, no SNMP, cheap, home use. **Managed**: **VLANs**, QoS (prioritize voice), **STP** to prevent loops, **port mirroring**, SNMP monitoring.
- **Access point (AP)**: bridges wireless to wired by MAC (not a router, no NAT).
- **Patch panel**: permanent cable runs from desks are punched down at the back; short patch cables on the front connect to switch ports, so moves and changes only touch the front.
- **Firewall**: allows or blocks by IP and port (layer 4); **next-generation** firewalls understand applications (layer 7). May terminate **VPN** tunnels, act as a **proxy**, and route (layer 3).
- **PoE**: power on the Ethernet cable for APs, cameras, phones. **Endspan** = PoE switch; **midspan** = injector with a non-PoE switch. Standards: **802.3af** 15.4 W (350 mA), **802.3at (PoE+)** 25.5 W (600 mA), **802.3bt (PoE++)** type 3 51 W, type 4 71.3 W (960 mA).
- **Hub**: multiport repeater; copies every frame to all ports, **half duplex**, gets slower with load; only 10/100 Mb/s, obsolete.
- **Cable modem**: **DOCSIS** over coax (broadband, many frequencies: TV, phone, data), up to about 1 Gb/s. **DSL modem** (ADSL: **asymmetric**, faster down than up) over phone lines; about **10,000 ft** from the central office (CO) limit; closer = faster.
- **ONT** (optical network terminal): converts ISP **fiber** to Ethernet/phone/coax at the premises. The **demarc** is the demarcation point where the ISP's responsibility ends and yours begins.
- **NIC**: network interface card (wired, fiber, wireless; onboard or expansion).

### Software Defined Networking
- **SDN** turns routers, switches and firewalls into software with three planes: **infrastructure/data plane** (forwarding, trunking, encryption, NAT), **control plane** (routing protocols, forwarding and NAT tables), **management/application plane** (SSH, GUI, SNMP, **API**). Modular and cloud friendly.

## 2.3 Wireless Networks
labs: computer-networks
@notes
### Wireless Network Standards
- **802.11** (IEEE) = **Wi-Fi** (Wi-Fi Alliance trademark and interoperability testing). Higher frequency means less range (more absorption); 2.4 GHz travels farther but is crowded (baby monitors, cordless phones, Bluetooth).
- Table (frequency, max rate):
  - **802.11a** (1999): 5 GHz, 54 Mb/s.
  - **802.11b** (1999): 2.4 GHz, 11 Mb/s.
  - **802.11g** (2003): 2.4 GHz, 54 Mb/s, backward compatible with b.
  - **802.11n = Wi-Fi 4** (2009): 2.4 and 5 GHz, up to 4 **MIMO** streams, 40 MHz channels, 150 Mb/s per stream, **600 Mb/s** total.
  - **802.11ac = Wi-Fi 5** (2014): **5 GHz only**, 160 MHz channels, **MU-MIMO** (multi-user), 8 streams at 867 Mb/s, about **6.9 Gb/s**. (Dual-band "ac" APs use "n" on 2.4 GHz.)
  - **802.11ax = Wi-Fi 6** (2021): 2.4 and 5 GHz, 20/40/80/160 MHz, uplink and downlink MU-MIMO, **OFDMA** (efficient in crowds: stadiums, trade shows), about 1.2 Gb/s per stream, **9.6 Gb/s** total.
- Typical AP range 40 to 50 m. **Long-range fixed wireless**: directional antennas (such as **Yagi**) for point-to-point between buildings; follow regulations (frequencies, power, indoor vs outdoor), outdoor safety and lightning protection; consider a pro installer.
- **RFID**: tags powered by the reader's signal (passive, no battery) or battery (active). Badges, inventory, pet chips. **NFC**: short-range two-way, based on RFID; contactless payment, pairing, phone as access card.

### Wireless Network Technologies
- Regulators set allowed frequencies, power and interference limits per country.
- **2.4 GHz**: only three non-overlapping 20 MHz channels, **1, 6, 11** (2412 to 2482 MHz). **5 GHz**: many more channels; bond into 40, 80 or **160 MHz** for speed.
- **Bluetooth** uses the unlicensed **ISM** (industrial, scientific, medical) part of 2.4 GHz; a **PAN** with about **10 m** range for consumer devices (class 1 industrial over 100 m).

## 2.4 Network Services
labs: networking-tcp-ip-dhcp-nat-subnets, windows-admin-powershell
@notes
### Network Services
- **DNS server**: converts FQDNs to IPs; distributed system, run by IT or an ISP, always in redundant sets.
- **DHCP server**: assigns IP, mask, gateway, DNS. SOHO routers and cable modems include one; enterprises run redundant ones. Home example: 1-week lease, pool 10.1.10.10 to .199.
- **File server**: central folders reachable from any device. **SMB** (Windows) or **AFP** (Apple); users just see a file manager.
- **Print server**: hardware (built-in or add-in card, wireless) or software on a PC that shares printers. Protocols: **SMB**, **IPP** (internet printing protocol), **LPD** (line printer daemon).
- **Mail server**: needs near 100 percent uptime and 24x7 support; run in-house, by an ISP or in the cloud.
- **Syslog / SIEM**: syslog sends logs to a central database; a **SIEM** (security information and event manager) collects and correlates them with big storage.
- **Web server**: answers HTTP/HTTPS requests with static or dynamic HTML pages.
- **Authentication server** (AAA/RADIUS/LDAP, Active Directory): one credential store for all services; critical, so redundant.
- **Spam gateway**: a mail gateway in the screened subnet (or cloud) scans inbound mail to tag or reject spam and phishing before the internal mail server.
- **UTM (unified threat management)** or next-gen firewall: URL filter, content inspection, malware and spam filtering, CSU/DSU, routing and switching, firewall, **IDS/IPS**, bandwidth shaping (QoS) and VPN in one box.
- **Load balancer**: spreads traffic over multiple servers, removes dead ones, and can do **TCP offload**, **SSL offload**, caching and **content switching** (route apps or pages to specific servers).
- **Proxy server**: users ask the proxy, it fetches and inspects the response; adds access control (login), caching, URL filtering.
- **SCADA / ICS**: supervises industrial machines (power, plants); expensive, high impact, so **segmented** from the main network.
- **Legacy systems**: old software or hardware still doing important work; hard to support or replace.
- **Embedded systems**: purpose-built devices (alarms, time clocks, door controllers); no OS access, rely on the vendor for patches.
- **IoT**: smart appliances, speakers, thermostats, doorbells; weak security, so put them on their **own segment**.

## 2.6 Network Connections
labs: networking-tcp-ip-dhcp-nat-subnets, computer-networks
@notes
### DNS Configuration
- DNS is a **hierarchical, distributed database**: root (13 clusters, over 1,000 servers), TLDs (.com, .org, country codes), domains, hosts. A full name is the FQDN (`www.example.com`). Check with `dig example.com` or `nslookup example.com`; several A records give redundancy.
- **Resource records** (30+ types). Simple text zone file or web GUI; back up before editing since DNS is critical.
  - **SOA** start of authority. **A** name to IPv4. **AAAA** name to IPv6. **CNAME** alias to another name. **MX** mail exchanger (points to a name that needs an A record). **TXT** free text (public).
  - **TTL**: how long clients cache the answer (such as 15 minutes).
- **TXT records for email security**: **SPF** lists servers allowed to send for your domain; **DKIM** publishes the public key that verifies digital signatures on your mail; **DMARC** says what receivers should do when SPF/DKIM fail (accept, quarantine/spam, reject) and where to send reports. TXT is also used for domain verification.
- `dig example.com TXT` or `nslookup -type=txt example.com` lists them.

### DHCP Configuration
- Server settings: **IP range (scope/pool)** and mask, **lease duration**, **DNS**, **gateway**, extra options (VoIP). **Exclusions** carve addresses out of a range; **reservations** bind a **MAC** to an address (static DHCP).
- Windows Server DHCP shows scope, address pool, address leases, reservations, scope options. SOHO router example: 10.10.10.2 to .100, lease 86,400 s (24 h), gateway 10.10.10.1.
- **Assignment types**: **dynamic** (any free address; returned to the pool after the lease), **automatic** (remembers previous clients and tries to give the same address), **reservation** (fixed per MAC, never given to anyone else).
- **Lease and timers**: renewed on reboot; can **release** manually. **T1 = 50 percent** of the lease (renew with the server), **T2 = 87.5 percent (7/8)** (rebind with any server). 8-day lease: T1 at 4 days, T2 at 7 days.

### VLANs and VPNs
- **LAN** = one broadcast domain. Separate switches per network waste money, power and management. **VLANs** logically split one switch into several broadcast domains (port 1 = VLAN 1, port 9 = VLAN 2). VLANs cannot talk to each other without a router. Benefits: security, fewer broadcasts, cost.
- **VPN**: encrypted tunnel across a public network to a **concentrator** (hardware, firewall or server software). Always-on or enabled by the user; protects traffic even on open coffee-shop Wi-Fi. The concentrator decrypts into the corporate network and encrypts replies.

## 3.1 Cables and Adapters
labs: hardware-diagnostics-and-repair
@notes
### Network Cables
- **Twisted pair** Ethernet cable: 4 pairs (blue, orange, green, brown, each solid and white-striped). Pairs carry equal and opposite signals; twisting and comparing at the receiver cancels interference, and each pair has a different twist rate to limit crosstalk.
- **Categories**: 1000BASE-T needs minimum **Cat 5** (Cat 5e = enhanced, tested more, now what you buy) to 100 m; 10GBASE-T uses **Cat 6** (shorter) or **Cat 6A** (augmented) to 100 m.
- **Coax**: center conductor, insulator, metal shield, jacket. **RG6** for cable TV and cable modems.
- **Plenum**: air-return space above ceilings. Cables there need **plenum-rated** jackets (**FEP**/low-smoke PVC) so a fire doesn't spread toxic smoke; normal jackets are PVC. Plenum cable is stiffer.
- **UTP** unshielded (typical) vs **STP** shielded (+ drain/ground wire). Jacket codes: **U** = unshielded, **S** = braided shield, **F** = foil: for example **F/UTP** (foil overall, no pair shields), **S/FTP** (braid overall, foil on pairs).
- **Direct burial** cable: waterproof, often gel-filled, shielded, with a drain wire; can go in the ground without conduit.

### Optical Fiber
- Light signals: no RF emission (hard to tap), immune to EMI, much longer distance than copper. Parts: **core**, **cladding** (lower refractive index keeps light in the core), **buffer coating**, **ferrule** at the connector.
- **Multimode**: wide core, many reflections, **LED** source, cheaper, up to about **2 km**. **Single-mode**: narrow core, one path, **laser**, up to about **100 km** without regeneration.

### 568A and 568B Colors
- **TIA-568** standard (ISO/IEC 11801 internationally); **T568A / T568B** define wire colors per pin of the **8P8C (RJ45)** connector. Pins 1,2,3,6 differ: **T568B pin 1 = white/orange, 2 = orange, 3 = white/green, 6 = green**; T568A swaps orange and green. Pins 4/5 (blue pair) and 7/8 (brown pair) are the same. Both work the same; use one consistently, both ends. (A **crossover** cable is defined by the IEEE pin-out, not by "A on one end, B on the other".)

### Video Cables
- **VGA**: 15-pin **DB-15 (DE-15)**, **blue**, **analog**, video only, degrades past 5 to 10 m.
- **HDMI**: digital, video **and audio**, 19-pin type A, about 20 m before dropouts.
- **DisplayPort**: digital packetized audio and video, locking latch, mini version; passively compatible with HDMI via a DP-to-HDMI cable.
- **DVI**: video only. **DVI-A** analog, **DVI-D** digital, **DVI-I** integrated (both). **Single link** (about 3.7 Gb/s) vs **dual link** (double).

### SATA Device Cables
- **SATA** (serial AT attachment): rev 1.0 1.5 Gb/s, 2.0 3 Gb/s, 3.0 **6 Gb/s**, 3.2 16 Gb/s; 1 m cable. **eSATA**: external, up to 2 m, different connector. Data = **7-pin** L-shaped; power = **15-pin** (or older **Molex**). One cable = one drive (point to point).

### SCSI Device Cables
- **SCSI** (small computer systems interface): daisy chain of 8 (narrow) or 16 (wide) devices; each has a **SCSI ID**; **LUN** names a drive inside an array; a **terminator** at the chain end. Parallel versions (such as a 68-pin Ultra 3), then **SAS (serial attached SCSI)**: point to point, no IDs or terminators, looks like SATA but uses different connectors. **iSCSI** = SCSI over IP (heavy use in virtual and storage networks).

### PATA Device Cables
- **PATA** (parallel ATA, also IDE/EIDE, ATA): 40-pin ribbon, speeds 16 to **133 MB/s**; 40-wire cable (original) and **80-wire** cable (same 40 pins, extra ground wires cut crosstalk). Keyed with a notch and a missing pin. Device 0 and 1 (master/slave). Legacy.

### Adapters and Converters
- **Electrically compatible** pairs need only a passive adapter: **DVI-D to HDMI**; **DVI-A to VGA** (640 x 480 limit). Analog VGA to digital DVI-D needs an active **converter**. **USB to Ethernet**, USB-C to USB-A, and multiport **USB hubs/docks** cover missing ports.

### Copper Connectors
- **RJ11**: 6 position, 2 conductor (phones, DSL port). **RJ45**: 8P8C (Ethernet). **F connector**: threaded coax (cable TV, DOCSIS). **Punch down block** (wires punched, RJ45 on other side). **USB**: A, B (printers), mini-B, micro-B, USB 3.0 (extra pins, blue), **USB-C** (reversible, small; carries USB, Thunderbolt, DisplayPort). **Molex**: 4-pin 12 V/5 V power for old drives and fans. **Lightning** (Apple 8-pin). **DB-9** (RS-232 console).

### Fiber Connectors
- **LC** (local; small, duplex transmit and receive), **ST** (straight tip; bayonet quarter-turn), **SC** (subscriber/square; push-pull).

## 3.2 Memory
labs: hardware-diagnostics-and-repair
@notes
### An Overview of Memory
- **RAM** = fast, temporary working storage for loaded programs and documents (not disk space). Check the motherboard manual for the exact type.
- **DIMM** (dual in-line memory module): 64-bit data width, clips hold it in; contacts on each side differ. **SO-DIMM**: about half the width, for laptops and small devices.
- **DRAM** (dynamic RAM) must be constantly **refreshed** and needs power; **random access** means any address can be read directly; **synchronous** (SDRAM) means a system clock paces transfers.
- **Notches (keys)** prevent installing the wrong generation; no backward compatibility.
- **SDR** moves one transfer per clock; **DDR** (double data rate) moves two. **DDR3**: twice DDR2 speed, up to 16 GB per module. **DDR4**: faster, up to 64 GB per module. **DDR5**: faster again, 64 GB per module (key moved).

### Memory Technologies
- **Virtual memory** (swap/page file, virtual RAM): drive space used as overflow RAM; slower. Windows can auto-manage the **paging file** (Performance Options > Advanced) or you set the size.
- **Multichannel**: dual, triple, quad channel spreads load across matched modules in matching (often color-coded) slots for more throughput.
- **Parity memory**: extra bit per byte; **detects** some errors, cannot correct. **ECC** (error-correcting code) memory **detects and corrects**; used in servers, VMs and databases.
- **Even parity**: choose the parity bit so the total number of 1s is even. Data 11100111 (six 1s) -> parity 0; 00000010 (one 1) -> parity 1; 10011000 (three 1s) -> parity 1. To check: count all 1s including the parity bit; odd means an error.
@cards
Q: What is DRAM?
A: Dynamic RAM that must be constantly refreshed and loses data without power.
Q: Why "synchronous"?
A: A clock paces memory transfers.
Q: DIMM data width?
A: 64 bits.
Q: DIMM vs SO-DIMM?

## 3.3 Storage Devices
labs: hardware-diagnostics-and-repair, secure-data-sanitization
@notes
### Storage Devices
- **HDD**: spinning magnetic **platters**, **spindle**, **actuator arm** and **heads** (both sides of each platter). **Non-volatile**, random access, but mechanical, so it is slow and will eventually fail. Speed ≈ RPM: **5,400 RPM** about 5.5 ms rotational latency; **15,000 RPM** about 2 ms. Sizes: **3.5 inch** (desktop), **2.5 inch** (laptop and desktop), M.2 (small).
- **SSD**: no moving parts, non-volatile, far faster. Interfaces: **SATA** SSD in 2.5 inch size (same connector as HDD); **mSATA** (mini-SATA, small card, now rare); **M.2** (small card). SATA uses the **AHCI** protocol and tops out near **600 MB/s** (SATA III), a bottleneck for SSDs.
- **NVMe** (non-volatile memory express): protocol for SSDs over **PCIe** (x4 lanes ~ 4 GB/s), lower latency and higher throughput. Uses an **M.2** slot; the slot may support only AHCI. **M.2 keys** (B key, M key, or B+M): match the SSD to the motherboard slot and check NVMe support. Install: slide in, one screw, no cables or power connector.
- **Flash memory** (EEPROM): non-volatile, limited write cycles, small and easy to lose, not archival. **USB flash drive, SD, microSD**, legacy **CompactFlash** and **xD**.
- **Optical**: laser reads pits; CD-ROM, DVD-ROM, **Blu-ray**; used for archive media; rarer on new systems; internal or USB external.

### RAID
- **RAID** = redundant array of independent disks: uptime/availability, **not a backup**.
- **RAID 0 striping**: 2+ disks, data split across them; faster, **no redundancy** (lose a disk, lose everything).
- **RAID 1 mirroring**: 2 disks with identical data; survives one failure; uses 2x the space.
- **RAID 5 striping with parity**: min 3 disks; parity distributed across drives; survives **one** drive failure (rebuild uses CPU, slower while degraded); usable space = n-1 drives.
- **RAID 10 (1+0)**: striped mirrors, min 4 disks; fast and redundant; survives one drive per mirror pair.
- Watch for failure alerts; replace failed drives promptly.

## 3.4 Motherboards
labs: hardware-diagnostics-and-repair, windows-deployment-and-drivers
@notes
### Motherboard Form Factors
- The motherboard holds CPU, RAM, expansion slots, connectors. Choose by case size, expansion needs and airflow. **ATX** (1995): standard desktop board, plentiful slots and RAM, 20/24-pin power. **ITX family** (VIA, 2001, **Mini-ITX**): small, same mounting holes as ATX so it fits ATX cases; for media centers and small-form-factor PCs, thin clients.

### Motherboard Expansion Slots
- A **bus** is the communication path between components. **PCI** (1994): parallel; 32-bit or 64-bit; 3.3 V or 5 V keyed; legacy. **PCIe** (PCI Express): **serial**, point-to-point **lanes** (x1, x2, x4, x8, x16, x32, "by"), each lane full duplex; more lanes = more bandwidth. A hook locks the card; fasten the bracket screw.

### Motherboard Connectors
- **Main power**: originally 20 pins, now **24-pin** (+3.3, +5, +12 V); keyed and latched; unplug mains first. **4-pin (or 8-pin) ATX 12 V** near the CPU for CPU power (labels: CPU, P4, ATX12V).
- **SATA** (storage), **eSATA** (external, via card or onboard), **M.2** (SSD, screw).
- **Pin headers**: front panel buttons and LEDs (power, reset, HDD light), front USB 2/3, front audio (AAFP), **TPM** header, fan headers.

### Motherboard Compatibility
- **Intel vs AMD**: different CPUs and sockets; you can't swap; cost vs performance changes with time. Lift the retention cover, place the CPU, close.
- **Server** boards: multiple CPU sockets, many RAM slots, rack-mount fit. **Desktop**: one CPU, 2 to 4 RAM slots, a few slots. **Small form factor**: minimal. **Laptop**: proprietary, designed for power efficiency, CPU **throttles** when hot, mostly not upgradeable; replace with the exact board.

### The BIOS
- **BIOS** (basic input output system) is firmware (now flash memory) that initializes CPU and RAM, runs **POST** (checks CPU, RAM, video, input, not a full diagnostic), finds the boot drive and boot loader. Some boards have a **backup BIOS** for failed upgrades.
- **Legacy BIOS**: text menus, basic config, no drivers or extensions. **UEFI** (unified extensible firmware interface, from Intel's EFI): standardized, graphical, **supports UEFI drivers**; tabs for main/system info, security (passwords), advanced (boot options), UEFI drivers.
### BIOS Settings
- **Enter setup** at boot with the vendor key (Delete, F2, F10, F12 or similar). VMs: Hyper-V and VMware Workstation Player expose BIOS; VirtualBox does not (use a UEFI simulator to practice).
- **Windows fast startup** (hibernate-style shutdown) skips the key prompt. Bypass: hold **Shift** while clicking Restart, use **Settings > Update & Security > Recovery > Advanced startup > Restart now**, msconfig, or interrupt boot three times.
- **Back up settings before changes**: export if offered, write them down, or photograph the screen.
- **Hardware control**: disable devices in BIOS so the OS can't see them. **Boot order**: for example USB, then SSD, then HDD, plus network and optical; enable or disable boot sources.
- **Disable USB ports** in high-security places (the 2008 DoD ban followed a worm on a USB stick).
- **Fan control**: intelligent cooling profiles (performance vs quiet); fans must be on motherboard fan headers.
- **Secure boot** (UEFI): checks digital signatures of the boot loader against keys stored in the firmware; blocks tampered boot code. OS must be signed; disable it only if the OS can't support it.
- **Passwords**: **user password** (required to start), **supervisor/administrator password** (needed to change settings); stored in firmware flash, not the OS. Forgot it? Reset the BIOS by shorting the **CLRTC / clear CMOS jumper** (power off, short pins, then power on). The older term **CMOS** persists.
- **CMOS battery (CR2032, 3 V)**: only keeps date and time on modern boards; a dead battery resets the clock at each boot.
- **TPM** (trusted platform module): hardware chip with its own processor and persistent storage for cryptographic keys; used by full-disk encryption (BitLocker); hardened against brute force; enable/disable in BIOS. **HSM** (hardware security module): appliance or card for managing many keys, with crypto acceleration; smart-card/USB versions for personal keys.

### CPU Features
- **32-bit** CPUs address 2^32 values (4 GB RAM); **64-bit** addresses vastly more (OS-limited). Drivers must match the OS type. **x86** = 32-bit, **x64** = 64-bit. A 64-bit OS runs 32-bit and 64-bit apps; a 32-bit OS runs only 32-bit. Windows installs 32-bit apps in **Program Files (x86)**.
- **ARM**: efficient, low power and heat, mobile and IoT, spreading to laptops.
- **Multi-core** (dual, quad ...): several cores plus caches and often a memory controller and **integrated GPU** in one package. **Hyper-Threading (HTT)** / SMT: one core appears as two, gaining about **15 to 30 percent**; needs CPU and OS support.
- **Virtualization extensions**: **Intel VT** and **AMD-V**; enable in BIOS if your hypervisor needs them.

### Expansion Cards
- Install: power off, open case, align key and slot, press down, screw in; then install the **driver** (current one from the vendor, Device Manager or an installer; some require uninstalling the old driver first).
- **Sound card**: better audio in/out (1/4 inch, line-in, S/PDIF digital). **Video card**: **discrete GPU** vs integrated graphics; plug the monitor into the card, not the board. **Capture card**: video input (HDMI, **SDI** over coax with BNC). **NIC**: add or multi-port Ethernet. Before buying, check slots, OS requirements, vendor compatibility lists and forums.

### Cooling
- **Airflow**: cool in at the front, warm out at the back; cable management matters. Case, card and CPU **fans**; board **fan controller**; quieter fans possible. Some systems are **passively cooled** (silent, small).
- **Heat sink**: copper or aluminum fins; hot after use. **Thermal paste/grease**: pea-sized dot between CPU and heat sink for good contact. **Thermal pad**: solid, cleaner, slightly less effective, single use (replace each time). Heat sink plus fan on top is common.
- **Liquid cooling**: CPU block, tubes, **radiator** with fans, pump; for gaming, graphics and overclocking.

## 3.5 Power
labs: hardware-diagnostics-and-repair
@notes
### Computer Power
- **Safety**: always unplug before working inside; capacitors can hold charge even when unplugged (follow the maker's discharge steps); don't connect yourself to the electrical ground system; respect electricity.
- **AC** (wall; alternates; US/Canada 110 to 120 V at 60 Hz; Europe 220 to 240 V at 50 Hz) vs **DC** (one direction; what components use). The **power supply (PSU)** converts AC to DC (+3.3, +5, +12 V). Nothing works without it.
- **Units**: **amp** (current, rate of electrons), **volt** (force), **watt = volts x amps** (120 V x 0.5 A = 60 W). Multimeter measures voltage.
- **Voltage switch**: manual 115/230 V selector on some PSUs (wrong setting means sparks and smoke); **auto-switching** PSUs accept 100 to 240 V, 47 to 63 Hz.
- **DC rails**: **+12 V** (CPU, PCIe cards, drive motors, fans), **+5 V** (legacy), **+3.3 V** (modern boards, M.2, RAM), **+5 V SB (standby)** (keeps the system able to wake), **-12 V** (old serial and LAN), **-5 V** (legacy, mostly gone). Positive/negative is relative to ground.
- **Main connector**: 24-pin (or 20-pin older); keyed and latched; a 24-pin fits a 20-pin board (4 pins unused). 4/8-pin for CPU.
- **Redundant PSUs** in servers: two units each able to carry 100 percent; one fails, the other takes over; **hot-swappable**.
- **Modular PSUs**: fixed, semi-modular or fully modular cables; fewer cables, better airflow.
- **Sizing**: add up wattage for CPU, GPU, drives, etc. (GPU documentation recommends a PSU wattage), then about **double** it as headroom; extra capacity does not make the PC faster. Form factors are standardized (ATX), so a PSU from any maker fits.

## 3.7 Printers
labs: hardware-diagnostics-and-repair, help-desk-ticketing-osticket
@notes
### Laser Printers
- Laser = high voltage + powdered toner + heat + pressure. Fast, high quality.
- **Imaging drum** (photosensitive/**OPC** drum; sometimes inside the toner cartridge): laser draws the image, toner clings, transfers to paper. **Fuser assembly**: heat and pressure melt toner onto the page (pages come out warm; very hot parts). **Transfer belt and roller** (color): CMYK toner (cyan, magenta, yellow, key/black) moves to the belt then the paper. **Pickup roller** grabs one page from the tray; **separation pad** stops multiples. Bad pickup roller = no feed or multiple pages. **Duplexing assembly** flips the page.
- **Seven-step laser process**: **1 Processing** (RIP; page rendered in memory), **2 Charging** (corona wire or charge roller gives the drum a **negative** charge), **3 Exposing** (laser discharges/writes the image, making it **positive** where it hits), **4 Developing** (negative toner sticks to the exposed areas), **5 Transferring** (toner moves to the paper), **6 Fusing** (heat and pressure), **7 Cleaning** (remove leftover toner from the drum). Steps 2 to 7 can repeat per page.

### Laser Printer Maintenance
- Replace the **toner cartridge** (often includes the **OPC drum**): power off, remove packing strips, **keep light off the drum** (leave the cover), install. Low-toner warnings let you keep printing for a while.
- **Maintenance kit** (feed rollers, fuser, etc.) by page-count; there is a **page counter** to reset afterward. Power off and unplug first; let the fuser cool.
- **Calibrate** after changing toner (density, auto or from test page).
- **Cleaning**: cold water, damp cloth, or **isopropyl alcohol (IPA)** on rubber rollers; no harsh cleaners. Use a **toner vacuum**, not a household vacuum. Wash toner off skin with **cold** water (warm melts it).

### Inkjet Printers
- Inexpensive, quiet, color; ink is costly, proprietary, fades and clogs. **CMYK** cartridges; the cartridge may integrate the **print head**. **Carriage** with a **belt** moves across the page; **feed rollers** pull paper; **duplexer** optional. **Calibration** aligns the colors (print a page and adjust).

### Inkjet Printer Maintenance
- **Streaks** mean clogged print heads: run the **cleaning cycle** or clean the head by hand. Replace cartridges (single K black plus CMY tri-color, or one per color); recycle them; then calibrate. **Paper jam**: remove the tray, pull paper along its path gently, release tension if offered, check for torn pieces.

### Thermal Printers
- Heats **thermochromic (thermal) paper**, no ink or toner. Quiet; **receipts**, labels. Paper darkens with heat and fades; clear tape can whiten it; not for archives. **Feed assembly** (roller cylinder with gears) moves the paper past a fixed **heating element**.

### Thermal Printer Maintenance
- Use the **exact paper** type and width; replace the roll. **Missing print** = dirty heating element: wipe with **IPA** (pens or cleaning cards). Blow out dust with compressed air or damp cloth; avoid ordinary vacuums (static). Keep paper away from heat sources.

### Impact Printers
- **Dot-matrix**: a print head with pins (such as 24) hits an **ink ribbon** against the paper; makes **carbon copies** and multi-part forms; low cost per page; **loud**; low resolution. **Tractor feed** (pin-fed) continuous perforated paper; **green bar** paper for mainframes.

### Impact Printer Maintenance
- Ribbon fades as ink is used: replace the cartridge (model-specific). The **print head** wears out; it is hot (heat sinks): let it cool, release lever, replace, usually replace the ribbon at the same time. Load tractor paper straight, holes on the pins, nothing blocking the feed, then press feed.

### 3D Printers
- **Additive manufacturing**: build layers from an electronic model. **FDM / filament printing**: melts filament in layers; common, large bed, low mess. **SLA / resin printing**: UV light or laser cures liquid **resin** layer by layer on a build platform; smoother detail but resin needs **protective gear** and **hazardous waste disposal**. **Print bed**: flat, level, clean, sticky enough to hold the object still.

## 4.1 Cloud Computing
labs: virtualization-proxmox
@notes
### Cloud Models
- The cloud lets you deploy a whole application (web, database, app servers) in one click, scale instances up and down, place them near users worldwide and pay for what you use.
- **Deployment models**: **private** (your own data center, one org), **public** (AWS, Microsoft, Rackspace; anyone), **hybrid** (private + public combined), **community** (several similar organizations share the cost of a private-style cloud).
- **Service models** (who manages what):
  - **IaaS** infrastructure as a service (also "HaaS"): provider gives CPU, storage, network and virtualization (sometimes the OS); you manage OS, middleware, runtime, data, apps. Example: rent a server and build the site.
  - **PaaS** platform as a service: provider gives a platform/building blocks (modules); you build the app and own data. Example: Salesforce platform.
  - **SaaS** software as a service: provider runs everything; you just log in. Examples: Gmail, Microsoft 365.
  - **On-premises**: you manage everything. Responsibility drops from you to the provider along the on-prem > IaaS > PaaS > SaaS line.

### Cloud Characteristics
- **Internal cloud**: own data center and racks, big up-front cost, no usage billing. **External cloud**: shared provider resources in multiple data centers.
- **Metered** (pay-as-you-go: storage, transfers, compute) vs **non-metered** (flat fee, such as Dropbox or Google Drive plans).
- **Rapid elasticity**: add or remove capacity quickly and invisibly. **High availability**: redundancy (generators, multiple network lines). **File synchronization** keeps data consistent across regions.
- **DaaS** (desktop as a service, such as AWS WorkSpaces) delivers a **VDI** (virtual desktop infrastructure) desktop to any device via remote display; needs a good network.

## 4.2 Client-side Virtualization
labs: virtualization-proxmox
@notes
### Client-side Virtualization
- Run several OSes at once on one machine; each guest sees its own CPU, RAM and storage but they share the hardware. A **hypervisor** (virtual machine manager) allocates resources and manages networking and security. IBM mainframes did this in the 1960s. On a desktop it runs on top of your OS; on a server it hosts many VMs.
- **Uses**: legacy software on an old OS next to a new one; different OSes without rebooting or partitioning; cost and time saving; **sandboxing/testing** (isolated VM, snapshot, roll back, mirror production safely).
- **Requirements**: CPU hardware support (**Intel VT**, **AMD-V**; enable in BIOS), **plenty of RAM**, **disk space** per VM (many GB), and correct guest **network configuration**.
- **Security**: **VM escape** (malware reaches the hypervisor and hops to other VMs, especially risky on shared hosting). VMs still need **host firewalls, antivirus**, patches. **Never trust downloaded pre-built VMs** (could contain malware); build your own.
- **VM networking**: **shared (NAT)**: VM uses a private IP and NATs through the host's address; **bridged**: VM gets its own IP on the LAN (like a physical device, via DHCP); **private (host-only)**: VM can talk only to other VMs, not outside.

- Before you change anything on a production system, get it approved and documented. Change control sets the scope, risk, rollback plan, schedule (a change window) and sign-off. Fixing a problem is a change like any other.

### The Troubleshooting Process
- **1. Gather information**: ask the user what happened, what changed, what the exact error is; try to reproduce it; check logs and recent updates. Back up first if you might touch data.

- **6. Verify full system functionality** and put preventive measures in place (so it does not return).
- **7. Document** findings, actions and outcome in the ticket or knowledge base; this helps the next tech.

## 5.2 Computer Hardware
labs: hardware-diagnostics-and-repair
@notes
### Startup Problems
- **POST beep codes**: vary by vendor, so do not memorize them; read the motherboard manual. Beeps with a blank screen point to video, RAM, CPU or bad BIOS settings.
- **Date/time wrong or reset**: the CMOS battery on the motherboard is dead; replace it. Removing it does not clear modern BIOS settings (stored in flash); use the clear jumper.
- **Boots from wrong drive**: check BIOS boot order, remove stray USB media.
- **Stop error / BSOD**: fatal; restart. If it flashes by, read Event Viewer. Look up the stop code on Microsoft's site. Causes: bad hardware, bad driver, bad app. Try last known good configuration, System Restore, driver rollback, Safe Mode (fewer drivers), reseat new cards, vendor hardware diagnostics (often built into UEFI).
- **Spinning wait cursor (macOS)**: app bug, bad hardware/RAM, or heavy paging. If stuck, force power off, then check console logs.

### No Display, No Power, Slow
- **Black screen**: check monitor power and video cables, check the monitor's input selector, brightness/contrast, swap in a known-good monitor, boot in VGA/low-res mode to separate driver from monitor.
- **No power**: test the outlet with a multimeter or tester, check the PSU switch and cables, check internal DC voltages. Fans spinning but nothing else means the motherboard may not get enough voltage; check POST.
- **Slow**: Task Manager (processes, Performance tab), background updates, full drive, fragmentation on HDD, laptop battery throttling, thermal throttling, antivirus scans.

### Heat, Smoke, Shutdowns, Noise
- **Overheating**: dust blocks fans and heat sinks. Clean and vacuum; monitor temperatures with a tool like HWMonitor.
- **Smoke or burning smell**: unplug immediately, open the case, find the failed part; maybe replace a part or the whole motherboard.
- **Sudden shutdown with no message**: check Event Viewer for thermal shutdown, check fans, run diagnostics, remove good components to isolate the bad one.
- **App crashes with no detail**: Event Viewer and **Reliability Monitor** (day-by-day graph of app, Windows and other failures); reinstall the app.
- **Grinding**: loose card, fallen heat sink or failing HDD (back up now). **Clicking/oscillating**: object in a fan. **Popping**: bulging or burst capacitors on the motherboard.
- **Freezing**: look for any response (drive light, Num Lock, Ctrl+Alt+Del); consider new hardware/drivers, full disk or memory, then full diagnostics.
- **Reboot loop**: note where it restarts (BIOS, splash screen, OS). Try last known good, Safe Mode, then disable "automatically restart" on failure so the stop error stays visible. Remove cards, swap RAM.

## 5.3 Troubleshooting Storage
labs: hardware-diagnostics-and-repair, secure-data-sanitization
@notes
### Storage Failures
- **"Cannot read from source disk", slow response, constant activity light, HDD clicking ("click of death")**: failing drive. **Back up first** if possible, then check cables, heat, and PSU load (new hardware may starve the drive). Run the manufacturer's diagnostics.
- **"Drive not recognized", "boot device not found", "operating system not found"**: check cables, BIOS boot order, stray USB media, a disabled drive in BIOS, another SATA/M.2 port, and try the drive in another computer.
- HDDs always eventually fail. Recovery companies are costly and can still fail; so keep backups. An SSD may fail by becoming read-only.

### RAID
- Hardware RAID controllers report failures at boot (e.g. "integrated RAID exception, volume inactive"). Use the RAID manager to see which drive failed.
- **RAID 0** (2+ drives): one failure loses everything. **RAID 1** (2+): survives one failure. **RAID 5** (3+): survives one failure. **RAID 10** (4+): survives one failure per mirror pair.

### SMART and Performance
- **SMART** is built-in drive health reporting (spin-up time, seek errors, power-on hours); rising error counts warn of failure. If a drive reports failing, back up and replace now.
- **IOPS** measures input/output operations per second: HDD about 200, SSD up to about a million; read and write differ.

### Missing Drives
- Internal drive missing in the OS: check BIOS, then cables, reseat M.2, else replace. External USB: check power and port. Network share: reconnect or re-run the login script.

## 5.5 Troubleshooting Mobile Devices
labs: hardware-diagnostics-and-repair
@notes
### Battery and Power
- Batteries lose capacity with each charge cycle; the fix for old ones is replacement. Save battery by watching signal (a phone hunting for signal drains fast), using **airplane mode** (turns off cellular, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, GPS radios), and checking per-app usage under Settings > Battery.
- **Swollen battery**: gas build-up. Stop using the device, never puncture it, replace it and dispose of the old one at a hazardous waste facility.
- **Slow or no charging**: clean lint from the port (do not bend pins), check the cable (try a known-good one), and measure outlet and charger voltage with a multimeter.

### Screen, Water, Heat
- **Cracked screen**: glass is bonded to the display, so replace the whole display (or the device). Back up first; cover cracks with clear tape to avoid cuts.
- **Water damage**: liquid contact indicators (LCIs) change color and void warranties. Power off at once, do not charge or power on, remove case, SIM, back and battery if possible, and let it dry for at least a day. Rice is a poor desiccant; silica gel is better.
- **Overheating**: sensors shut the device off. Heat comes from charging, CPU and display. Check app usage, and keep out of direct sunlight.

### Frozen, Interfaces, Malware, Drift
- **Unresponsive**: soft reset (hold power, slide to power off). Hard reset: Apple, hold power plus home or volume for about 10 seconds; Android, pull the battery or hold power and volume (varies by model).
- **Damaged charge/data port**: it is soldered to the system board, so repair often means replacing the board.
- **Malware symptoms**: unknown apps, high data use, pop-ups, high CPU, heat, rapid battery drain. Run a scanner or reset.
- **Cursor drift** (screen moves by itself): recalibrate touch input with the built-in calibration app.
- **Weak signal**: check cellular meter; indoors is worse. For Wi-Fi, limited range and interference, so change the AP channel.

### Testing and Bad Output

## 5.7 Troubleshooting Networks
labs: network-traffic-analysis, networking-tcp-ip-dhcp-nat-subnets
@notes
### No Connectivity: Work Outward
- Check the **link light** first. Then ping in order: **127.0.0.1 loopback** (local stack), **your own IP** (adapter), **default gateway**, then an internet address like **8.8.8.8** or **9.9.9.9**. The first failure shows where to look.
- **APIPA (169.254.x.x)** means DHCP did not answer; it is link-local only, and Windows shows "limited or no connectivity".

### Wireless
- **Partial ping replies** suggest interference, distance or weak signal. Check signal strength, move closer, set the AP to the best channel (auto or manual), avoid neighbors and sources such as microwaves and fluorescent lights.
- **SNR**: signal-to-noise ratio. You want a wide gap between signal and noise. Some noise always exists.

### Voice and Latency
- Real-time traffic (VoIP, streaming) shows trouble as robotic or underwater voices. **Jitter** is variation in time between frames; **latency** is time between request and response. Need fast, low-latency links, enough bandwidth, modern equipment. Use **packet captures** to measure.
- **Port flapping**: link light on and off. Check cables with a cable tester, try another switch port; if the problem moves with the cable it is the cable; if it stays at the port, the switch port; if it follows the device, replace the NIC.
