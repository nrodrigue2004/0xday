:: simple
Security people can read things, break things and see things that ordinary users cannot. That power is why the field runs on **ethics**: doing the right thing when nobody is checking.

### Core principles
- **Authorization first.** Only access systems you own or have clear permission to test. "It was unprotected" is not permission
- **Minimize harm.** Prove the problem with the least access and the least data. Don't read the whole database to show you could
- **Confidentiality.** What you learn on a job stays on the job
- **Honesty.** Report what you found accurately, including your own mistakes. Never fabricate or hide results
- **Competence.** Don't claim skills or do work you can't do safely
- **Duty to the public.** When your work shows serious risk to people, there are limits to silence

### Professional codes
Most organizations publish one: the **ACM Code of Ethics**, the **(ISC)² Code of Ethics** (protect society, act honorably, provide diligent service, advance the profession), **ISACA** and **SANS**. Break them and you can lose certifications as well as jobs. They are also frequent exam questions.

### The legal line, in plain words
Laws such as the US **Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA)** make it a crime to access a computer without authorization or beyond the authorization you were given. Other countries have similar laws. Good intentions don't remove the crime. That is why scope documents and written permission matter ([PEN300](../pentest/pentest-methodology.html)).

> If you find a vulnerability on a site you don't have permission to test, stop. Don't poke further to "confirm". You can still report it, carefully.

### Responsible (coordinated) disclosure
What to do when you find a flaw in someone else's product:
1. **Stop** at the minimum evidence. Don't access other users' data
2. **Find the right contact**: `security.txt` (`/.well-known/security.txt`), a vulnerability disclosure policy (VDP), a bug bounty program, or the vendor's security email. A CERT such as CERT/CC can mediate
3. **Report privately**: what, where, how to reproduce, impact, your contact details
4. **Give time**: the common norm is around **90 days** to fix (Google Project Zero uses 90, plus a grace period if a patch is imminent), shorter for actively exploited bugs
5. **Coordinate publication**: agree on a date, a CVE ID, and credit
6. **Don't extort**: asking for money in return for silence is not bounty, it is blackmail

**Full disclosure** (publishing everything at once) pressures vendors but exposes users. **Non-disclosure** keeps users unaware. Most of the industry prefers coordinated disclosure with a deadline.

### Bug bounty and VDP
A **bug bounty** pays for valid reports within a stated scope. A **VDP** invites reports but doesn't pay. Read the policy: scope, banned tests (DoS, social engineering), safe harbor language, and rules on data. Programs that promise "safe harbor" state that they won't pursue legal action for good-faith research that follows the rules.

### Common ethical dilemmas
- You find customer data exposed in a public bucket. Right: report it and don't download it
- A client asks you to soften a finding to avoid embarrassing the CEO. Right: report accurately; discuss wording, not truth
- You discover your friend's unauthorized access to a company system. Right: follow the incident or whistleblower process
- A tool you wrote is misused. Think about dual-use risk before release and how you describe and license it

### Insider risk and trust
Admins, developers and analysts have privileged access; good controls (separation of duties, logging, least privilege, review) protect both the company and the honest employee.

### Legal landscape (US-focused, not legal advice)
- **CFAA**: "exceeds authorized access" was narrowed by the Supreme Court in *Van Buren v. United States* (2021): it covers entering areas of a system you are barred from, not misusing data you may legitimately access
- **DOJ charging policy (2022)**: federal prosecutors are told not to charge good-faith security research, though state laws and civil suits can still apply
- **DMCA section 1201** bans circumventing copy protection, with exemptions for security research that are reviewed every three years
- **Contracts and terms of service** can create civil liability even when no crime occurs
- **Other countries**: the UK Computer Misuse Act, EU directives, local laws with different or no research exemptions
Before researching on a system you don't own, read the policy, consider the jurisdiction and, for anything risky, consult a lawyer. Organizations like the EFF publish researcher guidance.

### Handling data you shouldn't have
If you stumble onto sensitive data during legitimate work: stop viewing it, preserve only what's needed as evidence (with hashes), tell the right person immediately, secure the copy, and follow the data-handling terms. Privacy law (see [privacy law](privacy-law.html)) may apply to you as well.

### Offensive tooling and publication
Teaching and publishing exploit code is a long-standing debate. Responsible practice: publish proof-of-concept after a fix is available, avoid ready-to-run weaponized tools for unpatched bugs, describe defenses along with attacks and obey platform rules. This site does the same: labs are fictional and the guides stress defense and detection.

### AI, surveillance and dual use
Security knowledge can be used to protect or to surveil and manipulate. Consider the people affected by what you build: facial recognition, stalkerware, monitoring tools and AI systems have real harms. Ethical review is part of professional judgment, not an obstacle.

### Whistleblowing
When you see unlawful or dangerous conduct, use internal channels first where they are safe; know your protections (SOX, Dodd-Frank, the EU Whistleblower Directive, and state laws) and keep records. This is the hardest part of the job.

### How to practice ethics
Write down your own rules of engagement before you need them. Keep a log of decisions. Ask: Would I be comfortable if this appeared in the report, in the news, or in front of the person affected?
