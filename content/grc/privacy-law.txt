:: simple
**Privacy** is about people's rights over data about them. **Security** protects data from the wrong people. You need both: you can have perfect security and still break privacy law by collecting data you had no reason to hold.

### Key terms
- **PII**: personally identifiable information (name, ID number, address, email, IP address in some laws)
- **Sensitive data**: health, biometrics, financial accounts, race, religion, precise location, children's data. It gets stricter rules
- **Data subject**: the person the data is about
- **Controller**: decides why and how data is processed. **Processor**: handles it on the controller's behalf (your cloud provider, your payroll vendor)
- **Data minimization**: collect only what you need, keep it only as long as you need

### The laws you will hear about
| Law | Covers | Notable points |
|---|---|---|
| **GDPR** (EU, 2018) | Personal data of people in the EU, wherever the company is | Lawful basis needed, data subject rights, 72 hour breach notice, fines up to the greater of 20 million euros or 4% of global revenue |
| **CCPA / CPRA** (California) | Residents' personal information held by larger businesses | Right to know, delete, correct, opt out of sale or sharing |
| **HIPAA** (US) | Health information held by providers, plans and their vendors | Privacy, Security and Breach Notification Rules |
| **FERPA** (US) | Student education records at schools that receive federal funds | Parents or eligible students control access |
| **GLBA** (US) | Customer financial information at financial institutions | Safeguards Rule |
| **COPPA** (US) | Online collection from children under 13 | Parental consent |

> This is education, not legal advice. Laws change and have exceptions. A company asks its lawyer; a security person knows when to ask.

### GDPR in practice
**Lawful bases** for processing: consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task, legitimate interests. **Data subject rights**: be informed, access, rectify, erase ("right to be forgotten"), restrict, port, object, and not be subject to purely automated decisions. Requests must generally be answered within one month.
Other duties: **privacy by design and by default**, a record of processing, a **DPIA** (data protection impact assessment) for high-risk processing, a **DPO** (data protection officer) in some cases, a contract with each processor, and safeguards for **transfers** outside the EU (adequacy decisions, standard contractual clauses).
**Breach notice:** tell the supervisory authority **within 72 hours** of becoming aware, unless the breach is unlikely to risk people's rights. Tell the individuals without undue delay if the risk is high.

### US landscape
There is no single federal privacy law. Instead there are sector laws (HIPAA, GLBA, FERPA, COPPA), the FTC's authority over unfair or deceptive practices, and a growing set of **state laws** (California, Virginia, Colorado, Texas and many others). Every US state has its own **breach notification** law; deadlines and triggers differ (often 30 to 60 days, some "without unreasonable delay").

### HIPAA breach rules
Covered entities must notify affected individuals **within 60 days** of discovery. Breaches affecting 500 or more people also go to the HHS and the media promptly; smaller ones are reported to HHS annually. Properly encrypted data is generally a "safe harbor": loss of an encrypted laptop is not a reportable breach.

### Data classification
Label data so handling follows the label. A typical scheme: **Public**, **Internal**, **Confidential**, **Restricted**. Each level states where it may be stored, whether it must be encrypted, who may see it, how it is shared, and how it is destroyed. Match classification to [policy](policy-writing.html).

### Privacy and security controls
Encryption at rest and in transit, access control and logging, **pseudonymization** and **anonymization** (true anonymization is hard), retention schedules with deletion, vendor assessments, and training. Retention matters: data you deleted cannot be breached.
:: complex
### Cross-border data
Moving personal data across borders is regulated. The EU-US Data Privacy Framework, standard contractual clauses and binding corporate rules are the usual mechanisms. Some countries require data to stay inside them (**data localization**). Cloud architecture must account for this: region choice is a legal decision.

### Incident response and legal clocks
Build the clocks into your incident response plan: who decides if personal data was involved, who calls legal, who contacts regulators, where the contact list is. Time starts at *awareness*, so document when and how you became aware. Contracts with customers may add shorter deadlines (24 or 48 hours is common) than the law.

### Privacy engineering
- **Privacy by design**: data flow maps, default settings protect the user, collect least
- **DPIA / PIA** as a gate in development for new features
- **Consent management**: record what the user agreed to, honor withdrawal, respect browser signals like Global Privacy Control where required
- **Subject access request (DSAR)** process: verify identity, find data across systems, return it within the deadline
- **Deletion** across backups, analytics and partners
- Tools: data discovery and classification scanners, DLP

### Penalties and enforcement
Regulators fine for unlawful processing, inadequate security and late notification. Examples are large: GDPR fines in the hundreds of millions of euros for big tech companies; HIPAA settlements in the millions; class-action suits after breaches. Executives can be personally accountable in some regimes.

### AI and privacy
Training data can contain personal data; models may memorize it; automated decisions have special GDPR rights. Check [~/ai](../ai.html) for the security side and ask legal before sending personal data to an outside AI service.

### As a security person
Know what personal data your systems hold and where it flows; keep the data inventory current; classify; encrypt; restrict; log access; delete on schedule; know the clocks and whom to call.
