## TCP | UDP
Connection: Connection-oriented. A three-way handshake sets it up first. | Connectionless. It just sends datagrams.
Reliability: Acknowledges data, retransmits losses, delivers in order. | No delivery guarantee, no ordering, no retransmission.
Speed and overhead: Slower, with more header and state to track. | Faster and lighter, with a small fixed header.
Typical uses: Web (HTTP/HTTPS), SSH, email, file transfer. | DNS lookups, VoIP, video streaming, games, DHCP.
pick: Use TCP when every byte must arrive correct and in order. Use UDP when speed matters more than perfect delivery, or the application handles loss itself.
links: b:osi-and-tcpip u:basics/ports-and-protocols

## IPv4 | IPv6
Address size: 32 bits, written as four decimal numbers like 192.168.1.10. | 128 bits, written as hex groups like 2001:db8::1.
Number of addresses: About 4.3 billion, which ran out. | About 3.4 x 10^38, effectively unlimited.
NAT: Usually needed, because public addresses are scarce. | Not needed for address supply; every device can have a global address.
Notation and config: Manual, DHCP. | Manual, DHCPv6 or SLAAC (devices build their own address).
Security: IPsec is optional. | IPsec was designed in, but is still optional in practice. Firewalls still matter.
pick: Most networks run both (dual stack) today. IPv4 is not going away soon, but anything new should support IPv6.
links: g:networking-tcp-ip-dhcp-nat-subnets b:osi-and-tcpip

## Symmetric encryption | Asymmetric encryption
Keys: One shared secret key encrypts and decrypts. | A key pair: the public key encrypts, the private key decrypts (or signs).
Speed: Fast, fine for bulk data. | Slow, used for small things like key exchange and signatures.
Main problem: Getting the shared key to the other side safely. | Slower, and key management and trust (certificates) are the hard part.
Examples: AES, ChaCha20. | RSA, ECC, Diffie-Hellman.
pick: In practice they work together. Asymmetric crypto agrees on or protects a session key, then symmetric crypto encrypts the actual data (this is what TLS does).
alias: public key encryption = Asymmetric encryption, private key encryption = Symmetric encryption, secret key = Symmetric encryption
links: u:crypto/symmetric-modes u:crypto/public-key b:cia-and-crypto

## Hub | Switch | Router
OSI layer: Layer 1 (physical). | Layer 2 (data link), some are layer 3. | Layer 3 (network).
What it does: Repeats every signal out of every other port. | Learns MAC addresses and sends frames only to the right port. | Forwards packets between different networks using IP addresses.
Collision and traffic: One shared collision domain, so it is noisy and slow. | Each port is its own collision domain. | Separates broadcast domains and connects LANs to the internet.
Where you see it: Almost never now. | Inside a LAN, connecting devices. | At the edge of a network, home or office.
pick: A hub is obsolete. A switch connects devices inside one network. A router connects networks to each other (your home router also bundles a switch, Wi-Fi and NAT).
links: g:computer-networks b:osi-and-tcpip

## Virus | Worm | Trojan
How it spreads: Attaches to a file or program and needs a user to run it. | Spreads on its own across networks, no user action needed. | Does not self-spread. It pretends to be something useful.
Needs a host: Yes, it infects files. | No, it is standalone. | No, it relies on you installing it.
Typical damage: Corrupts or infects files. | Consumes bandwidth, carries payloads, spreads fast. | Opens a backdoor, steals data, drops other malware.
pick: The difference is how it gets in and moves: a virus rides a file, a worm moves by itself, a trojan tricks you into running it.
links: g:malware-analysis s:2.4

## SIEM | SOAR
Purpose: Collects and correlates logs, raises alerts, supports search and investigation. | Automates the response with playbooks and ties tools together.
Input and output: Logs in, alerts and dashboards out. | Alerts in, automated actions out (block IP, disable account, open a ticket).
Who uses it: SOC analysts, for detection and triage. | SOC and IR teams, to cut repetitive work.
Examples: Splunk, Elastic, Wazuh, Microsoft Sentinel. | Cortex XSOAR, Splunk SOAR, Shuffle, Tines.
pick: SIEM finds and shows the problem; SOAR does the repetitive steps after the alert fires. Many products now bundle both.

## IDS | IPS
Action: Detects and alerts only. | Detects and blocks the traffic inline.
Placement: Out of band, on a copy of traffic (TAP or SPAN port). | Inline, so traffic passes through it.
Risk: Misses nothing it can't see, but it won't stop anything by itself. | A false positive can block real traffic or break a service.
Examples: Snort and Suricata in IDS mode, Zeek. | Snort and Suricata in IPS mode.
pick: IDS watches and tells you. IPS watches and acts. Same detection engine, different placement and response.

## Authentication | Authorization
Question answered: Who are you? | What are you allowed to do?
How it works: Passwords, MFA, certificates, biometrics. | Permissions, roles, ACLs, policies.
Order: Happens first. | Happens after you are authenticated.
Example: Logging in to a server with a key. | That account can read /var/log but not edit /etc.
pick: Authentication proves identity. Authorization decides access. Accounting (the third A) records what you did.

## Encryption | Hashing | Encoding
Reversible: Yes, with the key. | No, it is one-way. | Yes, by anyone, no key.
Purpose: Keep data secret (confidentiality). | Check data wasn't changed (integrity) and store passwords. | Convert data to a format another system can handle.
Key needed: Yes. | No (but a salt helps for passwords). | No.
Examples: AES, RSA. | SHA-256, bcrypt. | Base64, URL encoding, ASCII.
pick: Encoding is not security. Hashing proves or stores without being reversible. Encryption hides data that you need to read back later.

## HTTP | HTTPS
Encryption: None. Anyone on the path can read and change it. | TLS encrypts the traffic and authenticates the server.
Port: 80 | 443
Certificate: None. | Needs a certificate from a trusted CA.
Use today: Only for redirects or internal testing. | The default for everything on the web.
pick: Always use HTTPS. HTTPS is HTTP carried inside TLS.

## Vulnerability | Threat | Risk
Meaning: A weakness in a system, process or person. | Anything that could exploit a weakness and cause harm. | The likelihood and impact of a threat exploiting a vulnerability.
Example: An unpatched server. | An attacker or malware. | A breach of that server costing money and data.
You control it by: Patching, hardening, configuring. | Mostly you can't; you reduce exposure. | Mitigate, transfer, accept or avoid.
pick: Risk = threat x vulnerability x impact, roughly. No weakness, or no one to exploit it, means little risk.

## Red team | Blue team | Purple team

## Firewall | WAF
Layer: Network and transport (IPs, ports), some inspect more. | Application layer (HTTP requests).
Protects against: Unwanted connections, port scans. | SQL injection, XSS, bad bots against a web app.
Placement: At the network edge or on the host. | In front of a web application.
pick: A firewall decides who may connect to what. A WAF reads the web requests and blocks malicious ones. Use both.

## VPN | Proxy
What it covers: All traffic from the device (or chosen networks), encrypted in a tunnel. | Usually one application or protocol (often web).
Encryption: Yes, between you and the VPN endpoint. | Not by itself.
Hides your IP: From the sites you visit, yes. | Yes for the proxied traffic.
Typical use: Remote access to a company network, privacy on public Wi-Fi. | Content filtering, caching, web access control.
pick: A VPN secures a connection to a network. A proxy relays requests for you. A VPN gives encryption; a plain proxy does not.

## Proxy | Reverse proxy
Sits in front of: Clients. | Servers.

## DoS | DDoS
Source: One attacking system. | Many systems, often a botnet.
Blocking: Block the one source. | Hard: traffic comes from thousands of addresses.
Scale: Limited by one machine. | Can reach hundreds of Gbps.

## Phishing | Spear phishing | Whaling
