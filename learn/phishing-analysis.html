Phishing is still how most breaches start. Learn to read email headers, authentication results and hidden links, then work a user-reported phish like a SOC analyst.

1simple2mid3complex4hands-on+ref

01 simple // the idea in plain words

A phishing email pretends to be someone trustworthy to get you to click a link, open an attachment, or hand over a password or money. The tells are usually small:

  • urgency or fear ("your account will be closed today")
  • a sender name that looks right but an address that doesn't
  • a link whose text says one thing while the real address says another
  • requests that skip normal process (gift cards, wire transfers, "keep this confidential")

An email file (.eml) is plain text: headers on top, a blank line, then the body.

$ ls ~/case
$ head -12 ~/case/reported.eml

When in doubt, report it. A false alarm costs the SOC two minutes; a click can cost the company weeks.

Why phishing works

It uses urgency, authority, curiosity and fear: "Your account will be closed", "invoice attached", "the CEO needs gift cards". It is cheap to send millions of messages and only a few people need to click. Phishing delivers credential theft (fake login pages), malware (attachments, links to downloads) and fraud (business email compromise, where no link is needed at all).

Types

Mass phishing, spear phishing (targeted at one person), whaling (executives), smishing (SMS), vishing (voice), quishing (QR codes) and BEC (impersonating someone to redirect payments).

02 mid // how it's actually used

Headers that matter

  • From: is what the user sees and is trivially forged. Return-Path: (envelope sender) and Reply-To: often reveal the real sender
  • Received: lines are added by each mail server, newest on top. The bottom one is closest to the real origin
  • Authentication-Results: holds the verdicts of SPF (is this server allowed to send for the domain?), DKIM (is the signature valid?) and DMARC (do they align with the From domain, and what should happen if not?)
$ grep -iE "^(from|reply-to|return-path):" ~/case/reported.eml
$ grep -i "authentication-results" ~/case/reported.eml
$ grep "^Received:" ~/case/reported.eml

Look-alike domains

Typosquats swap characters (rn for m, 0 for o), add words (-secure, -login) or change the TLD. Read domains letter by letter.

Email authentication in plain words

  • SPF: the sending IP is allowed to send for this domain.
  • DKIM: the message carries a signature the domain's key can verify, so it was not altered.
  • DMARC: tells receivers what to do when SPF or DKIM fail *and align* with the visible From domain (none, quarantine, reject).

A "pass" only means the message really came from the domain in question, and a look-alike domain can pass everything. Check the Authentication-Results header and the Reply-To and Return-Path mismatches.

Reading a message in order

  1. Display name versus real address; who is the actual sender domain?
  2. Received headers read bottom to top: where did it really enter the mail system?
  3. Links: hover to see the real URL, expand shorteners, check domain age and registrar (whois).
  4. Attachments: hash them, never open them on a normal machine. Macros, ISO, ZIP with password, HTML attachments and OneNote files are common carriers.
  5. Language and context: unexpected request, mismatched tone, urgency, payment change.

03 complex // what the pros do

Hidden content

HTML email can show one link and point to another, and bodies are often base64 or quoted-printable encoded so filters and humans don't see the text. Decode the part to see what the user saw, and extract every URL:

$ grep -A3 "Content-Transfer-Encoding: base64" ~/case/reported.eml
$ grep -oE "https?://[^\" >]+" ~/case/reported.eml | sort -u

Safe handling

  • Defang indicators when you share them: hxxps://evil[.]com
  • Never open links from the analyst workstation; use a sandbox or URL scanner
  • Hash attachments (sha256sum) and look the hash up instead of opening the file

Response

Search the mail logs for everyone who got the same message (same subject, sender or URL), pull it from all mailboxes, block the sender and domain, and check proxy logs for anyone who clicked. If someone entered credentials, reset them and revoke their sessions, because attackers log in within minutes.

Investigating the infrastructure

Look up the domain (whois, creation date within days is a red flag), DNS records, TLS certificate (Certificate Transparency at crt.sh), hosting IP and reputation (VirusTotal, urlscan.io for a safe screenshot of the page, abuse reports). Search your proxy and DNS logs for any other user who contacted the domain; that is your impact list.

Response checklist

Pull the message from every mailbox (search by sender, subject or message ID), block the sender, domain and URL, reset passwords and revoke sessions for anyone who entered credentials, check for new inbox rules and OAuth app consents (a favorite persistence trick), scan endpoints for anyone who opened the attachment, and report to the provider. Then tell users what happened without blame, because people report faster when they are not afraid.

Reducing the risk

MFA (phishing-resistant, like FIDO2 keys, beats codes that can be relayed), DMARC at reject, external-sender banners, link rewriting and sandboxing, an easy "report phish" button and regular simulations with training that teaches instead of punishes.

04 hands-on // ticket queue

d.kim: "Got this from HR about updating my direct deposit. The logo looked right but something felt off. I did NOT click."

Analyze reported.eml. Work out where the link in the email really goes (the visible text lies), then check proxy.log to see whether anyone in the company visited it.

Resolve the ticket with the username of the employee who clicked, e.g. jdoe.

This one is a ticket in the queue. Open the attachments, investigate in the console under the ticket (every ▶ run above works there), then resolve it with your answer. Hints cost 15 points, wrong answers 5.
