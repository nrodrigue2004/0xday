~/careers/interview

The questions every interviewer asks, whatever the job. Learn the shape of a good answer, then fill it with your own real stories.

Every path below has its own role-specific questions too. Start here, then go to the path you're aiming at.

cat METHOD

how to answer

Answers are built from your own material. Model answers below show the shape and the level of detail. Replace the examples with things you actually did. Interviewers can tell when a story is borrowed, and a follow-up question ("what would you do differently?") exposes it fast.

STAR for behavioral questions. Situation (one sentence), Task (what was yours to do), Action (what *you* did, with specifics), Result (a number or an outcome, then what you learned). Aim for about 90 seconds. Most weak answers spend 80 percent on the situation and skip the result.

For technical questions: answer in layers. Give the one-sentence definition first, then how it works, then a real example or a trade-off. If you don't know, say what you do know, then how you would find out. "I haven't used that, but here is how I would approach it" beats a guess stated as fact.

Prepare four stories and reuse them across questions: something you built, something that broke and how you fixed it, a time you learned something fast, and a time you disagreed or got feedback. Lab projects count when they are real work you can walk through in detail.

grep -i 'tell me' questions

opening questions

  1. [easy] Tell me about yourself.
    Present, past, future in about a minute. Present: "I'm finishing a cybersecurity degree and I run a homelab where I practice detection and administration." Past: one or two pieces of evidence that match this job (a project, an internship, a lab). Future: why this role is the next step. Don't recite the resume line by line.
  2. [easy] Why do you want this job?
    Connect the role to something specific you have already done. "I enjoy the triage side of security, and I've been practicing it with Security Onion alerts at home" is stronger than "I'm passionate about cybersecurity." Mention one thing about the team or company you actually looked up.
  3. [easy] Why should we hire you over other candidates?
    You don't know the other candidates, so don't compare. Name two or three strengths that match the posting, each with proof: what you built, what you measured, what you fixed. Close with willingness to learn the stack they use.
  4. [easy] Walk me through your resume.
    Go in reverse order of relevance, not chronology: start with the experience closest to this job. For each item say what the problem was, what you did, and the outcome. Skip anything you couldn't answer follow-up questions about.
  5. [easy] What are your strengths?
    Pick one or two that the job needs and back each with an example. "I document what I do, so my homelab notes let me rebuild a VM from scratch" is concrete. Avoid generic words like "hard-working."
  6. [easy] What is your biggest weakness?
    Give a real, fixable weakness and what you are doing about it. For example: "I used to dive into a problem without writing down what I'd already tried, so I repeated steps. Now I keep a running log in each ticket or lab." Don't pick a fake weakness like perfectionism.
  7. [easy] Where do you see yourself in five years?
    Honest and aimed at growth in the same field: deeper technical skill, maybe a specialty, maybe mentoring newer people. Don't say you want the interviewer's job, and don't say you'll have left the field.
  8. [easy] What are your salary expectations?
    Research the range for the role and location first (levels.fyi, Glassdoor, the posting itself, the BLS occupational handbook). Give a range anchored on the market, say you are flexible depending on the whole package, and ask what range they budgeted if you can.
  9. [easy] Do you have any questions for us?
    Always yes. Ask about the first 90 days, what a good first year looks like, how the team handles on-call or escalation, how they train new people, and what the hardest part of the job is. Don't ask about salary or vacation in a first screen.
  10. [easy] Are you willing to work shifts, weekends or on-call?
    Be honest. SOC and help desk roles often run 24/7 rotations, and entry-level people usually get the odd hours first. If you are fine with it, say so plainly; if there are limits, state them early and politely.

grep -i 'time when' questions

behavioral (STAR)

  1. [normal] Tell me about a time something you built or configured broke.
    Pick a real failure with a clear cause. Example shape: "I changed a firewall rule on my pfSense box and locked myself out of the management interface. I got back in through the Proxmox console, restored the saved config, and now I snapshot the VM and export the config before every change." Own the mistake, show the fix, show the process change.
  2. [normal] Describe a time you had to learn something quickly.
    Name the deadline or need, how you learned (docs, labs, asking someone), and the result. A good example is picking up a tool for a project and then using it unprompted afterward.
  3. [normal] Tell me about a time you disagreed with someone.
    Focus on how you handled it: you asked questions, found the actual difference, used data or a test to decide, and kept the relationship. Never trash a former coworker or teammate.
  4. [normal] Tell me about a difficult user or customer.
    Show empathy and process: you listened, restated the problem, found the actual cause (often not the one reported), explained it without jargon, and followed up. Help desk stories fit here.
  5. [normal] Tell me about a time you made a mistake at work.
    Same structure as the broken-build story but about people or process: what happened, how fast you disclosed it, how you fixed it, what you changed. Interviewers hire people who report mistakes early.
  6. [normal] How do you prioritize when everything is urgent?
    Describe a method: impact and number of people affected first, then deadline, then effort. Say you communicate the order to stakeholders rather than silently dropping things. In IT and security, "service down" and "active compromise" outrank everything.
  7. [normal] Tell me about a project you're proud of.
    Walk through the goal, the design choices and why, one problem you hit, and the outcome. Have a diagram or screenshot ready. Be able to go three levels deeper on any component you mention.
  8. [normal] How do you stay current in the field?
    Name specific sources, not "I read a lot." For example: CISA and vendor advisories, a couple of podcasts or blogs, TryHackMe or HackTheBox rooms, and a homelab where you try what you read. Mention the last thing you learned.
  9. [normal] How do you handle working under pressure or during an incident?
    Stay methodical: confirm scope, communicate status on a schedule, follow the runbook, write things down as you go. Admit what you don't know and escalate early. Calm and clear updates matter more than speed.
  10. [normal] Tell me about working on a team.
    Pick a group project or shared lab. Say what your piece was, how you coordinated (Git, tickets, check-ins), and one thing the team did better because of how you worked.
  11. [normal] What do you do when you don't know the answer?
    Narrow the problem, search the docs and logs, try a small test, then ask with specifics ("here is what I checked and what I saw") after a reasonable time, not after hours stuck and not after thirty seconds.
  12. [normal] Tell me about a time you explained something technical to a non-technical person.
    Use an analogy, skip acronyms, check understanding, and give them the one action they need to take. Help desk, tutoring and presenting a project all work as examples.

grep -i 'integrity' questions

judgment and ethics

  1. [normal] You notice a coworker doing something that looks against policy. What do you do?
    Verify what you actually saw, avoid accusing, and report through the proper channel (manager, security team, or ethics line). Don't confront them in public and don't investigate with access you don't have.
  2. [normal] You find sensitive data you shouldn't have access to. What do you do?
    Stop, don't open or copy it further, record where it was (not the contents), and report it to your manager or the data owner right away. Misconfigured access is a finding, not a perk.
  3. [normal] A manager asks you to skip a security step to hit a deadline.
    Explain the specific risk in business terms, offer a faster safe option, and if the answer is still no, get the decision in writing and escalate according to policy. Don't quietly comply and don't quietly refuse.
  4. [normal] Is it ever okay to test a system you don't own?
    No, not without written authorization. Testing needs a defined scope, rules of engagement and sign-off. CTF platforms and your own lab are the places to practice.

grep -i 'start' questions

closing questions

  1. [easy] What would your last manager or professor say about you?
    Two honest strengths and one growth area, in the words they would actually use. Better if you can attach a specific example for each.
  2. [easy] When can you start?
    Give an honest date. If you are still in school or serving notice, say when you finish and whether part-time or early work is possible.
  3. [easy] Anything else you'd like us to know?
    Use it for one point you haven't yet made, such as a project they didn't ask about, then end with interest in the role. Keep it under 30 seconds.

cat ASK_THEM

questions to ask them

  • What does a successful first 90 days look like in this role?
  • How is the team structured, and who would I work with most?
  • What tools and platforms does the team use day to day?
  • How do you train and develop new people? Is there a budget for certifications?
  • What is the hardest part of this job that the posting doesn't mention?
  • What are the next steps and timeline for the process?

cat RED_FLAGS

things that sink an interview

  • Saying "I have no weaknesses" or "my weakness is working too hard."
  • Claiming a skill or tool on the resume that you can't discuss for five minutes.
  • Speaking badly about a past employer, teacher or teammate.
  • Arriving with no questions for them.
  • Guessing confidently instead of saying "I'm not sure, but here is how I'd find out."
