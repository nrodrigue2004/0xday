4.2 Attack Types

study notes

Denial of Service

  • DoS: make a service unavailable, by overload, a software flaw, a distraction while another attack runs, or plain physical action (cut power, water leak). Accidents count: a switching loop without STP floods the network, and one huge download can saturate a small link.
  • DDoS: many devices (a botnet) attack together. It is asymmetric: attackers with few resources beat big ones.
  • Reflection and amplification: the attacker sends small spoofed requests (source = victim) to open servers; they answer the victim with far bigger replies. Protocols abused: NTP, DNS, ICMP. Example: a 28-byte DNS query with the ANY type returned about 1,300 bytes. Defenses: patching, rate limits, anti-spoofing filters, closing open resolvers, upstream scrubbing.

VLAN Hopping

  • VLANs separate traffic unless a router connects them. Two tricks cross without a router:
  • Switch spoofing: the attacker pretends to be a switch; if the port auto-negotiates a trunk (DTP), they reach every VLAN on it. Fix: disable trunk auto-negotiation, set ports manually to access, and list the allowed VLANs on trunks.
  • Double tagging: a frame with two 802.1Q tags. The first switch strips the tag that matches the native VLAN and forwards the frame, the next switch reads the second tag and drops it onto the target VLAN. Traffic is one-way (no replies), enough for DoS. Fix: don't put users in the native VLAN, change native VLAN from 1 to an unused ID, and tag the native VLAN.

MAC Flooding

  • A MAC address is 48 bits (6 bytes, hex); first 3 bytes = OUI (vendor), last 3 = NIC serial. The burned-in address is stored in ROM.
  • A switch learns source MACs per port into a MAC (CAM) table; entries age out in about 5 minutes (STP also uses MACs). Frames for known MACs go out one port only; unknown destinations are flooded to all ports.
  • MAC flooding: send many frames with random source MACs to fill the limited table; then all traffic floods like a hub and the attacker can sniff it. Fix: port security (limit MACs per port, sticky MACs, violation action).

ARP and DNS Poisoning

  • Spoofing = pretending to be another person/device (email, caller ID, IP, DNS). It enables on-path (man-in-the-middle) attacks.
  • ARP has no authentication and accepts unsolicited replies. In ARP poisoning the attacker sends a reply "192.168.1.1 is at attacker-MAC", the victim caches it, and all its router traffic goes through the attacker (usually forwarded on, so nobody notices). Defenses: dynamic ARP inspection (DAI), static entries, 802.1X.
  • DNS poisoning: change records on the DNS server or forge responses in transit (often from an on-path position), or edit the client hosts file (which overrides DNS). Victims are sent to the attacker's server. Defenses: DNSSEC, patching, DoT/DoH, locked-down hosts file.

Rogue Services

  • Rogue DHCP server: DHCP has no authentication, so a fake server can hand out bad IPs, gateways or DNS (outage or on-path). Defenses: DHCP snooping (only trusted ports may answer), AD-authorized DHCP servers. If found, remove it and renew all leases.
  • Rogue access point: an unauthorized AP plugged into the LAN (or OS Wi-Fi sharing). Find with wireless scans and walk-arounds; prevent with 802.1X (NAC) on switch ports.
  • Evil twin: malicious AP copying a real SSID/captive portal, often at higher power. Always use VPN or HTTPS.
  • On-path attack: sits between two parties to read or change traffic. Examples: evil twin, ARP poisoning, session hijacking, HTTPS spoofing, Wi-Fi eavesdropping. Encryption limits the damage.

Social Engineering

  • Phishing: spoofed message to steal data. Check the sender domain, link target, spelling and graphics; open suspicious links only in an isolated VM, better, don't click.
  • Shoulder surfing: watching a screen (also through windows, binoculars, or hijacked webcams). Use privacy filters, sit back to a wall, face screens away from windows.
  • Tailgating: follow an authorized person through a door without their knowledge. Piggybacking: they let you in knowingly (hands full, "hold the door"). Prevent with badges, one-at-a-time rules, access control vestibules (airlocks), and employees who challenge unbadged strangers.
  • Dumpster diving: sorting through trash for names and documents. Prevent by locking or fencing bins, cameras, shredding or burning.

Malware

  • Virus: needs a human to spread. Worm: self-replicates over the network with no help (more dangerous, uses unpatched vulnerabilities). Ransomware: encrypts data and demands payment for the key. Trojan: disguised as something wanted. Rootkit: hides inside the OS, hard to detect or remove. Keylogger: records keystrokes and credentials. Adware/spyware: ads and spying for profit. Bloatware: unwanted preinstalled software (not malicious, wastes resources). Logic bomb: triggers on a date or event. Botnet / bot: remote-controlled infected hosts. Backdoor: hidden remote access.
  • Delivery: links, pop-ups, drive-by downloads. Defense: patch OS and apps, don't click, backups.

flashcards

self-check

1. Sniffing shows traffic for other hosts after a switch was hit with thousands of fake source MACs. Attack?

  • a) ARP poisoning
  • b) MAC flooding
  • c) Double tagging
  • d) DNS poisoning
answer

b) The table filled, so the switch floods like a hub.

2. Which feature stops a laptop from offering IPs to clients on an access port?

  • a) DHCP snooping
  • b) STP
  • c) NTP
  • d) Port mirroring
answer

a) Only trusted ports may send DHCP offers.

3. An attacker sends frames with two 802.1Q tags and gets no reply. Why?

  • a) Switch spoofing is one-way
  • b) Double tagging is one-way
  • c) STP blocked it
  • d) DNSSEC
answer

b) Replies cannot be routed back across VLANs.

4. A person follows an employee through a badge door without being noticed. Best control?

  • a) Privacy filter
  • b) Access control vestibule
  • c) MAC filter
  • d) Shredder
answer

b) Forces one person at a time.

short lab: Spot the poison

arp -a on a host shows the gateway 192.168.1.1 and another host 192.168.1.14 with the same MAC. What happened and what are the next steps?

walkthrough

Two IPs with one MAC means the .14 machine claimed the gateway's IP (ARP poisoning). Find .14's port via the switch MAC table, isolate it, flush ARP caches (arp -d *), and enable dynamic ARP inspection.
