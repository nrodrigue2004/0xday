2.3 Wireless Devices

study notes

Wireless Technologies

  • IEEE 802.11 standards have friendly names: 802.11ac = Wi-Fi 5, 802.11ax = Wi-Fi 6 (6E extends into 6 GHz), 802.11be = Wi-Fi 7.
  • Frequencies and channels: 2.4, 5 and 6 GHz bands, divided into numbered channels (2.4 GHz channel 6 is centered on 2.437 GHz, 5 GHz channel 44 on 5.220 GHz). Channel width (bandwidth) 20, 40, 80 or 160 MHz: wider is faster but uses more spectrum. The 2.4 GHz band has only three non-overlapping channels (1, 6, 11) at 20 MHz, 5 GHz has many more, 6 GHz even more.
  • Older clients may only do 2.4 GHz. Band steering makes the AP nudge dual-band clients onto 5 GHz (better throughput) instead of letting them pick by signal strength alone.
  • Regulation: the FCC in the US allocates spectrum; the ITU coordinates globally. 802.11h adds interoperability: DFS (dynamic frequency selection) moves the AP off channels that conflict with other users (and tells clients), TPC (transmit power control) lets the AP tell clients to lower power.

Wireless Networking

  • IBSS = ad hoc, device to device with no AP (used when first configuring IoT gear).
  • SSID = network name. BSSID = the AP's radio hardware (MAC) address. ESSID = the same SSID shared across multiple APs so clients roam seamlessly across the building.
  • Captive portal: first connection redirects to a page for terms or login; success adds you to an access table for a set time (for example 24 hours).
  • Security modes: open (no security), OWE (opportunistic wireless encryption: encrypts without a password, isolates users), WEP (broken), WPA/WPA2/WPA3 Personal = PSK (one shared passphrase), Enterprise / 802.1X (each person authenticates with their own credentials, so disabling an account cuts access). Enterprises should use 802.1X.
  • Antennas: omnidirectional spreads signal in all directions (put the AP in the middle). Directional focuses signal: Yagi (high gain, narrow), parabolic (dish, collects and focuses, long distance). Gain is in dB: every +3 dB roughly doubles power.
  • Autonomous AP: standalone (home). Lightweight AP: the brains live in a wireless LAN controller (WLC) managed over CAPWAP (control and provisioning of wireless access points), a single pane of glass for deploy, monitor, change, report.

Network Types

  • Mesh: APs link to each other and heal themselves if one drops (home mesh). Ad hoc / IBSS: two devices directly, no AP. Point-to-point: AP-to-AP links over long distance (building to building) needing the right antennas, power and frequency. Infrastructure: clients connect to a central AP (most common); some APs offer client isolation so wireless devices cannot talk to each other.

Wireless Encryption

  • Wireless needs authentication, encryption (confidentiality) and a message integrity check. WEP: vulnerable cryptography. WPA: temporary fix that ran on WEP hardware. WPA2 (2004): AES with CCMP (counter mode with CBC-MAC, encryption and integrity together). WPA3 (2018): AES with GCMP (Galois counter mode) and GMAC for integrity. Use the newest your devices support.

flashcards

self-check

1. Employees leave and must immediately lose Wi-Fi access without changing a shared password. Best?

  • a) WPA2-PSK
  • b) WPA2/WPA3 Enterprise with 802.1X
  • c) Open with captive portal
  • d) WEP
answer

b) Per-user credentials can be disabled individually.

2. Three adjacent APs on 2.4 GHz cause heavy interference. Which channels would you use?

  • a) 1, 6, 11
  • b) 2, 3, 4
  • c) 6, 7, 8
  • d) 1, 2, 3
answer

a) Only these three do not overlap at 20 MHz.

short lab: Design the Wi-Fi

A 3-floor office with 150 users, guests and laptops. Pick standards, bands, security and management.

walkthrough

Wi-Fi 6/6E APs with lightweight management by a WLC, one ESSID for staff with WPA3-Enterprise (802.1X) and a separate guest SSID on its own VLAN with captive portal and client isolation. Prefer 5/6 GHz with band steering, 2.4 GHz on channels 1/6/11 only for legacy. DFS and TPC on.
