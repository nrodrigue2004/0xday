4.6 Given a scenario, implement and maintain identity and access management

key ideas

  • Provisioning/de-provisioning, permission assignments, identity proofing, federation, SSO (LDAP, OAuth, SAML), interoperability, attestation.
  • Access models: mandatory, discretionary, role-based, rule-based, attribute-based, time-of-day restrictions, least privilege.
  • MFA: something you know, have, are, somewhere you are. Hard/soft tokens, security keys, biometrics.
  • Password concepts: length, complexity, reuse, expiration, age; password managers; passwordless.
  • Privileged access management: just-in-time permissions, password vaulting, ephemeral credentials.

study notes

Identity and access management (IAM) decides who gets in, what they can do, and how that is kept under control. Expect scenario questions that ask for the best model or factor.

Identity and access management

  • Provisioning: creating accounts and assigning access when someone joins. De-provisioning: removing access promptly when they leave or change role. Delays here are a leading cause of insider and former-employee incidents.
  • Permission assignments and implications: least privilege, need to know. Over-permission accumulates ("permission creep") when people change roles; review periodically.
  • Identity proofing: verifying a person is who they claim before issuing credentials (documents, in-person checks, knowledge checks).
  • Federation: trust between organizations so one login works across them. The identity provider (IdP) authenticates; the service provider (SP) relies on it.
  • Single sign-on (SSO): authenticate once, access many systems. Convenient, but a compromised SSO account exposes many systems, so protect it with MFA.
  • Standards:
    • LDAP: directory access protocol (Active Directory speaks it).
    • SAML (Security Assertions Markup Language): XML-based federation and SSO, common in enterprise and between organizations.
    • OAuth: authorization framework letting an app act on your behalf with limited scope, without your password ("allow this app to read my calendar").
    • OpenID Connect (OIDC): identity layer built on OAuth for authentication.
    • Kerberos: ticket-based authentication in Active Directory. Uses a key distribution center and timestamps (clocks must be in sync).
    • RADIUS/TACACS+: centralized AAA for network access (TACACS+ encrypts the whole payload and separates authentication, authorization and accounting).
  • Interoperability, attestation (proving a device or software is in a known good state).
  • Password vaulting / privileged access management (PAM): store admin credentials in a vault, check them out for a limited time, record sessions, and rotate secrets. Just-in-time (JIT) permissions grant elevated access only when needed.

Access control models

Quick tell: "labels/clearance" → MAC. "Owner decides" → DAC. "Job function/group" → RBAC. "Attributes/context" → ABAC. "Rules/ACL with conditions" → rule-based.

Multifactor authentication (MFA)

Factors are different categories of proof. Two passwords are not two factors.

FactorExamples
Something you knowPassword, PIN, security answer
Something you haveSmart card, hardware token, phone app, security key (FIDO2)
Something you areFingerprint, face, iris, voice
Somewhere you areLocation, IP, geofence
Something you doBehavior: gait, typing rhythm
  • OTP types: HOTP (counter-based), TOTP (time-based, 30-second codes in authenticator apps). Push notifications and SMS codes are weaker (SIM swapping, push fatigue); FIDO2/WebAuthn security keys and passkeys resist phishing.
  • Biometrics are measured by FAR (false acceptance: an impostor gets in), FRR (false rejection: a legitimate user is locked out), and the CER/EER (crossover error rate where they are equal; lower is better). Biometrics are convenient but cannot be changed if stolen.
  • Authentication, authorization and accounting all remain separate steps.

Password security

  • Modern guidance (NIST SP 800-63B): length over complexity, allow long passphrases, check against breached/common password lists, no forced periodic changes without evidence of compromise, don't use security questions or hints.
  • Rules you still see: minimum length, complexity, history (no reuse), maximum/minimum age, lockout after failed attempts, expiration.
  • Password managers generate and store unique passwords per site.
  • Passwordless options: passkeys, security keys, biometrics plus device.
  • Stored passwords must be salted and hashed with a slow algorithm.
  • Account types: user, privileged (admin; use separate accounts and PAM), service (non-interactive; rotate credentials, restrict logon), guest, shared (avoid; breaks accountability).
  • Account policies: expiration for contractors, disable on termination, location and time restrictions, impossible travel detection.

More detail from the lessons

IAM lifecycle

  • IAM covers employees, vendors, contractors and customers, and data on devices, in a data center or in the cloud. It starts when a user needs access and ends when access is removed (onboarding, changes after a promotion or move, offboarding). It tracks name, attributes, group permissions and application access, and logs and monitors access (often required by regulation).
  • Provisioning and deprovisioning give the right permissions and limit ongoing access (never admin by default). Users get permissions through groups (a new hire joins the email group). Documents a user creates are private to them by default, and users have limited access to the core operating system, which also limits malware.
  • Identity proofing: resolution (verify they are who they say), extra validation (something only they know, security answers), and attestation through government documents such as a passport or driver's license, in-person meeting, or automated checks using credit report questions about past addresses or assets.

SSO and standards in detail

  • Authentication flow: client to a central point (VPN concentrator, Active Directory) with a username and password, an authentication server validates and approves. SSO: log in once and reach resources (printers, shares) for a time window, such as 24 hours before re-authenticating. Your authorization process must support it.
  • LDAP: access to large directories; based on the X.500 specification from the ITU (ancestor: DAP over OSI). Names are distinguished names of attribute=value pairs (CN = common name); objects form a directory information tree with containers for country, organization, organizational units and leaf objects.
  • SAML: authenticate to a third-party database instead of keeping your own. Not designed for mobile devices. Flow with three parties: client (browser), resource server, and an authorization/identity server. The client requests a URL, the resource server sends an encrypted SAML request that redirects to the authorization server, the user logs in, the server issues a SAML token, and the client presents the token to the resource server.
  • OAuth: authorization framework built by large companies for mobile and multi-device use. It is paired with OpenID for authentication. Example: Zapier asks for permission to view, edit, create and delete in your Google Drive, and your Allow click creates the link.
  • Federation: network access without a local authentication database, such as signing in with Twitter, Facebook or LinkedIn. The site must have a relationship set up with those providers.
  • Interoperability drives selection: a new VPN concentrator that supports LDAP fits an existing Active Directory; an OAuth app needs authentication via an API that feeds OAuth.

Access control in detail

  • Policy is defined, then IT turns it into OS-level rights. Least privilege is the baseline for any model and limits damage from malware running as that user.
  • MAC: labels on resources (confidential, secret, top secret); the administrator decides who gets what (a shipping clerk might get confidential, a senior manager top secret).
  • DAC: the data creator controls access and permission levels (read-only vs modify). Flexible but less secure because every user must choose correctly.
  • RBAC: administrator makes groups (manager, director, team lead, project manager), assigns rights to the group, and adds users to groups. In Windows these are groups (for example a shipping and receiving group, and a managers group that can also view shipping logs).
  • Rule-based: system-enforced rules created by the administrator and attached to objects; users cannot change them. Examples: data in a lab accessible only 9 AM to 5 PM, or a web form only usable with the Chrome browser.
  • ABAC: next-generation model combining many criteria such as source IP, time of day, action (read or write), and relationship to the data.
  • Time-of-day restrictions work across models; include the user's time zone for global companies. Examples: training room network off midnight to 6 AM, conference room access limited after 8 PM, R&D databases only 8 AM to 6 PM.

MFA details

  • Factors: something you know (password, PIN, an unlock pattern), something you have (smart card plus PIN, USB security key with your certificate, hardware token showing numbers also computed by the server, software token on your phone, SMS code), something you are (biometrics store a mathematical representation, not a picture; hard to change; combine with other factors since biometrics can be bypassed), and somewhere you are (a login from another country 10 minutes after you were elsewhere is refused; IP address is imperfect and harder with IPv6; combine with GPS).

Password security details

  • Entropy means unpredictability: avoid single words, mix upper and lower case, numbers and special characters. Minimum 8 characters, rising over time; phrases give longer passwords.
  • Password age and expiration (30, 60, 90 days; 15 or 7 for critical systems), reminders before expiry, and history to block reuse.
  • Use a different password per account; a password manager stores them in an encrypted database protected by extra credentials or MFA, ships in many operating systems, has enterprise versions, gives a health summary (compromised or weak passwords) and generates random passwords.
  • Passwordless: face unlock on a phone, a Windows PIN. Usually initially set up with a password or another factor.
  • Just-in-time permissions: a technician requests access from a central clearinghouse or password vault under pre-set policy; the vault creates ephemeral credentials from the primary ones (primary credentials never shown) and deletes them after the session. If an attacker breaches an ordinary account, they do not have admin access.

Exam traps

  • OAuth = authorization; OIDC = authentication layer on top; SAML = XML federation/SSO.
  • FAR lets the wrong person in; FRR keeps the right person out.
  • A password plus a PIN is one factor (both something you know).
  • Kerberos needs synchronized clocks.
  • RBAC is the best fit for large, role-structured organizations.
  • MAC is the strictest; DAC is the most flexible.
