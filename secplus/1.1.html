SY0-701domain 1.0 · General Security Concepts1.1

1.1 Compare and contrast various types of security controls

key ideas

  • Four categories: technical (firewalls, encryption), managerial (policies, risk assessments), operational (guards, awareness training: done by people), physical (locks, fences, badges).
  • Control types describe what a control does: preventive, deterrent, detective, corrective, compensating, directive.
  • One control can be several types: a camera is physical, and deters and detects.
  • Compensating controls are the substitute when the ideal control isn't possible (isolate an unpatchable server).
  • Directive controls tell people what to do (policy, signage) but don't enforce anything.

study notes

Security controls are the safeguards that stop, spot, limit or repair security events. The exam describes every control with two labels: a category (who or what implements it) and a type (what job it does). Practice sorting any example into a cell of the grid below.

The four categories (who implements it)

Shortcut: ask "is a computer doing the control, a document, a person, or a physical object?" That gives technical, managerial, operational, physical.

The six control types (what it does)

  • Preventive: blocks the event before it happens. Firewall rule, door lock, guard checking IDs, an onboarding policy.
  • Deterrent: discourages an attacker from trying. It does not stop access. A login splash screen with a legal warning, a threatened demotion or dismissal, a front-desk greeter, warning signs.
  • Detective: finds and records an event that is happening or has happened. It does not stop it. Log collection and review, login reports, patrolling guards, motion detectors.
  • Corrective: acts after detection to reverse the damage or get running again. Restoring a ransomware-infected machine from backup, a policy for escalating and reporting incidents, calling law enforcement, a fire extinguisher.
  • Compensating: a substitute when the proper control is not available yet. Blocking the vulnerable port at the firewall until the vendor ships a patch, separation of duties, requiring several guards on shift at once, a generator during a power outage.
  • Directive: instructs people to act securely. It is weak because it relies on people complying. A policy saying sensitive files must go in the encrypted folder, compliance and security training, an "Authorized personnel only" sign.

More detail from the lessons

  • Controls exist to prevent events, limit the impact of ones that happen, and limit damage once someone is in. They protect people, buildings and physical systems as well as data.
  • Technical also covers OS policies, antivirus and firewalls. Managerial controls live in the security policy documentation and in standard operating procedures. Operational is guards, monthly lunch-and-learns, posters and awareness programs.
  • Preventive per category: firewall rule (technical), onboarding policy (managerial), guard shack checking ID (operational), door lock (physical).
  • Deterrent per category: application splash screen (technical), threat of demotion or dismissal (managerial), front reception greeter (operational), warning signs (physical).
  • Detective per category: collecting and reviewing system logs (technical), reviewing login reports daily or weekly (managerial), property patrol (operational), motion detectors (physical).
  • Corrective comes after detection and may let the business continue with minimal downtime. Examples: erase a ransomware-hit computer and restore a known good backup (technical), policies that roll unusual events up into an alert (managerial), contacting law enforcement when someone jumps the fence (operational), a fire extinguisher (physical).
  • Compensating is often temporary until a plan resolves the incident. Example: the vendor promises a patch later, so add a firewall rule today. Also separation of duties (managerial), multiple security guards on shift so none has complete access (operational), and a generator during a power outage (physical).
  • Directive is relatively weak because it directs people toward the more secure choice rather than enforcing it. The encrypted-folder policy makes the user decide what is sensitive. An "authorized personnel only" sign has no lock behind it.

Exam traps

  • One real-world control can be several types. A security camera is physical, detective (it records) and deterrent (people see it).
  • Deterrent vs preventive: a deterrent only discourages. If it physically or technically stops you, it is preventive.
  • Corrective vs compensating: corrective happens after an event to fix it. Compensating stands in for a control you cannot use right now, before or during the problem.
  • Detective vs corrective: an IDS alerts (detective). Removing the malware or restoring the system (corrective).
  • Directive controls are called out as relatively weak because the user has to make the right decision (what counts as sensitive?) and then obey.
  • Controls change as technology changes and every organization chooses different ones, so the exam gives scenarios and expects you to classify them rather than recite a fixed list.
  • Questions often say "which category" or "which type". Read which one is asked: category = technical, managerial, operational or physical; type = preventive, deterrent, detective, corrective, compensating or directive.
  • NIST sometimes calls managerial controls "administrative". Treat the words as the same.

How to practice

  1. Take any security measure you know (CCTV, MFA, a clean-desk policy, a patch window, RAID).
  2. Name its category, then every type it can honestly fit.
  3. Write the matrix from memory until you can fill all 24 cells.
