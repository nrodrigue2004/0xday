1.3 Explain the importance of change management processes and the impact to security

key ideas

  • Change management: request, impact analysis, approval (change advisory board), test, schedule a maintenance window, backout plan, document.
  • Stakeholders and owners must sign off; the backout plan is how you undo a failed change.
  • Technical impacts: allow/deny lists, restarts, downtime, legacy apps, dependencies.
  • Update diagrams, policies and version control after every change.

study notes

Change management is the formal process for altering systems without breaking them or opening a hole. Uncontrolled changes cause outages and are a common root cause of security gaps, so the exam treats the process as a security control.

Business process side

A good change request goes through the same stages every time:

  1. Request and ownership: someone proposes the change and a named owner is responsible for it.
  2. Stakeholders: everyone affected is identified and informed (application owners, help desk, other teams, sometimes customers).
  3. Impact analysis: what could go wrong, what depends on this system, how much risk does it add, what is the benefit.
  4. Test results: the change was tried in a sandbox or test environment first. A sandbox lets you try the change safely without touching production.
  5. Approval process: a change advisory board or an approver signs off. Approvals leave a record of who decided and why.
  6. Backout plan: the exact steps to undo the change if it fails. Written and tested before the change starts. A change without a backout plan should not be approved.
  7. Maintenance window: the scheduled low-impact time (late night, weekend) when the change happens, so fewer users are disrupted.
  8. Standard operating procedure (SOP): the documented, repeatable way to do routine changes the same way every time.

Detail from the lessons, stage by stage:

  • Why a formal process exists: one change in a company can hit hundreds or thousands of systems, so changes go through a committee (the change control board) instead of anyone changing anything whenever. The goals are uptime and availability, everyone being informed, and no mistakes. A system that never gets updated is less secure, so the process must make updating routine, not rare.
  • The request form records the reason for the change, the scope (one system or many), the schedule (date and time), the affected systems and the impact. The board then weighs the risk of making the change against the risk of not making it, for example a big change in a busy season versus leaving a known vulnerability open.
  • Owner vs technician: the owner of the application or data starts the request and is kept informed, but usually does not run the process or make the change. After the change the owner tests their own system and confirms it works. Example: shipping and receiving owns the label printing software, and IT does the upgrade.
  • Stakeholders are not always obvious: a label printing upgrade also touches accounting (shipping reports), delivery times, revenue recognition and ultimately the CEO's view. IT has to research who is affected.
  • Risk of the change: rate it high, medium or low, but think wide. A fix may not fix anything, may break something else, may crash the OS or corrupt data (so you need backups). Risk of not changing: an unpatched vulnerability stays open to attackers, an application stays unavailable, or a dependent service stops working.
  • Sandbox testing: load a duplicate of production, apply the change there, and test thoroughly with zero effect on real users. Also test the backout procedure in the sandbox, even if the change itself worked.
  • Backout plan: many outages come from a "minor" change with no way to revert. Sometimes it is as simple as uninstalling the patch and confirming the original files are back. Hard-to-revert changes need other techniques. Take a full backup before any change so you have a last resort if the backout also fails.
  • Scheduling is the hard part, not approval. Use off hours or maintenance windows (nights, weekends, holidays). 24/7 organizations have almost no window. Seasonal freezes are normal: a retailer may freeze all changes from Thanksgiving to New Year's.
  • The process itself is documented, readable on the intranet, part of the SOPs, and updated over time to be more efficient. No one changes the network without approval.

Exam pattern: "what should have been done before the change failed?" Look for testing, a backout plan, or an approval.

Technical implications (what the change touches)

  • Allow lists and deny lists: firewall rules, application control and URL filters change what is permitted. Allow lists permit only listed items (secure by default). Deny lists block listed items and allow everything else.
  • Restricted activities: a change may be limited by scope, so you document exactly what is permitted and what is not (for example, "restart the web service only, do not touch the database").
  • Downtime: planned outages must be scheduled and communicated.
  • Service restart and application restart: some patches only take effect after a restart. Restarting can drop connections, interrupt jobs and expose startup problems.
  • Legacy applications: old software may break with a new OS, library or security setting, or may not be patchable at all. Compensating controls (isolation, firewall rules) are common.
  • Dependencies: services rely on other services, databases, libraries or accounts. Changing one thing can break another far away. Map dependencies during impact analysis.

Technician's view (more lesson detail):

  • Allow list vs deny list in practice: an allow list means only named applications run and everything else is blocked. A deny list lets everything run except what is named. Antivirus and anti-malware act like one very large deny list of known-bad code. Typical change tickets add a dangerous app (known vulnerabilities, known malware carrier) to a deny list, or add a needed app to an allow list.
  • Scope limits: the board approves a defined scope and window. A two-hour window to upgrade printer drivers does not let you also change other applications because time is free. But scope can expand when needed to finish the primary goal, for example if the driver upgrade only works after editing a config file on every machine. A simple, necessary change like that may be allowed under policy, which is why the change control process should document how scope changes are handled.
  • Downtime thinking: change control does not always mean downtime, but a window is set aside in case. For 24/7 environments, use a primary/secondary switch: move users to the secondary, update the primary, monitor, and switch back to the secondary if there are problems, since it has not been changed. This is often automated. Tell people about outages through email or a central change calendar.
  • Restart types: a reboot may be required for the new config to take effect. Reboots also prove the system recovers properly after a power loss. A lighter option is restarting a service (Windows Services, Task Manager, a Linux daemon). For updating an executable, users must log out, fully close the app and reopen it.
  • Legacy applications: they run for years, the developer may be unsupported or out of business, and nobody understands them (the "do not touch this system" sign). Documenting how they are installed brings them into the normal support cycle, even though old apps on old OSes keep odd quirks.
  • Dependencies can span systems. Example: new firewall management software may need all the firewalls to run a newer code version first, so you update the firewalls, then the management software.
  • Documentation is part of the change: the only constant is change, so documents go stale fast. Update network diagrams, IP addresses, configs and procedures for new apps as part of each change.
  • Version control tracks router configs, Windows patches, registry changes and anything else with a point A to point B. Many apps and OSes have it built in (click to run a different version). Otherwise install third-party version control.

Documentation and version control

  • Update diagrams (network, data flow) and update policies and procedures after every change so documentation matches reality. Stale diagrams hurt incident response and audits.
  • Version control keeps every revision of configs, scripts and documents, shows who changed what and when, and lets you roll back to a known good version.
  • Keep records of the change itself: ticket, approval, test results, date, who implemented it.

Putting it together

StageQuestion to ask
BeforeWho owns it? Who is affected? What is the impact? Was it tested? Who approved? Is there a backout plan?
DuringIs it in the maintenance window? Is it limited to the approved scope?
AfterDid it work? Are diagrams, policies and version history updated? Were stakeholders told?

Exam traps

  • A backout plan is about reversing a failed change. A disaster recovery plan is about recovering from a major failure. Do not mix them.
  • Emergency changes still need documentation, even if approval is fast.
  • Change management protects availability and integrity first.
